On 2026-10-13 a lab called Nature Will [announced Natura-1](https://x.com/naturewill/status/2109858475333570565)
as "our first general foundation model for the physical world". The thread makes three claims
worth an engineer's time. It is a 50-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts transformer pretrained on
one million hours of egocentric human experience. It drives 20-DoF dexterous hands after
post-training on "a limited amount of robot data". And its novelty is not a new block but "the
arrangement of multimodal tokens within a single sequence and the causal structure of attention".

The long-form source is a [technical blog post](https://www.naturewill.com/blog/natura-1). There is
no paper. I searched arXiv for the name and the lab, the Hugging Face model API
(`/api/models?search=natura` and `?author=naturewill`) and the web for a repository. I found nothing:
no paper, no code, no weights, no model card. Everything below is therefore **reported** unless I
say otherwise. The useful exception is that the blog's charts are HTML, not pictures, and each data
point carries its raw value in a `data-value` attribute. I read those, and that is where the
**measured** numbers in this piece come from.

## Why dexterous hands are the hard case

A parallel gripper has one degree of freedom. A 20-DoF hand has twenty, and the blog's framing is
that "the action space grows exponentially with the number of hand joints". The more practical
problem is data. Robot demonstrations are collected by teleoperating a robot, so they only exist
where a robot already works. The blog puts it as a loop: "a robot can only gather data in settings
where it already works, and it can only work in settings where data already exists".

Humans do not have that problem. People already use their hands in nearly every environment, and
head-mounted cameras record it cheaply. So the bet is the one several labs are now making: pretrain
on first-person human video, then adapt to a robot hand with a small amount of robot data. The
open lineage here is long. Physical Intelligence's [π0](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.24164) and
[π0.5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16054) put a flow-matching action expert beside a vision-language
backbone. NVIDIA's [GR00T N1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14734) mixes real robot data, human video
and synthetic data. World-action models such as [FLUX 3 Action](/articles/flux-3-action) and
[Flex-π](/articles/flex-pi) denoise future video and actions together so that video pretraining
becomes motor knowledge. Natura-1 sits closest to the last group, at a larger scale and with far
more human data.

## The mechanism: one episode, one sequence

The blog's model section is short, so I quote it where it matters. Natura-1 "models an entire
episode autoregressively as one sequence of language, image, video, and action tokens". The backbone
is "a standard MoE transformer", and "modality-specific inductive bias lives only in the sequence
layout and the attention pattern".

Concretely, that means no separate vision tower talking to a separate action head through
cross-attention, which is how many VLAs are wired. There is one stream. An instruction comes first,
then the starting image, then the episode unrolls as runs of video tokens, each followed by action
tokens. A transformer reads it left to right.

Three design choices follow from that layout.

**Every step is supervised in one pass.** Because the whole episode is one sequence under a causal
mask, a single forward and backward pass trains a prediction at every timestep. The blog contrasts
this with "a single sampled observation–action window", which is how most robot policies are
trained: sample one moment, predict one action chunk. With teacher forcing, every timestep's
prediction is conditioned on the true past, so an episode of $T$ steps yields $T$ supervised
predictions for the price of one sequence. This is the language-model trick applied to robot
episodes, and it is why the blog can say the design lets physical learning "approach the training
efficiency of frontier language and video models". That last part is a claim, not a measurement.

**Video and action are predicted together.** "At any moment, what the camera sees and what the hand
does are two projections of the same physical state." Natura-1 keeps past video as memory and
learns to predict future video "as part of its world model". Video and action are trained "together
using flow matching and joint denoising, with a separate loss formulation for each modality". In
flow matching the model learns a velocity field that carries noise to data. Here, noised future
video tokens and noised future action tokens sit in the same sequence and are denoised together, so
an action can be conditioned on the frames it will cause. The payoff is supervision density: action
labels are scarce, video is abundant, and video "dominates the tokens of every trajectory". A model
graded on predicting the video gets far more gradient per episode than one graded on actions alone.

**Language has to change the future.** The blog's "Prediction Is Not Understanding" paragraph names
a real failure mode: in most clips the scene alone predicts what happens next, so a model can learn
to ignore the instruction. Natura-1 is "shaped so that a difference in stated intent yields a
different predicted future, in video and action alike", using fine-grained captions from the data
pipeline. How it is shaped (a contrastive loss, paired captions, instruction dropout) is not
described.

The widget lays out one episode as the blog draws it. Move **now** and the timesteps before it
become clean memory while those after it become noised targets. The mask shows what is stated and
what is not. Stated: attention is causal across the episode, so a timestep sees the instruction,
the first image and every earlier timestep, never a later one. Not stated: what attention looks
like inside one timestep. Joint denoising of a step's video and actions suggests those tokens see
each other (block-causal), which is what FLUX 3 Action does. But the blog never says so, so the
default view leaves those cells hatched. Token counts per frame, frames per step and the action
chunk size are not published either; the counts in the widget are schematic.

### What the sequence does not tell you

The model is a 50B MoE, and that is all. The blog gives neither the number of experts, the routing
scheme, nor the **active** parameter count. For an MoE the active count sets the per-token compute
and therefore the latency. A 50B MoE could activate 3B parameters or 20B, and the serving story
changes by an order of magnitude between those. Readers who want the trade-off spelled out can see
the [mixture-of-experts architecture page](/architectures/mixture-of-experts). The tokenizers for
video and actions, the context length and the number of denoising steps at inference are also not
given.

## The data: a million hours, and how they got there

The data section is the most concrete part of the blog. The corpus is "1 million hours of
egocentric human data, consisting of 61,702,471 episodes, 317,842 tasks, and 14,286 scenes"
(reported). It is "primarily built through controlled data acquisition, complemented by large-scale
internet ego videos", with monocular, stereo, multi-camera and wrist-mounted views.

Dividing one by the other gives an average episode of about **58 seconds** (reasoned: one million
hours × 3,600 s ÷ 61,702,471 episodes = 58.3 s). That is a sensible unit for "one sequence": long
enough to hold a multi-step task, short enough to fit in a transformer context once video is
tokenized. The blog does not say how long the trained context actually is.

The pipeline has three stages that matter for robot transfer:

1. **Cleaning.** Deduplication by visual features across vendors and internet sources, sensor
   validation, and for internet video, shot segmentation, hand-visibility checks and VLM scoring.
2. **Motion reconstruction.** An in-house labeller recovers dense hand and body motion from
   monocular and stereo video. The blog reports "a 41% reduction in MPJPE compared with existing
   open-source approaches on an internal benchmark with motion capture ground truth" (reported). The
   benchmark, the baselines and the absolute error are not named, so the 41% cannot be placed. This
   stage is where human video becomes action labels: the reconstructed hand pose is the "action"
   in a human episode.
3. **Language annotation.** Hierarchical captions, goal to subtask to step, written by an agentic
   annotator that shares context across an episode.

The system "processes 350,000 hours of ego data per week" (reported), so the whole corpus is about
three weeks of pipeline throughput (reasoned: one million ÷ 350,000 = 2.9). The X thread's "44K → 1M
hours" refers to the scaling study below, not to collection history.

Two things are missing that would decide how much of this transfers. First, the retargeting from a
human hand to the robot hand. The blog says only that "anthropomorphic dexterous hands offer the
closest correspondence available today". Second, the robot data: "a limited amount", with no hours,
episode count or task count. Without those I cannot tell whether "limited" means tens of hours or
thousands. If you work on reconstruction from video, the [TrackEverything](/articles/trackeverything)
piece covers the dense-3D-tracking side of the same problem; the
[Ego2Act and EgoTools](/articles/egocentric-video-benchmarks) article covers how egocentric
understanding is being benchmarked.

## Infrastructure: the numbers with no hardware attached

The blog makes four infrastructure claims, all reported:

| Claim | Value | What is missing |
|---|---|---|
| Training MFU | "exceeds 50%", 98% GPU utilization | GPU model, count ("hundreds"), precision |
| Attention kernel throughput | ≈1,200 TFLOP/s, "over 6×" a tuned FlexAttention | GPU, precision, sequence shapes |
| End-to-end speedups | 1.7× training, 2× inference | baseline system |
| Serving latency | "within 100 milliseconds" end to end | GPU, how many, active params, chunk size |

The kernel number says something about the hardware even though the blog does not. An H100 SXM's
dense BF16 peak on NVIDIA's spec sheet is 989 TFLOP/s. An attention kernel averaging 1,200 TFLOP/s
over forward and backward cannot be BF16 on an H100, so it is running on a newer part, at lower
precision, or both (reasoned). That matters for the latency claim. Under 100 ms per observation is
plausible for a 50B MoE with a few billion active parameters on a datacenter GPU or a small
multi-GPU node. The infrastructure figure shows hybrid tensor, context and expert parallelism
("TP · CP · EP") in the inference workers, which suggests the serving path spans several GPUs. It
is not something anyone will put on a robot. Treat "real time" here as "real time from a rack".

## Checking the scaling claim

The thread's headline is: "With egocentric training data scaled from 44K to 1M hours ... Prediction
MSE error on real-robot data falls by 67.7%, without fine-tuning on robot data."

<Figure
  src="/articles/natura-1/fig2.jpg"
  alt="Natura-1 scaling validation: four log-log panels of teacher-forced action error against training data hours from about 44 thousand to 1 million. Human validation MSE falls from 0.0320 to 0.0168 and MAE from 0.0916 to 0.0574; robot validation MSE falls from 0.0272 to 0.0088 and MAE from 0.1034 to 0.0525. Each panel shows eight measured checkpoints and a dashed power-law fit."
  caption="Teacher-forced action error against training hours, eight checkpoints per panel, with power-law fits. Lower is better (Natura-1 technical blog, scaling figure)."
/>

I took the eight raw values per panel from the chart's HTML and recomputed everything (measured):

| Validation set | Metric | First checkpoint | Last checkpoint | Change | Blog says |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Human, held out | MSE | 0.032041 | 0.016766 | −47.7% | 47.7% |
| Human, held out | MAE | 0.091569 | 0.057439 | −37.3% | 37.3% |
| Robot, never trained on | MSE | 0.027169 | 0.008764 | −67.7% | 67.7% |
| Robot, never trained on | MAE | 0.103448 | 0.052470 | −49.3% | 49.3% |

A least-squares fit of log error on log hours gives back the blog's printed power laws to four
digits: robot MSE $= 1.7193 \cdot D^{-0.3893}$ with $R^2 = 0.948$ in log space, and human MSE
$= 0.3569 \cdot D^{-0.2235}$ with $R^2 = 0.981$ (measured; the blog prints 0.3570 for the human
coefficient). The arithmetic is honest. What it measures is narrower than the thread suggests.

<ScalingCheckpoints />

**It is teacher-forced prediction error, not task success.** The blog says so: "Both evaluations use
teacher forcing, conditioning each prediction on the past observation." The model sees the true past
of a recorded robot episode and is scored on how close its predicted actions come to what the
teleoperator did. That is a useful signal of transfer, since the robot set was never trained on. But a
policy can match a demonstrator's average motion closely and still fail at the moment of contact, which
is where dexterous tasks are won or lost. Nature Will reports no curve of real-robot success against
pretraining hours.

**The x-axis is training progress, most likely within one run.** The checkpoint positions in the
chart's data are 43,660, 87,321, 130,981 and 174,641 hours, then 314,679, 578,264, 736,415 and one
million (measured). The first four are exact multiples of the first: 1×, 2×, 3× and 4×. With the
blog's own wording ("as training advances from approximately 44,000 to one million hours"), the
natural reading is checkpoints along one run, labelled by hours of data seen so far. If so, data
and compute grow together on this axis, and the curve cannot separate "more data" from "more steps".
A data-scaling claim proper would train separate models on 44K-hour and 1M-hour corpora at matched
compute. The blog may have done that; the chart does not show it (reasoned).

**The tail is flattening.** Over the last three checkpoints, from 578K to one million hours, robot
MSE drops 8.6% and human MSE 7.4% (measured). The power-law fit for robot MSE has the lowest $R^2$ of
the four panels, 0.948, and the last points sit above the fitted line. That is not a broken curve,
just a reason not to extrapolate it to ten million hours.

## Checking the benchmark

The thread's video speaks for itself, and the blog's six long-horizon workflows (wontons, a shipping
box, tea, calligraphy, a screen protector, a yogurt bowl) are worth watching. Note the dagger: those
six "use additional tuning on top of Natura-1". The zero-shot claims rest on a separate benchmark.

The headline is Natura-1 at 53.4 (Base) and 59.1 (Agentic) against π0.5 at 13.5 and GPT-6-Astra at
16.7 (reported). Four things limit what that comparison says.

**It is not the same robot.** Natura-1 runs on a dual-arm ROKAE AR5 with Wuji 20-DoF hands. On that
platform "neither baseline produces a scored result": π0.5 fails even after fine-tuning on Nature
Will's dexterous-hand data, and GPT-6-Astra, used "directly as a coding-based policy", does not score
within the 60-minute per-task limit. The 13.5 and 16.7 come from an ARX AC One dual-arm **gripper**
platform, with π0.5 fine-tuned on gripper demonstrations of "exactly the same general tasks". To its
credit, the blog states that "a strictly like-for-like baseline" is unavailable. The chart still
puts the bars side by side. A gripper scoring 0.0% on bimanual coordination and "Not scored" on
in-hand manipulation is measuring the robot as much as the policy.

**The benchmark is in-house and unpublished.** Twelve capability dimensions in three groups
(dexterous control, instruction following, long-horizon execution). No task list, trial count,
scoring rubric or success criterion is published. The four example tasks shown come with their
"closest demonstrations retrieved from the training set", which is a good practice I wish more
labs copied. It is still four tasks chosen by the authors.

**The overall score is not the mean of its parts.** I averaged the twelve per-dimension scores from
the blog's chart (measured). Natura-1 Base averages 56.0 against an overall of 53.4. Agentic averages
66.2 against 59.1. On grippers, over the eleven dimensions that were scored, GPT-6-Astra averages 30.3
against 16.7 and π0.5 15.9 against 13.5. Averaging the three group means instead does not match
either. The overall is presumably weighted by tasks or trials, which the blog does not publish, so a
reader cannot reproduce 53.4 from what is shown.

**The trial counts look small.** In-hand manipulation is 16.7% for both Natura-1 variants. Tool and
object generalization is 100.0% for both. Agentic's recovery score is 83.3% and its tool use 66.7%.
Those are one sixth, one, five sixths and two thirds, which is what a handful of trials per dimension
looks like (reasoned; the counts are not published, and other cells such as 35.4% are not small
fractions). A two-point gap between variants on such a dimension can be one trial.

Two smaller points. "Natura-1 Agentic" is never defined in the text; it scores higher but takes
139.31 s per task against Base's 63.16 s (reported), which is consistent with a planning-and-retry
wrapper around the same policy, though that is my inference. And execution-time ratios such as
"43.3× faster than GPT-6-Astra" also cross platforms; I get 43.3 and 4.6 from the reported seconds
too (reasoned), but a gripper and a hand do not run the same motions.

## What I'd take from it

The architecture is the interesting part, and it is a clean bet. Put the whole episode in one
sequence, keep the transformer standard, predict future video and actions together, and let the
abundant modality (video) carry most of the gradient. Teacher forcing over a whole episode is a real
efficiency win over window sampling, for the same reason it is in language modelling. The data
pipeline is described with more specificity than most labs offer: 61,702,471 episodes, 317,842
tasks, 14,286 scenes, a 41% MPJPE claim, a weekly throughput.

What the release does not support is a quantitative comparison with anything. The robot-transfer
number is a prediction error along what looks like one training run. The benchmark compares hands
with grippers on a private suite whose aggregate cannot be rebuilt from its parts. The 50B is total
parameters with the active count withheld, and the 100 ms loop has no hardware attached. None of
that makes the demos less impressive; a nearly 30-minute single take of a robot following spoken
instructions is hard to fake. It means the evidence today is the video, not the numbers.

If you want an open model in the same family to read and run, [FLUX 3 Action](/articles/flux-3-action)
publishes weights and code for joint video-action denoising, [Flex-π](/articles/flex-pi) shows the
same shared-latent idea with switchable outputs, and [Cosmos 3](/articles/cosmos-world-model) puts
reasoning and generation in one sequence. For a flow-matching expert bolted onto a VLM rather than a
single stream, see [Qwen-Drive-1.0](/articles/qwen-drive-1-0). Natura-1 is the biggest bet yet on
human video as the pretraining corpus for hands. Whether it wins will show when someone outside the
lab can measure it.
