Two months ago Qwen shipped **Qwen-Image-3.0** as an API and published no architecture, no
parameter count and no benchmark table — a capabilities announcement, which is what
[the piece on it here](/articles/qwen-image-3) had to work with. On 20 September 2026 they
shipped **Qwen-Image-2.1**: a lower version number, released *after* 3.0, with the weights
attached.

So this time there are files to read, and I read them rather than the blog. Every number
below comes from
[`Qwen/Qwen-Image-2.1`](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen-Image-2.1) and its ModelScope
mirror — `config.json` per component, the safetensors indexes, the tensor headers pulled
over HTTP range reads, and the `LICENSE` file. They do not all say what the announcement
says, and the gaps are worth knowing before you plan around this model.

Two days on, other people have run it, and this piece has grown to take their numbers: a
measured **2.55x** for the prefix cache, native-resolution RGBA samples the release never
published, and the same pipeline run at **3.05 GiB** of VRAM and claimed at **2 GB**. Where
a measurement tests something I derived from the files, I have said which way it went. And
since every one of those numbers was taken on a GPU, I ran the thing on four CPU cores with
no GPU at all, twice. The first pass, at 8 steps, showed only that it runs. The second used the
model's own settings, and
[at 512 x 512 it gets every word on the board right in about an hour, on 9.90 GB of 4-bit weights](#running-it-on-four-cpu-cores-with-no-gpu-at-all),
with a gallery beside it.

## The denoiser stops being an MMDiT

Qwen-Image 1.0 and its December refresh were both `QwenImageTransformer2DModel`: 60 joint
blocks, 24 heads of 128 (3,072 wide), a separate text stream with its own modulation and
its own MLP, and a text encoder whose states arrived at `joint_attention_dim: 3584`. That
is an MMDiT, in the MM-DiT-from-SD3 sense, and it weighed 20,430,401,088 parameters.

2.1 is `QwenImage21Transformer2DModel`, and the tensor names give it away before the config
does. There is no `txt_mlp`, no `add_q_proj`, no `to_add_out` — no second stream at all.
Text is projected once by `txt_in` into the same 4,096-wide space, condition-image latents
are substituted into the sequence at the slots the vision-language encoder reserved for
them, the target image's tokens are appended, and one stack of 32 blocks runs over the lot.

| | Qwen-Image 1.0 / 2512 | Qwen-Image-2.1 |
|---|---|---|
| denoiser class | `QwenImageTransformer2DModel` | `QwenImage21Transformer2DModel` |
| blocks | 60, dual-stream | 32, single-stream |
| width | 24 x 128 = 3,072 | 32 x 128 = 4,096 |
| `mlp_ratio` | 4 (implicit) | 3 |
| modulation | per block | one shared projection |
| `patch_size` | 2 | 1 |
| denoiser params | 20,430,401,088 | 7,115,124,736 |
| text encoder | Qwen2.5-VL-7B | Qwen3-VL-8B |
| VAE | `z_dim` 16, 8x spatial, RGB | `z_dim` 64, 16x spatial, RGBA |
| licence | Apache-2.0 | Qwen Research (non-commercial) |

Two of those rows do most of the shrinking, and neither touches depth or width.
`mlp_ratio: 3` is unusual — nearly every transformer since 2017 uses 4 — and the modulation
is a single top-level `Linear(4096 → 16384)` that every block slices its scales and gates
out of, rather than 32 copies of it. The diffusers port says so in as many words: *"blocks
hold no modulation parameters of their own."* There are no bias tensors, and the final
AdaLN emits scale only, no shift.

<ParamBudget />

The 7B claim is exact. The framing around it is the part to watch: the README's "just 7B
parameters in its visual generation component" is true of the component and not of the
thing you download, which is 33.1 GB of BF16 and FP32 weights before you add the rewriter.
Some of that is dead: the Qwen3-VL text encoder ships its 622,329,856-parameter `lm_head`,
`tie_word_embeddings` is `false`, and the pipeline never generates a token. vLLM-Omni's
recipe notes the same thing from the other direction — it excludes "its vision tower and
unused `lm_head`" when quantising.

## Block-causal attention, and why the cache is exact

The interesting engineering is in how the sequence is masked. The release calls it
"mixed-granularity attention"; the implementation is one line:

```python
# diffusers/models/transformers/transformer_qwenimage21.py
allowed = ((q_idx >= kv_idx) | same_image_block) & key_valid[batch_idx, kv_idx]
```

The joint sequence is causal, the way a language model is causal — except that every image
block, each reference image and the target, is internally bidirectional. Text tokens get
token-level granularity; image tokens get chunk-level. The release's own figure is the mask
itself, which is the right way to draw it:

<Figure
  src="/articles/qwen-image-2-1/fig1.png"
  alt="An attention mask drawn as a grid of query rows against key columns, divided into four segments — system prefix, optional input image, edit instruction, target image. The text segments form causal staircases; the image segments are solid blocks, bidirectional within themselves. A bracket labels the first three segments 'Compute once, Reuse K/V' and the target image segment 'Compute each step'."
  caption="The mixed-granularity mask: causal across the sequence, bidirectional inside each image block (Qwen-Image-2.1 release, architecture figure)."
/>

Causality alone would not buy anything during sampling, because a diffusion step changes
every token's activations. What makes the prefix reusable is a second switch,
`causal_condition: true`, and it is the detail worth carrying away from this release. Text
and condition-image tokens are modulated from `t = 0` instead of from the sampled timestep
— the code builds a modulation tensor with `batch_size + 1` rows and routes the prefix to
the extra one. Their activations therefore do not depend on the step, so their K and V do
not either, and caching them is exact rather than approximate. The library refuses to let
you get this wrong:

```
kv_cache requires `causal_condition=True`. The cache is only valid because text and
condition-image tokens modulate from t=0, which makes their activations independent of
the denoising step.
```

Whether that is worth anything depends entirely on how much prefix there is, and the
arithmetic is simple enough to do exactly. `patch_size` is 1 and the VAE compresses 16x, so
an image is exactly `(H/16) × (W/16)` tokens: a 1024px reference costs 4,096 of them, a
2048px target costs 16,384.

<PrefixCache />

At four references and a 1024px target that puts 19.8% of the sequence in the recomputed
part, against the "about a fifth" the vLLM-Omni recipe reports for the same configuration —
which is the cross-check I wanted, because it means the model matches the serving stack's
behaviour rather than my reading of the code. Drag the references to zero and the saving
collapses to nothing, which is also what the recipe says: "plain short-prompt
text-to-image has almost nothing to cache." This is an editing optimisation that a
text-to-image benchmark cannot see.

<Callout type="note">
The README claims up to **10 reference images**. vLLM-Omni, the serving path the same
README lists as Day-0 supported, caps a request at **four** and rejects a fifth with a 400.
Two days later there are two more implementations to ask: diffusers imposes no cap at all —
`image` takes a list and every entry becomes its own block — and the ncnn/Vulkan build
documents `-i` as repeatable "1 to 10 times". So the 4 is vLLM-Omni's policy rather than the
model's, which is the reading I could not justify from two documents and can from four.
</Callout>

### The cache, as three other people measured it

First the mechanics, from `diffusers` rather than from the announcement, because the
interesting part is that nothing here is trained to be cacheable. On the first denoising
step every layer projects Q, K and V for the whole joint sequence and keeps only the prefix
half — post-RoPE, shaped `(batch, prefix_tokens, heads, head_dim)`, one pair per layer. On
every later step the model cuts the sequence down *before* the blocks run at all:

```python
# diffusers/models/transformers/transformer_qwenimage21.py
if kv_cache_mode == "cached":
    joint_hidden_states = joint_hidden_states[:, prefix_len:]
    rotary_emb = rotary_emb[prefix_len:]
    modulation_mask = modulation_mask[prefix_len:]
```

so the target positions are the only ones projected at all, and each attention layer puts
the prefix back with `torch.cat([cached_k, key], dim=1)`. Target queries attend over
`[fixed prefix + current target]`. `use_kv_cache` defaults to `True` in the pipeline; with
`true_cfg_scale > 1` there are two caches, one per branch, so guidance doubles the cache as
well as the work.

One line in there is worth stealing on its own. The cache is written with `.clone()`, not
`contiguous()`, and the comment says why: at batch size 1 the prefix slice already counts as
contiguous, so `contiguous()` hands back the same view and the cache pins the *entire*
prefill K and V for every step of the loop. On the configuration below that is 6.13 GiB
retained instead of 4.13 GiB. One word, two gigabytes.

<CacheLedger />

2.55x is not a suspicious number. It is very close to the only number this architecture
allows. Two 1024px references and a 1024px target make a 12,544-token sequence of which the
target is 32.7%; one full step plus 39 cached ones works out to 2.62x fewer
multiply-accumulates, and the measurement lands at 97% of that. The cleaner way to see it is
to divide back out: the recomputing run does 9.18 PFLOP in 50.57 s and the cached run does
3.51 PFLOP in 19.86 s, which is 182 and 177 TFLOPS — 58% and 57% of the A100's BF16 peak.
The cache does not make the machine more efficient. It gives it less to do, and the saving
arrives where the mask says it should.

The memory goes the other way, and the arithmetic is just as tight. K and V for one prefix token, across
all 32 layers in bf16, is exactly `2 * 4096 * 2 * 32` bytes — half a mebibyte, a number worth
memorising. An 8,448-token prefix is therefore 4.13 GiB of cache, and the measured peak rose
by 3.7 G. That is 90% of the arithmetic, and the gap is the allocator reusing what the
shorter sequence stopped needing.

<Callout type="tip">
The falsifier I attached to `PrefixCache` asked for a measured step-time breakdown showing
the cached path saving *substantially less* than the token share predicts, because the win
is bandwidth-bound or because cache reads cost more than the recompute they avoid. That did
not arrive. The opposite did: at these sequence lengths the DiT is compute-bound, the
effective throughput is unchanged either way, and the arithmetic is a tight bound rather
than a loose one. A third party also reports the cached and uncached images are bit-exact,
which is what `causal_condition` promises and what I could only argue from the code.
</Callout>

Two caveats, because 2.55x is the best case in the set. It is DiT time on a warmed A100, not
a job: the second measurement in the ledger is end-to-end on an RTX 5070 Ti and comes out at
1.30x, because the text encoder and the VAE are outside the cache's reach and four steps
give it only three to work with. And at zero references the widget above still says 1.05x.
None of this touches plain short-prompt text-to-image, which remains the case the published
benchmark measures.

## The alpha channel is real; the published evidence is 684 pixels wide

The feature that actually distinguishes this checkpoint is in `vae/config.json`, one line
from the top: `in_channels: 4`, `out_channels: 4`. The autoencoder takes and returns RGBA.
`z_dim` is 64, `scale_factor_spatial` is 16, `decoder_base_dim` is 144 against a
`base_dim` of 96 — so the decoder carries 259,044,052 of the VAE's 337,740,404 parameters
and the encoder only 78,675,680, a 3.3:1 split. It is stored in FP32, alone among the three
components, which is why 337M parameters occupy 1.35 GB.

This folds in the December 2025 `Qwen-Image-Layered` model as a capability of the base
checkpoint rather than a separate download, and the prompt format is a blunt instrument:
you ask for it in English, in the prompt, with a sentence the model card spells out —
`This is an RGBA image with transparency. … The image has alpha channel and the background
is transparent.`

  src="/articles/qwen-image-2-1/fig3.png"
  alt="A cartoon dragon in Chinese new-year dress riding a lion-dance lion, holding a lantern and a firecracker, with no background. The empty area is drawn as a pale grey and white checkerboard, showing that those pixels are transparent rather than white."
  caption="A published RGBA sample, composited here onto a checkerboard rather than white so the alpha channel survives the page (Qwen-Image-2.1 release, transparency showcase)."
/>

I measured that file's alpha channel rather than taking the claim. All 256 levels are
present, so it is not a one-bit matte — but the histogram is sharply bimodal: 53.1% of
pixels fully transparent, 23.5% fully opaque, 22.6% within 31 levels of one of those two
extremes, and **0.8% of the image anywhere between alpha 32 and 223**. That is a clean cutout
with an anti-aliased fringe, not translucency. It is also a 684 x 685 web asset, well under
the 2048px the card gives as the default generation size, and downsampling manufactures
fringe alpha on its own — so even the fringe is not safely attributable to the model.
The release publishes no
native-resolution RGBA sample, which means the one claim you most want to check from the
artifacts is the one you cannot.

Two days later you can. Someone validating a local installation generated the transparency
workflow twice at 1024 x 1024 — once through the official INT8 weights and once through a
4-bit GGUF conversion of the same denoiser — and committed both PNGs. I ran the same
histogram over them.

<AlphaBands />

The midrange share at native resolution is **0.55%** and **0.48%**, against 0.8% in the
downscaled release asset. It went *down*, which is the direction the resampling argument
predicted, and it stayed down under 4-bit quantisation. Put the other way: in both native
files, **99.1% of pixels sit within seven levels of fully transparent or fully opaque**. This
is a matte generator. It is a good one — the edges are clean and the shape is right — but
if you are planning around glass, smoke or a soft shadow, plan on compositing them yourself.

There is a second thing in those files that the model card does not mention, and it will
cost somebody an afternoon:

<Figure

The transparent pixels are not empty. The VAE decodes RGB there too — a saturated purple,
mean `(153, 56, 195)` over the pixels below alpha 8, with visible horizontal banding near
the bottom edge. Anything that reads the PNG without asking for the alpha channel gets a
purple rectangle with a teapot on it: `cv2.imread` without `IMREAD_UNCHANGED`, a JPEG
export, a downstream model fed the file directly. The guide that shipped these samples warns
about it in one line; the model card does not.

## The licence is the news

Every open Qwen image model before this one is Apache-2.0. I checked all of them on the
Hub:

| Repo | Released | Licence |
|---|---|---|
| `Qwen/Qwen-Image` | Aug 2025 | Apache-2.0 |
| `Qwen/Qwen-Image-Edit` | Aug 2025 | Apache-2.0 |
| `Qwen/Qwen-Image-Edit-2509` | Sep 2025 | Apache-2.0 |
| `Qwen/Qwen-Image-Edit-2511` | Dec 2025 | Apache-2.0 |
| `Qwen/Qwen-Image-Layered` | Dec 2025 | Apache-2.0 |
| `Qwen/Qwen-Image-2512` | Dec 2025 | Apache-2.0 |
| `Qwen/Qwen-Image-Bench` | May 2026 | Apache-2.0 |
| **`Qwen/Qwen-Image-2.1`** | **Sep 2026** | **Qwen Research License** |

The `LICENSE` file is dated 20 September 2026 and says what it says:

> You are granted a non-exclusive, worldwide, non-transferable and royalty-free limited
> license … to use, reproduce, distribute, copy, create derivative works of, and make
> modifications to the Materials **FOR NON-COMMERCIAL PURPOSES ONLY**.

with `"Non-Commercial"` defined in clause 1(i) as *"for research or evaluation purposes
only"*, and commercial use routed to an email address. Both prompt-rewriting checkpoints
carry the same terms. So does the VAE — there is no separate licence for it, which matters
because autoencoders get lifted out of releases and reused more often than denoisers do,
and this is the first 16x RGBA one worth lifting.

This is not a use-restriction licence in the RAIL sense; there is no acceptable-use annex
and no list of forbidden applications. It is a plain commercial restriction, with Chinese
governing law and exclusive jurisdiction in the Hangzhou courts. "Open-source" is the word
the blog, the README and the model card all use. The [OSI definition](https://opensource.org/osd)
has said no field-of-endeavour restrictions since 1998, and this is one.

The downstream is already inconsistent about it, within hours of release. Of the two FlagOS
repackages that Qwen's own README links,
[`FlagRelease/Qwen-Image-2.1-BF16-zhenwu-FlagOS`](https://modelscope.cn/models/FlagRelease/Qwen-Image-2.1-BF16-zhenwu-FlagOS)
is labelled **apache-2.0** on ModelScope, which is not a licence that repo is in a position
to grant. Among the community GGUF conversions that appeared the same day, two carry
`qwen-research` and one carries no licence field at all. None of that is Qwen's doing, and
all of it is the predictable consequence of shipping a research licence into a family
people have spent a year treating as Apache.

Two days on, the picture is better than that paragraph suggests, and the correction belongs
here rather than in a footnote. Every redistribution people are actually installing from
carries the right terms. The official ComfyUI repack
([`Comfy-Org/Qwen-Image-2.1`](https://huggingface.co/Comfy-Org/Qwen-Image-2.1)) declares
`license_name: qwen-research` and links back to Qwen's own `LICENSE` file, which is the most
correct form any of them takes. Both `abenzerps` GGUF conversions declare `qwen-research`
with `base_model` set, missing only the link. The ncnn conversion
([`nihui-szyl/qwen-image-ncnn`](https://huggingface.co/nihui-szyl/qwen-image-ncnn)) ships the
Qwen Research License verbatim, says in the card that the files are "for research and
evaluation purposes only", that commercial use needs a separate licence, and that the
inference tool's own Apache-2.0 "does not change the licensing terms of the
Qwen-Image-2.1 model files" — a distinction most repackagers do not bother to draw. Its one
gap is metadata rather than terms: the card carries no `license:` field, so the Hub shows the
repository with no licence tag and it will not appear in a licence filter. The installers
are cleaner still, because they redistribute nothing: MIT scripts that download the official
weights by pinned revision and verify them by SHA-256. The FlagOS `apache-2.0` label remains
the outlier, not the pattern.

Three more repositories belong in that audit, because the CPU runs further down use or
measure them. Unsloth's two
([`unsloth/Qwen-Image-2.1-GGUF`](https://huggingface.co/unsloth/Qwen-Image-2.1-GGUF) and
[`unsloth/Qwen-Image-2.1-FP8`](https://huggingface.co/unsloth/Qwen-Image-2.1-FP8)) declare
`license_name: qwen-research`, link `license_link` to Qwen's own `LICENSE` and set
`base_model` — the same, most complete form as the ComfyUI repack. leejet's
[`leejet/Qwen-Image-2.1-GGUF`](https://huggingface.co/leejet/Qwen-Image-2.1-GGUF), whose files
produced every CPU image in this article, says in its README body that the quantised files
follow the Qwen Research License and links it, but carries no `license:` field, so the Hub
shows it with no licence tag — the ncnn repository's gap exactly, terms right and metadata
missing.

And one finding that cuts the other way. The text encoder inside `Qwen/Qwen-Image-2.1` is,
as far as I can check without downloading 17.5 GB twice, stock
[`Qwen/Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct`](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct): all 750
tensors match it in name, shape, dtype and byte size, 17,534,247,392 bytes on both sides, and
the six tensors I sampled — first and last language layers, a middle MLP, a vision block, the
embeddings and `lm_head` — are byte-identical over their first 4 MB. That model is Qwen's own
Apache-2.0 release. So the research licence on this directory covers the denoiser and the VAE
in any sense that matters: 8.77B of the pipeline's 16.2B parameters are obtainable from Qwen
under Apache-2.0 already, and the GGUF encoder the CPU runs below use is Qwen's own Apache-2.0
conversion of it, `Qwen/Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct-GGUF`.

## What the one published eval measures

There is no technical report. arXiv has no Qwen-Image-2.1 paper; the most recent Qwen image
paper is the benchmark's, from May. The GitHub repo is a README, a LICENSE, and a
`prompt_rewrite/` directory — no inference code of its own, no training code, no evaluation
harness. The entire quantitative record of this release is one chart.

<Figure
  src="/articles/qwen-image-2-1/fig2.png"
  alt="A bar chart of 29 image models ranked by a single total score from 67.01 down to 46.17, with Qwen Image 2.1 highlighted in green at 60.28 in seventh place. Below each bar, a second row shows parameter counts: open models are labelled with sizes from 6B to 80B, while closed models show a padlock icon on a full-height bar and a footnote that closed models' parameter counts are not disclosed."
  caption="The only quantitative claim in the release: one aggregate score across 29 models, on Qwen's own benchmark (Qwen-Image-2.1 release, Qwen-Image-Bench evaluation comparison)."
/>

Name the denominators, because the chart does not.
[Qwen-Image-Bench](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Qwen/Qwen-Image-Bench) is **1,000
expert-written bilingual prompts**, scored across a three-level rubric — 5 pillars, 23
sub-capabilities, **56 verifiable facets**. Each facet is judged Fail, Pass or Excel, mapped
to **0, 60 and 100**, `N/A` excluded, and averaged up the tree. The judge is **Q-Judger**, a
Qwen3.6-27B fine-tune trained on 130,000+ annotated pairs from 80 art-academy annotators,
run at `temperature 0`, `seed 42`, thinking on.

So: Qwen's model, on Qwen's prompts, scored by Qwen's judge, against competitors Qwen ran
itself. That is not disqualifying — the benchmark, the judge and the dataset are all
published under Apache-2.0, which is more than most vendors do, and you can rerun it. It is
also not third-party, and the chart does not say so.

Three things the chart leaves out that its own sources state:

1. **The leaderboard it reuses is the Chinese-prompt run.** Eighteen of the 29 bars match
   the benchmark's published leaderboard to the hundredth, and that leaderboard carries a
   footnote: *"results are computed based on Q-Judger's evaluation of images generated from
   Chinese prompts. We will release the results for image generation from English prompts
   soon."* The chart inherits the denominator and does not mention it.
2. **The human-agreement figure is a model-level rank correlation.** Spearman
   $\rho = 0.92$ is impressive and it is computed over $N = 18$ **models**, not over images.
   It says the judge orders a field the way experts do. It does not say the judge is right
   about any particular picture, and it says nothing at all about whether a 0.46-point gap
   between two adjacent bars is a real difference.
3. **It is a text-to-image benchmark.** Transparency, ten-reference composition, circle- and
   mask-guided local editing, identity preservation — everything this release actually added
   — is measured by none of it. The chart scores the one capability 2.1 did not change.

The rubric is worth putting the totals back onto, because 60 is not an arbitrary number on
that scale. It is the score of a model that passes every facet and excels at none.

<RubricMath />

That gives the chart a reading it does not advertise. Qwen-Image-2.1 at 60.28 is the
lowest bar on it that could, in principle, have zero Fails — everything below is carrying a
floor on how often the judge said the image failed outright, up to 23.1% for the last bar.
Against its own line the improvement is real and large: 49.23 for Qwen-Image 1.0, 52.06 for
2512, 60.28 here, at roughly a third of 1.0's denoiser size. Against the field it is
seventh of 29, below Qwen's own `Qwen Image 3 Pro` at 62.36, and the six models above it are
all closed.

<Callout type="warn">
The second row of that chart draws every closed model's parameter count as a full-height
bar with a padlock, footnoted "closed-source models' parameter counts are not disclosed".
A bar whose height encodes *unknown* sits on the same axis as bars whose height encodes 6B
and 80B. Read the row as a lookup table, not as a chart.
</Callout>

## What it costs to run, from someone other than the vendor

The release makes an efficiency argument — "compact", "lightweight", "low computational
cost" — and publishes no latency, no throughput and no memory figure to support it. On the
day it landed the only measured numbers anywhere were third-party, in the
[vLLM-Omni recipe](https://recipes.vllm.ai/Qwen/Qwen-Image-2.1), taken on one NVIDIA GB300
at 1024 x 1024 and 40 steps:

| Config | Peak memory | Time per image |
|---|---:|---:|
| BF16 | 34.0 GB | 3,282-4,492 ms |
| DiT FP8, `img_mlp` kept BF16 | 32.0-32.7 GB | 3,356-4,706 ms |
| Text-encoder FP8 | 27.5-28.1 GB | 3,427-4,770 ms |

Two things follow. 34 GB peak means a "7B" model does not fit a 24 GB card without
offloading — the pipeline is 33.1 GB of weights before activations, of which the denoiser
is 14.2 GB. And FP8 on Blackwell buys memory, not speed: none of the quantised rows
is faster, which is what you expect when the bottleneck is bandwidth rather than tensor
cores. Qwen ships no quantised export of its own; every FP8, W8A8 and GGUF build is a
partner's or the community's.

### Down the ladder: 33.1 GB, then 3.05 GiB, then 2 GB

Two days of community work produced two much smaller numbers than that table, and they are
smaller in completely different ways.

<FootprintLadder />

The ladder carries three more kinds of row than it did. Unsloth's
[GGUF](https://huggingface.co/unsloth/Qwen-Image-2.1-GGUF) and
[FP8](https://huggingface.co/unsloth/Qwen-Image-2.1-FP8) repositories are there with byte counts
read from the Hub; the FP8 row is a GPU route. Unsloth's guide states 11 GB of VRAM for the GGUF
route, 24 GB for FP8 or INT8 (6 GB with offloading, at what it says is less than twice the
time), and 12–16 GB of RAM for
CPU-only, and says of all of them that "memory figures are estimates, not tested minimums" —
which is why the host-memory view draws them dashed. The last two rows are this article's own
runs on a four-core CPU, measured further down: 9.86 and 11.40 GiB of peak RSS at 512², under the
15.21 GiB the 3090's tightest profile needed on the host, and on the time view bars that dwarf
every GPU row, which is the honest shape of doing this without one.

The **3.05 GiB** configuration is the ComfyUI GGUF route, and it trades four separate
things, all of them stated. Precision first: the denoiser goes from bf16 to `Q4_K_M`, which
is 5.18 bits per parameter and takes 14.23 GB to 4.60 GB; the text encoder goes to an INT8
ConvRot export at 8.53 bits including scales, 17.53 GB to 9.35 GB; the VAE is cast from fp32
to bf16, 1.35 GB to 0.68 GB. The download drops from 33.1 GB to 14.63 GB. Second, the prompt
rewriter is gone — 9.41B parameters and 18.82 GB of the path Qwen's README recommends — and
the validation report says plainly that no prompt-enhancer integration was tested. A
distilled 0.75B replacement for that rewriter has since appeared, and
[the piece on it here](/articles/qwen-image-2-1-pocket-rewriter) checks what it keeps. Third,
placement: the text encoder runs on the CPU, the VAE decodes on the CPU, and the server log
shows **2,186 MiB of denoiser weights on the GPU against 2,301 MiB offloaded**, so less than
half the 4-bit model is resident and the rest streams in per step.

Fourth, and this is the number that belongs next to the footprint: **time**. At 512 x 512
and 40 steps the same package goes 111.75 s to 195.28 s as the budget tightens from 5.55 GiB
to 3.05 GiB. At 1024 x 1024 it goes 60.35 s to 145.90 s for 15.39 GiB down to 6.14 GiB. And
the memory does not disappear, it relocates: process RSS in the tightest profile is
**15.21 GiB**, against 2.4 GiB in the ordinary ones. You are buying VRAM with system RAM and
wall-clock: at 1024 that is 2.4x the time for 40% of the memory.

<Callout type="warn">
The 3.05 GiB was measured on a 24 GiB RTX 3090 launched with `--reserve-vram 20`, which the
report is explicit is "a placement hint, not a hard CUDA allocation limit", and it says so
before anyone can quote it otherwise: no physical 4, 6, 8, 12 or 16 GiB card was tested, no
RAM-constrained host, no Windows install, no AMD/Intel/Apple backend. Nor was the
transparent-RGBA workflow run at that budget — it was validated in the standard profile at
15.46 GiB. And there is no quality comparison against bf16 or INT8 anywhere in the report.
It is an honest document, which is exactly why it is worth reading before quoting the
headline from it.
</Callout>

The **2 GB** claim is a different animal, and the arithmetic is the interesting part. It
comes from an ncnn/Vulkan port — a portable executable, no CUDA, no PyTorch, no Python,
running on NVIDIA, AMD, Intel and Apple Silicon — and unlike the GGUF route it claims full
bf16 with no quantisation. That claim checks out, from eight bytes. The first four bytes of
each shipped `.bin` are `83 8b 34 01`, and `ncnn/src/modelbin.cpp` reads tag `0x01348B83`
as bfloat16; all three components carry it. The denoiser reconciles to the release exactly:
**14,230,275,488 bytes against the safetensors index's 14,230,249,472**, 26 KB apart on
14.2 GB, which is ncnn's own per-layer headers.

What is missing from the conversion is the part this article already flagged as dead. The
whole package is 31,191,738,224 bytes, which is 1.92 GB under the 33.1 GB release, and the
shortfall is two things and only two: the 622,329,856-parameter `lm_head` that the pipeline
never generates a token with (1.24 GB), and the VAE halved from fp32 (0.68 GB). Subtract
both and you land within **3.6 MB** — 0.01% — of the shipped total. Somebody else read the
same headers and drew the same conclusion, and then acted on it.

So where does 2 GB come from? Not from a smaller model. The project's own requirements
section asks for **16 GB of RAM** on Linux and macOS, and on Windows for
`(half of system RAM) + (GPU memory) >= 16GB`, because WDDM lets a Vulkan application map
only half the host's RAM. 2 GB is the GPU's share of a 16 GB budget, and that 16 GB is not
arbitrary either: the denoiser, the vision tower and the VAE are 14.23 + 1.16 + 0.66 =
16.05 GB, which is the sampling stage once the 15.14 GB text encoder has run and been
released. The weights live in host memory and the GPU reads them as it goes.

What that costs is not published. One release, dated 22 September, no benchmark table, sample
images "TBA". The floor is easy to compute and worth stating: if only about 2 GB of a
14.23 GB denoiser is resident, each of 40 steps drags most of it across the host link, so on
PCIe 4.0 x16 at a realistic 25 GB/s that is about 0.57 s per step and 23 s per image of pure
transfer before any arithmetic happens. On unified memory — Apple Silicon, an integrated GPU
— there is no link to cross at all, which is presumably why the target is Vulkan and not
CUDA. A memory figure with no latency figure next to it is a claim about where the weights
sit, not about how fast they run.

One last thing puts the two halves of this article on the same axis. The prefix cache buys
its 2.55x by *spending* memory: half a mebibyte per prefix token, 4.13 GiB at two 1024px
references and **8.13 GiB at four**. That is more than the entire GPU budget of the 3.05 GiB
profile. The configuration that makes this model fit a small card and the optimisation that
makes multi-reference editing fast are the same trade run in opposite directions, and on one
card you do not get both.

## Running it on four CPU cores, with no GPU at all

Every figure in the section above was taken on a GPU — a GB300, an A100, a 3090 told to
pretend it was smaller — and the two most-quoted numbers are both claims about where the
weights *sit* rather than about what it takes to have them. 3.05 GiB is a placement hint on
a 24 GiB card. 2 GB is the GPU's share of a 16 GB budget the *host* has to supply. Neither
answers the question a reader without a GPU actually has, which is whether a GPU is the
floor at all.

So I ran it on the box that builds this site: four cores of an Intel Xeon at 2.80GHz, 15 GB
of RAM, no `/dev/dri`, no CUDA, no Vulkan, no accelerator of any kind. It generates images.
I did it twice. The first pass was a smoke test — 8 sampling steps, a fifth of what the
vendor uses — and its pictures were fair evidence that the model runs and unfair evidence of
what it draws; the site owner looked at them and said as much. The second pass uses the
model's own settings, and pulls apart the three things the first pass had tangled together:
the step budget, the noise schedule and the quantisation. The second pass comes first here,
because it is the one that describes the model.

### The files

The runner is [`stable-diffusion.cpp`](https://github.com/leejet/stable-diffusion.cpp) at
commit `2bb7294`, built with `-DGGML_NATIVE=ON` against AVX-512F/BW/DQ/VL and AVX512-VNNI —
the project whose author also published the Qwen-Image-2.1 GGUFs. Every weight file was
fetched at a pinned revision and checked against the Hub's own SHA-256 before it was used:

|---|---|---:|---:|
| Denoiser, pass 2 | `leejet/Qwen-Image-2.1-GGUF` &middot; `qwen_image_2.1-Q4_K.gguf` | 4,197,494,816 | 4.72 |
| Denoiser, both passes | `leejet/Qwen-Image-2.1-GGUF` &middot; `qwen_image_2.1-Q2_K.gguf` | 2,561,716,256 | 2.88 |

Bits per parameter are read from each GGUF's own tensor table, not from the file name: every
one of these denoisers holds exactly 7,115,124,736 parameters, the same count the safetensors
headers gave at the top of this article. The Q4_K pipeline is **9,900,789,304 bytes**, the
Q2_K one **8,265,010,744** — against 14.63 GB for the 3090 profile and 33.1 GB for the path
the README recommends. Note which component moved. The denoiser is the 7B everybody talks
about, and at Q2_K it is the *smallest* file in the pipeline: the 4-bit text encoder is 1.96x
its size. That is this article's opening ratio surviving all the way down the quantisation
ladder.

On the first pass disk was the binding constraint — 10.93 GB free, which is why it went
straight to Q2_K. On the second it was not, and something else was.

### What "the model's own settings" are

Three sources, and they do not all say the same thing.

- **Qwen's model card.** Every example calls the pipeline with `num_inference_steps=40` and
  seed 42, at 2048 x 2048, and none of them passes a guidance argument.
- **The diffusers pipeline.** `QwenImage21Pipeline.__call__` defaults to
  `num_inference_steps: int = 40` and `true_cfg_scale: float = 1.0`, and guidance switches on
  only when `true_cfg_scale > 1` *and* a negative prompt is supplied. So the vendor's
  default is 40 steps with classifier-free guidance **off** — one denoiser pass per step, not
  two. The scheduler is `FlowMatchEulerDiscreteScheduler` with a resolution-dependent shift:
  `base_shift` 0.5, `max_shift` 0.9, anchored at 256 and 8,192 latent tokens, exponential
  time shift, and `shift_terminal: 0.02`.
- **Unsloth's guide** gives two recipes and says to use the one for the backend actually
  running the model. For diffusers: 40 steps, CFG 1.0 ("CFG disabled"), keep the pipeline's
  scheduler — which agrees with the model card. For native stable-diffusion.cpp: 20 steps
  ("CLI default and Unsloth's recorded test"), cfg **6.0** ("upstream sd.cpp example"),
  Euler, and an automatic flow shift. Unsloth's own GGUF samples were rendered at 1024²,
  20 steps, cfg 6.0.

So the vendor and the sd.cpp example disagree about the one setting that doubles the cost.
The vendor runs one denoiser pass per step; the sd.cpp example runs two, at a guidance scale
the vendor's pipeline never applies by default. My first pass borrowed guidance from the
second camp for one of its three runs, and that is the run whose exposure collapsed.

The schedule disagrees too, and nobody's guide mentions it. For this model sd.cpp picks its
generic Flux schedule — `base_shift` 0.5, `max_shift` 1.15, anchored at 256 and 4,096
tokens, no terminal stretch — which I read out of `src/runtime/denoiser.hpp` and
`src/pipeline/request.cpp` at the commit I built. At 512 x 512 that gives a shift of 0.630
against the vendor's 0.539. The bigger difference is at the far end. The vendor stretches the
schedule so the last non-zero noise level is 0.02; sd.cpp's default ends at **0.211 at
8 steps**, 0.090 at 20 and 0.046 at 40, and then jumps to zero in one Euler step. At 8 steps
the final step is asked to remove a fifth of the full noise level in one move, and I expected
that to be why the first pass looked unfinished. It was not, and the run that shows it is
below.

Either way, for the second pass I did not trust "automatic". Every run hands sd.cpp the
vendor's exact sigma schedule through `--sigmas`, computed from the model's own
`scheduler_config.json` with the formula diffusers uses, and the schedules are in the data
file beside the images.

### Memory: a ceiling I did not know was there

The first attempt at the second pass died. Q4_K at 512 x 512 ran its sampling steps and was
killed during the VAE decode with signal 9, and the kernel log is specific about why: not the
machine's 15.7 GB but a **memory cgroup**. The sandbox these commands run in caps its shell at
14,345,842,688 bytes — 13.36 GiB — and in a cgroup the page cache counts. Without memory
mapping, sd.cpp reads each weight file into the page cache and then copies it into its own
buffers, so 9.90 GB of weights were charged twice until reclaim caught up. The process was
killed at 10.27 GiB of anonymous memory, having spent 413 seconds of system time — against
789 of user time — fighting the limit; the cgroup had hit it 8,373,181 times since the
container booted 18 minutes earlier. Its two sampling steps took 119.76 s and 115.78 s.

`--mmap` fixes it. The weights are mapped straight from the GGUF files, charged once, and
evictable, so pages nothing is touching — the text encoder's, once conditioning is done — can
be dropped rather than held in a private copy. The same 2-step run then finished: 89.39 s and 90.28 s per step, 9.9 s of
system time, zero refused allocations, and a cgroup peak of 12.08 GiB against the 13.36 GiB
cap (sampled every half second). Every second-pass run uses it. The lesson travels: inside
a container, a memory figure is a figure for *charged* memory, and whether the runner reads or
maps its weights moves it by roughly the size of the weights.

With it, peak RSS was **11.40 GiB for the Q4_K runs and 9.86 GiB for Q2_K** at 512², counting
the mapped weights, with a sampled cgroup peak of 13.34 GiB — page cache filling the space it is
allowed, not the process needing it. sd.cpp's own compute buffers at 512² are small beside the
weights and lopsided among themselves: 7.18 MB for the text encoder, 234.03 MB for the denoiser
and **2,450.50 MB for the VAE**, the one component that ever materialises anything at full
pixel resolution.

guidance off, at either quantisation.

parameters at Q3_K. And one tensor gets treated differently at the bottom than everywhere
else. This denoiser has a single modulation projection shared by all 32 blocks —
`modulation.1.weight`, 4,096 x 16,384, 67,108,864 parameters, 0.94% of the model — which this
article flagged as a place the architecture saved parameters. leejet's seven rungs are
uniform — the same 192 tensors at the named type and the same 73 in BF16 in every file, from
`Q2_K` to `Q8_0` — so it keeps that tensor in BF16 throughout. Unsloth upcasts it at every rung above the bottom, to Q4_K, Q5_K or Q8_0, and quantises
it to 2 bits in its `Q2_K`.

That makes leejet's Q2_K against Unsloth's Q2_K a fair test of whether *where* the bits go
matters at the bottom: 2,561,716,256 bytes against 2,466,137,824, and the same 192 tensors at
Q2_K in both. Diffing the two tensor tables leaves exactly 69 differences. Sixty-five go *up*
in Unsloth's file — the 64 query/key norms and the output modulation, BF16 to F32 — and three
small ones go down to Q8_0: the output projection and the two timestep-embedding layers. The
one that goes all the way down is the shared modulation, BF16 to Q2_K.

### Where the wall clock goes

<CpuLedger />

Five things the second pass measures cleanly, all at 512 x 512 with guidance off:

- **87.09 s per step at Q4_K, 79.43 s at leejet's Q2_K, 79.64 s at Unsloth's** — medians of
  the per-step times sd.cpp prints. Q2_K's file is 39% smaller and its steps are only 9% faster,
  so on this CPU a step is mostly arithmetic, not bytes; halving the weights buys disk and
  memory, not much time. sd.cpp kept 3.76 to 3.82 of the 4 cores busy in every clean run.
- **The headline took 3,863 s, and should have taken about 3,535.** Its wall-clock comes from
  the job queue's timestamps, because sd.cpp's own log for that run is gone: I edited the shell
  script that launched it while it was still executing, bash resumed reading the edited file at
  a stale offset when the run ended, and a fragment of the new script overwrote the log. The
  image had already been written. Fifteen per-step readings survive from checks I made during
  the run, from 86.48 s to 124.09 s, and the slow ones sit in windows where other jobs were on
  the box — my own downloads and a type-check, another agent's `pnpm validate`, and page cache
  from freshly downloaded files squeezing the mapped weights. From the clean 20-step run's
  medians, 40 steps is 4.92 + 40 x 87.09 + 46.06 = 3,535 s, about 59 minutes. That figure is
  arithmetic on measurements, not a measurement.
- **Text encoding is free.** 4.36 to 5.85 s for 8.77B parameters of Qwen3-VL, under 0.4% of a
  20-step run.
- **The VAE is not.** 39.60 to 46.06 s to decode at 512², and its compute buffer is 2,450.50 MB
  — ten times the denoiser's 234.03 MB. At 20 steps that is 2.5% of the run; the 91.36 s the
  first pass logged was the same decode on a shared box.
- **20 steps is half of 40 and costs half.** 1,822.84 s for the gallery's setting against about
  3,535 for the vendor's, and on the chalkboard the two images are the same picture. Whether that
  holds on harder prompts is what the gallery tests.

It drew three conclusions, and the second pass is the test of each.

- **"Guidance buys text fidelity and resolution does not."** Withdrawn. At 8 steps that was
  what the pictures showed; at 20 steps, with guidance off, 512 x 512 renders every word at
  either quantisation. The underexposed cfg 4.0 frame was not a price paid for legibility but a
  second symptom of too few steps, at a guidance scale the vendor does not ship.
- **"4.53x the per-step time for 4x the tokens — close to linear, slightly over."** Wrong in
  its detail. That 512² run was sharing the box: sd.cpp averaged 2.91 busy cores out of 4,
  against 3.51 in the 256² run beside it. Against the clean second-pass median for the same
  Q2_K weights at 512², 79.43 s, the ratio is **3.58x** — under linear, not over.
- **"Disk, not RAM, was the binding constraint."** True for that pass, on 10.93 GB of free
  disk. On the second, disk was not the limit and a memory cgroup was.

What it got right survives: the pipeline runs end to end with no GPU at all, the text encoder
costs almost nothing, and the VAE's activation memory at small resolutions dwarfs the
denoiser's — 613.19 MB against 65.24 MB at 256², 2,450.50 MB at 512².

### What this does and does not settle

It settles the floor, and it moves the answer from "it runs" to "it works". No GPU anywhere in
these runs, no Vulkan, no unified memory: 9.90 GB on disk and 11.40 GiB resident for the 4-bit
pipeline, and at the vendor's own settings a 512² image in an hour that gets every word on the
board right. At 20 steps it is half an hour. The 2 GB and 3.05 GiB figures above ask how little
of a GPU you can get away with; this asks whether you need one, and at 512² the answer is no, if
you have an hour and the text is what you care about.

What it does not settle is how far below the full model this is. Every image here is 4-bit or
2-bit, at a quarter of the pipeline's default pixel count and a sixteenth of the model card's,
and nobody has published a bf16 image at the same seed to hold them against — the 3090 report
has no quality comparison either. It does not settle guidance at a fair step budget, because I
did not run cfg above 1.0 past 8 steps. And it does not reach native resolution: the VAE's
compute buffer is 2,450.50 MB at 512² and grows with the pixel count, so on this box's
13.36 GiB cgroup a 1024² decode would likely need sd.cpp's tiled VAE — an inference from the
buffer sizes, not something I ran. The time that would cost is in the falsifiers, as an
extrapolation rather than a number.

## What is missing

Shortest useful list, all verified by absence:

- **No technical report.** No arXiv preprint, no PDF, no architecture section beyond eight
  bullet points in a README.
- **No training data disclosure.** Nothing about corpus, scale, filtering, or the licensing
  of what it learned from. The flagship multi-reference demo is a generated group photograph
  assembled from six publicity portraits of identifiable real people, and the release has
  nothing to say about that either.
- **No eval for anything new.** The benchmark covers text-to-image; the transparency,
  editing and reference-composition claims are supported by curated examples only. No
  GEdit, no ImgEdit, no alpha-matting metric, no identity-similarity number.
- **No first-party quantisation.** BF16 shards only, plus an FP32 VAE.
- **No inference code in the repo.** The GitHub repository ships the prompt rewriter and
  nothing else; the model runs through third-party libraries from day one, which is a
  reasonable choice and worth knowing before you file an issue.
- **No limitations, bias or intended-use section** in the model card. Not one line.
- **No separate licence for the VAE.**

## The take

Qwen-Image-2.1 is a good piece of engineering and a worse release than the six that
preceded it. The architecture work is genuine and legible from the files: a single-stream
DiT at a third of the previous denoiser's size, a shared modulation projection and
`mlp_ratio: 3` that together account for 3.69B of the parameters it does not have, a 16x
RGBA autoencoder that makes transparency a property of the latent space instead of a second
model, and a block-causal mask whose prefix cache is exact because someone thought to
modulate the condition from `t = 0`. That last one is the idea I would steal, and two days
of other people's benchmarks have not dented it: 2.55x measured against 2.62x predicted,
bit-exact output, and the same effective throughput on both paths. The engineering is the
part of this release that holds up under someone else's stopwatch.

What surrounds it is thinner than the version number suggests. One aggregate score on the
vendor's own benchmark, on the vendor's own judge, on the Chinese-prompt run, measuring the
capability that did not change. No report, no data, no numbers for the new features, no
first-party quantisation. And a licence that moves a year-old Apache-2.0 family to
non-commercial research terms while the announcement keeps saying "open-source" — which is
the one change here that decides whether you can use it at all, and the only one the blog
post does not mention.
