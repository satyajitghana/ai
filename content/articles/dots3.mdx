The 67th International Mathematical Olympiad ran in Shanghai on 15–16 July 2026, with **666 contestants from 117 countries** — records for both. The dots team, Xiaohongshu's AI lab, was invited by the IMO Organizing Committee and entered an internal version of **dots-note-3.0**.

It scored **42 out of 42**. Full marks on all six problems, marked by the committee's own graders. [Seven human contestants](https://studio.dots.ai/dots/dots3-en.html) matched that.

Two details make this more interesting than the headline.

## No formal language anywhere

The usual way to make a model's proof trustworthy is to make it machine-checkable: a person translates the problem into Lean, the model works in Lean, and a checker certifies the result. The guarantee is airtight and the approach has a ceiling — as the dots team puts it, many real problems "are difficult to formalize completely, which limits the generality of this path."

dots-note-3.0 did not do that. It **read the organizers' original LaTeX problems directly** and worked end to end in natural language plus Python.

Which means nothing verified its reasoning except itself, and then a panel of human graders. That is a much weaker guarantee going in and a much stronger result coming out — the thing being certified is a proof a mathematician read and accepted, not a term a type-checker accepted.

## The loop that replaces the checker

<ProofLoop />

The method is three stages: **Proof, Verify, Refine**. Generate candidate proofs; assess them for correctness and completeness and produce specific suggestions; rewrite the weak parts; repeat. The dots team describes "multiple rounds of parallel reasoning, review, correction, and synthesis," and is explicit that self-review is "one of the abilities we focused on optimizing during the training."

The failure modes they name are precisely the ones that survive an answer-key and die under a grader: *reasoning errors, logical leaps, and insufficiently rigorous steps*. An answer-only benchmark cannot see any of the three.

The thesis is stated plainly enough to quote:

> We believe that recursive self-improvement begins with recursive self-critique. Genuine optimization is impossible without the active identification of errors and shortcomings.

I would frame the IMO result as a test of that claim rather than a mathematics result. The competition is unusually well-suited to it: the problems are hard, the proofs are long, the marking is adversarial and human, and there is no partial credit for a plausible-looking argument with a hole in it.

## The scoreboard, and the page counts

<MedalBoard />

Six problems, 7/7 on each. The part I find most informative is not the scores — they are all the same number — but the **lengths**: 3, 10, 6, 5, 4, and 3 pages, 31 pages of proof in total.

That distribution does not match the competition's usual difficulty ordering. P6 is traditionally the hardest slot and got one of the two shortest proofs; **P2 took ten pages**, more than three times either of them. Page count is a rough proxy for how much work the model needed to make an argument airtight, and it is the only signal here that varies at all.

P3 is the one the human experts singled out. Wang Qiantong, a CMO gold medalist:

> The inductive approach to Liu Bang's side of Problem 3 is extremely elegant. Contestants' common approach is essentially to transform it into a graph-connectivity problem, but the dots model used a remarkably ingenious and elegant induction to overcome the genuinely difficult part of the problem.

And Liu Hanzuo, a two-time CMO gold medalist, on the solution overall: "compact in structure and natural in its logic; even among IMO solutions, it would rank as one of the more concise and elegant approaches."

Praise for *elegance* is a different claim from praise for correctness, and it is the harder one to fake.

## It is the small one

The line that reframes the whole announcement is near the bottom:

> Note is currently the lightest model in the dots3 family. The complete family will include note, jazz, and aria in three different sizes.

The model that scored 42/42 is the **smallest of three**. No parameter count is given for any of them, and the entry was an *internal version* rather than anything released.

The dots team says it expects to open-source dots-note-3.0 "in the near future."

## What is not on the page

Quite a lot, and it is worth listing because the result is strong enough to invite over-reading.

**No compute or time budget.** How many rounds the Proof-Verify-Refine loop ran, how much parallel sampling fed the synthesis step, how long each problem took, what it cost — none of it is published. The IMO gives human contestants 4½ hours per day for three problems. Whether the model operated under a comparable constraint is not stated, and for a method built on "multiple rounds of parallel reasoning," that is the number that determines what the result means.

**No architecture, no size, no training details.** dots-note-3.0 is described only by its position in a family.

**No independent reproduction is possible.** The weights are internal. The verification that exists is real and unusually good — an official committee marking submitted proofs is a far better check than a self-reported benchmark — but it is a one-time event on a model nobody else can run.

**Six problems is six data points.** A perfect score is a perfect score, and it is also a small sample of a very particular kind of mathematics.

## Why it still matters

Strip away what is unverifiable and one claim survives intact: a model produced six proofs that human olympiad graders certified as complete and rigorous, working from the original problem statements with no formalization step and no external checker. The correctness came from the model's own critique loop.

That is the interesting result, and it is not really about mathematics. Most valuable work — research, analysis, planning — cannot be formalized and cannot be automatically graded, which is exactly the regime where a model has to catch its own errors or nobody will. The IMO is a rare case where that regime has an authoritative answer key held by people whose job is to find holes.

They looked for holes in 31 pages and did not find any.
