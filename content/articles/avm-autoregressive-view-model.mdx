A Gaussian splat of a room is a promise about every viewpoint at once. You fit a few million ellipsoids to your photos, and from then on any camera you place gets a render that agrees with every other render, because they all come from the same ellipsoids. The price is that the splat knows nothing you didn't photograph. Point a camera at the unphotographed side of the sofa and you get floaters, or nothing.

A video model has the opposite problem. Give it one photo and a camera path and it will happily invent the other side of the sofa. Walk the camera away and back, though, and the sofa that comes back is a new sofa, because the model only conditions on its last few seconds of output and the first one has fallen out of its window.

[AVM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23417), the Autoregressive View Model from Meta and the University of Oxford, takes the video model's side and fixes the forgetting with a lookup. It never builds a 3D representation. It keeps every view it has seen or made, keyed by camera pose and timestamp, and before generating the next short chunk it fetches the old views whose cameras could see what the new one will see. The [project page](https://autoregressive-view-model.github.io/) puts it as "a scene as a stream of views". The code and weights are not out; the X post that brought it to my feed ([@jonstephens85](https://x.com/jonstephens85/status/2123612190586646908)) says so too: "No code yet."

## Views with coordinates

The unit AVM works in is a "view": an image (or a short block of video frames) paired with a camera pose and a timestamp. The paper's interface is phrased like a language model's. You prefill spatial prompts, then the model generates blocks of target views one at a time, each conditioned on the prompts and on its own earlier output.

<Figure
  src="/articles/avm-autoregressive-view-model/fig1.jpg"
  alt="Left, context views in two groups: prefill (four photos of a living room with camera icons) and history. They feed AVM, which outputs target views of the living room, each with its camera drawn as an inset; an arrow carries the targets back into history."
  caption="Prefill views and the model's own earlier clips both become context for the next clip. No 3D representation is fitted at any point (paper, Figure 1)."
/>

Three kinds of prompt share that one interface, and the model is trained on all of them:

- **Consolidated context**: images or clips of the same scene with their timestamps thrown away (temporal index set to 0). A set of photos of a room is this.
- **Synced context**: a clip from another camera at the same timestamps as the target. A second camera rig filming the same event is this.
- **Prefix context**: the first frames of the target clip itself, used to continue motion from one chunk to the next.

The architecture under it is ordinary. AVM fine-tunes Wan2.1-I2V-14B (from [Wan-AI](https://huggingface.co/Wan-AI)), a 14-billion-parameter image-to-video diffusion transformer trained with flow matching. Its VAE turns video into latents where each latent covers an $8\times 8\times 4$ block of pixels and frames. Context latents and target latents are concatenated into one sequence and the transformer attends over all of it.

<Figure
  src="/articles/avm-autoregressive-view-model/fig2.png"
  alt="Diagram: four context views (two images with unknown timestamps, a video clip with unknown timestamp, and a synced video clip at t = 0.0 s) and one noisy target video clip at t = 0.0 s feed one AVM transformer, which predicts flow for every slot; only the target's flow is used."
  caption="The N-in-1-out layout: N context slots (images, clips, a synced clip) and one target clip in one sequence. Context views with no timestamp say 't unknown'. The project page's version of the diagram (paper, Figure 3)."
/>

Each slot's input is three things stacked along channels: the noisy latent $\mathbf{z}_{i,t}$, a reference latent $\mathbf{c}_i$, and a binary mask $\mathbf{m}_i$. For context slots the reference is the clean latent and the mask is all ones; for target slots both are zero. That makes $2C+1$ input channels, and it is how the same network can be told "this slot is evidence, copy it" or "this slot is the question, denoise it". The paper calls it N-in-1-out: N context slots, one target clip. During training each of the N slots is randomly an image, a clip or a synced clip from the same scene, which is what lets one set of weights handle a single photo, a dense capture or a multi-camera rig.

The objective is plain rectified flow. With $\mathbf{z}_t=(1-t)\mathbf{z}_0+t\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, the model predicts a velocity and is trained against $\mathbf{v}=\boldsymbol{\epsilon}-\mathbf{z}_0$:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{FM}}=\mathbb{E}\,\lVert\hat{\mathbf{v}}-\mathbf{v}\rVert_2^2 .
$$

## Where the camera goes into the network

A view model needs the network to know where each view was taken. AVM splits the pose in two and encodes the halves differently, and this is the decision the rest of the design leans on.

**Rotation** goes in as absolute position embeddings derived from pixel-aligned camera rays: for each patch, the direction its pixel looks in, added to the patch embedding. This is the usual ray (Plücker-style) conditioning that view-synthesis models use, and it keeps orientation cues per pixel.

**Translation** goes in as rotary position embeddings applied to the attention queries and keys, in metric units and in the scene's original world coordinates. RoPE encodes position as a rotation of query and key vectors, so the attention score between two tokens depends on the difference of their positions, not on the positions themselves. The paper's reason: "RoPE keeps conditioning in distribution for large absolute coordinates". A camera 40 metres down a street produces the same kind of relative offsets to its neighbours as one at the origin, so walking far does not push the conditioning into values the network never saw in training. That is what the "perpetual view streaming" demos lean on.

**Timestamps** get sinusoidal embeddings added to the patch embeddings: constant for a static scene, increasing for a dynamic one. Text goes through T5 into cross-attention.

The camera poses are metric. That matters later: it is what makes "find the views near this camera" a geometric query with real units.

## The pool, and why the context stays bounded

Here is the mechanism the repost was excited about. Every view, whether it came from the user's input (the paper calls these **prefill**) or from the model's own output (**history**), goes into one shared context pool. Each entry carries its camera pose and timestamp. For each new chunk, AVM builds the context out of four parts:

1. **Sink**: prefill views kept for the whole rollout, so the input's style and content keep anchoring the output.
2. **Retrieved**: views pulled from the pool by camera pose (spatially adjacent views, as consolidated context) or by timestamp (temporally aligned views, as synced context).
3. **Motion**: the most recent latent frames, for temporal continuity.
4. **Target**: the block being generated.

The sequence length is

$$
L = L^{\mathrm{sin}} + L^{\mathrm{rtr}} + L^{\mathrm{win}}, \qquad L^{\mathrm{win}} = L^{\mathrm{mot}} + L^{\mathrm{tgt}} .
$$

The paper's examples for the local window: the base model generates long chunks, with $L^{\mathrm{mot}}=2$ and $L^{\mathrm{tgt}}=19$ latent frames; the causal model generates short blocks inside a fixed window, $L^{\mathrm{mot}}=15$ and $L^{\mathrm{tgt}}=3$. The pool can grow without limit. The attended context does not, because only a handful of pool entries are retrieved per chunk. That is the whole trick for long rollouts: memory is unbounded, attention is bounded, and the camera pose is the index.

The temporal indices get reassigned at every forward pass. Retrieved spatial views get index 0 (they are "same scene, time unknown"), sink views get 0, and motion plus target views form one consecutive run. So a view generated four minutes ago can sit in the context looking like a fresh photo of the place.

The widget below is a top-down toy of that bookkeeping. A camera walks a loop out of one end of a room and back. Every view joins the pool. At the target view, the sink is the first frame, the motion window is the last few frames, and retrieval adds a few old views by pose.

The thing to look at in the toy is what the motion window cannot give you. Recent frames see most of what is ahead, since a camera walking forward has been looking at the next stretch for a while. But they are the model's own recent output. An error there feeds straight into the next chunk, which is how video models drift. A retrieved view from the first pass, or the prefill sink, carries the original appearance of the place, and the model can copy from it. That is what a revisit needs.

## Picking which old views to fetch

How do you choose views by pose? The appendix compares three policies, and the toy implements a 2D version of each.

**Greedy nearest** ranks pool views by a pose distance,

$$
d(q,r)=\big\lVert P_q^{-1}(P_r-P_q)\big\rVert_{\mathrm{F}},
$$

where $P_q$ and $P_r$ are the $4\times 4$ projection matrices of the query and the candidate, and takes the closest ones. It is cheap. My reading of where it can fall short is redundancy: on a smooth trajectory the nearest poses can be frames just before the motion window, which look at what the window already covers. The paper does not break its failures down that way.

**Spatial** coverage samples 3D anchor points along rays through a $g\times g$ grid of the target image, at depths set from the median distance to pool cameras. A candidate "covers" an anchor if the anchor projects inside its image at positive depth. The policy repeatedly takes the candidate that covers the most still-uncovered anchors, within a radius, until the budget is full or nothing adds coverage. This is set cover, done greedily, and it prefers views that see different parts of the target's frustum.

**Spatial + angular**, the default, runs the spatial pass and then fills up to four target-relative headings (parallel, opposite, left, right) that are still missing, searching beyond the spatial radius if needed. Both coverage policies then apply pose non-maximum suppression.

The paper's Table 8 measures them on selected scenes, replaying camera poses under the base model's chunk schedule with $L^{\mathrm{win}}=21$:

| Policy | Mean retrieved slots | Overlap | Chunks with nothing retrieved |
|---|---|---|---|
| Greedy nearest | 5.3 | 73.0% | 0.0% |
| Spatial | 3.5 | 65.1% | 22.0% |
| Spatial + angular | 6.5 | 84.4% | 0.0% |

Greedy nearest is not the worst of the three on overlap: it beats the spatial pass alone (73.0% against 65.1%), because the spatial pass comes back empty on 22% of chunks, when nothing in the pool within its radius covers the new view's anchors. The angular pass fixes that by reaching further for views facing other ways, and the combination leads. The toy is not a replication of that table. Its pool holds only the walk's own history, and at its default target greedy nearest happens to fetch recent near-duplicates while the spatial passes fetch frames from the first pass; move the target and the ordering changes.

This retrieval is geometric, computed from poses alone: no image features, no learned scoring. It also needs the poses to be right. The pool is indexed by metric camera poses supplied with the input (or requested by the user for generated views), so a capture whose poses are wrong retrieves the wrong views. The paper leaves other cues, such as geotags on Internet photos, for later.

For the causal model the pool is not a pile of images. Every pooled view is stored as its key-value cache, so retrieving a view means loading its KV states, not re-encoding it. The paper notes that retrievable KV states can be offloaded to CPU and loaded back to the GPU when selected. During prefill, the causal model fills its cache with one zero-noise forward pass per input block.

## Making it causal

A bidirectional video diffusion model denoises a whole clip at once over 50 steps. That is the base model: 50 Euler steps with classifier-free guidance, with separate guidance on context and target views for static scenes ($s=1.2$ on context, $s=5$ on targets) and text-only guidance for dynamic ones ($s=5$ throughout).

For streaming, the paper turns it into a causal model in two stages.

**Off-policy.** Keep the flow-matching loss but switch to sparse attention: bidirectional within a block, causal across blocks. Train with a mix of teacher forcing (condition on clean copies of earlier blocks) and diffusion forcing (condition on noisy earlier blocks). The paper is candid about why it mixes: teacher forcing halves the effective context length, and diffusion forcing creates a train-test mismatch because inference conditions on denoised history. The attention mask in this stage gives synced context a six-view window and diffusion forcing an 18-view window.

**On-policy.** Then distil on the model's own rollouts with distribution matching distillation plus an adversarial loss, which brings sampling down to 4 steps with guidance folded in. The rollouts grow longer over training. Their camera paths are 80% the ground-truth trajectory reflected out and back (to reach the requested length while advancing time) and 20% synthetic paths from primitives (dolly, pan, orbit, spiral, figure-eight, revisit loops). And the distillation is "context-augmented": both score models see the same sink, retrieved and motion context that inference will use, instead of only the one overlap frame standard distillation conditions on.

There is one more training trick worth naming, error-recycling fine-tuning. The model's own prediction errors are stored in a timestep-binned bank and added back to later batches' noisy latents and context latents, with probability 0.5 per batch of getting none. It teaches the network that its context is sometimes slightly wrong, which in a rollout it always is.

The ablation shows how much the on-policy stage matters. Right after causal initialization, revisit PSNR on the WorldScore loop-closure paths is 10.12. After on-policy tuning it is 28.04, against 28.53 for the slow base model.

## What the tables say

All of these are the paper's numbers, on its own benchmark protocol, with frames scored at 448×448. None of them were reproduced, because nothing was released.

**Revisits.** On 26 WorldScore static scenes with a 721-frame loop-closure path, revisit PSNR is 28.04 for AVM against 12.29 for the best world-model baseline (DreamX-World), 11.06 for LingBot-World and 10.70 for HY-WorldPlay 1.5. Turn retrieval off and it falls to 11.13, the same level as the baselines. That one ablation is the strongest evidence in the paper that the pose-keyed lookup, not the bigger backbone, is what keeps revisits consistent. Shrinking the local window from 21 to 15 latent frames barely moves it (28.04 to 28.00), which supports the bounded-context argument.

The same table has a number that cuts the other way, and the abstract does not mention it. On the "3D consistency" metric for revisits, AVM scores 53.13 and LingBot-World 57.00. AVM leads on five of the six revisit metrics; this is the sixth. The paper's own summary says "five of six".

**Camera control.** From one starting image, on DL3DV's 141 scenes (first 81 frames of each path), AVM's camera score is 96.48 against SEVA's 97.07. On WorldScore's static split (26 scenes, 721 frames), it is 70.67 against SEVA's 68.94, GEN3C's 83.74 and 3.91 for the best world model. So: in the range of dedicated view-synthesis models (ahead of one, behind another), and far more controllable than world models that take action or camera commands without metric grounding.

**Sparse reconstruction** from seven posed images on DL3DV: PSNR 24.14 against SEVA's 23.91, LPIPS 0.100 against 0.106, geometric matching 0.83 against 0.75. Modest on fidelity, as the paper says itself.

**4D reconstruction** on MultiCam (render a second camera's 81 synchronized frames from the first camera's video): PSNR 21.16 against 13.53 for ReCamMaster.

**Visual quality**: FID 18.69 on DL3DV against a next-best 25.04, which mostly says the 14B Wan prior survived fine-tuning.

**Where it is weak**, by its own tables: text alignment is 39.65 against 54.07 for the best world model (the garden-party prompt in Figure 5 still produces an indoor room), and its dynamic degree is the lowest of the four models, 24.88 against 35.55. Fewer of its videos are classified as moving at all. A model trained mostly on static posed scenes, with a camera-unconditional slice of 2M unlabeled clips to keep the video prior alive, moves the camera well and the world less.

The training set is about 6M labeled scenes (ScanNet++, RealEstate10K, DL3DV, SpatialVID, uCO3D, synthetic environments, game renders, multi-camera video, cubemaps) and about 2M unlabeled clips, trained on five-second clips with a resolution curriculum from 448×448 up to 720×1280.

What the paper does not give: any speed. There is no frames-per-second figure, no latency, no GPU count for inference. The causal model's 4-step sampling with KV caching is built for streaming, and the page says the design "adapts to future scaling and real-time deployment", but nothing in the paper says it runs in real time today. A 14B diffusion transformer with dozens of context latents per chunk is not something I would assume runs interactively on anything short of a datacenter GPU.

## Against splats, honestly

[3D Gaussian splatting](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.04079) and AVM answer the same query (render this camera) with opposite storage.

A splat stores **geometry**. After fitting, a render is a rasterization: milliseconds, deterministic, and two renders from nearby cameras agree by construction because they read the same primitives. Consistency is free; coverage is the cost. [Capture pipelines](/articles/3dgs-capture-pipelines) spend all their effort on getting enough well-posed views, and a [single-image splat](/articles/depth-pro-2-single-image-splats) only works because a depth model fills in geometry that was never seen.

AVM stores **evidence**. Its "scene" is the pool of posed views plus 14B parameters of prior. A render is a few denoising passes of a giant transformer over retrieved views. Consistency is not guaranteed by anything; it is learned, and it holds as well as retrieval finds the right views and the model copies from them. In exchange, coverage stops being a hard limit: unseen regions get plausible content, the scene can move, and a text prompt can steer what appears.

Some concrete consequences of that difference, from reading the paper rather than running anything:

- **Two renders of the same pose need not match.** A splat re-renders identically. AVM samples; the same camera visited twice gets two samples conditioned on different retrieved context. Revisit PSNR of 28.04 is good for a generator, and a splat of a well-captured room would beat it as a reconstruction by construction. The comparison the paper makes is with other generators.
- **Nothing to measure.** There is no point you can click on and get a coordinate. If your use is measurement, as-built capture or anything metric (mine usually is), AVM gives you pictures, not a model.
- **No per-scene optimization.** Dense prefill loads 189 photos as context with a fast prefill, no fitting. For splats that fitting step is minutes to hours per scene.
- **Cost per frame is inverted.** A splat is expensive once and cheap forever. AVM is free to "fit" and expensive for every frame you look at.

The site's [Echo-3 teardown](/articles/echo-3-world-model) is the explicit side in a shipping product: a few minutes of phone video turned into a metric splat room of about 7.3 million flat discs, cheap to render forever after. AVM never makes that conversion.

## The neighbours

**WorldCrafter.** The closest relative on this site is [WorldCrafter](/articles/worldcrafter), which also keeps a video world model consistent on revisits by picking past frames by how much of the upcoming views they cover. The selection idea is nearly the same as AVM's spatial pass. The differences: WorldCrafter runs its picks through an encoder inherited from a view-synthesis model and reads out latents at the future camera, and it released weights. AVM puts everything into one 14B transformer's context, covers images, video and multi-camera rigs with one interface, and released nothing.

**DVSM.** The `nvidia/DVSM` Space is [DVSM: Decoder-only View Synthesis Model Done Right](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29891), from NVIDIA. Its abstract describes a decoder-only network that "represents scenes implicitly as a KV-cache". That is the same storage idea as AVM's pooled KV states, in a view-synthesis model: its abstract benchmarks novel-view synthesis from input views, against per-scene-optimized 3DGS among others, and does not describe generating unseen regions. Its Space runs: you upload images or a short orbit, it estimates poses with π³, and a time-boxed viewer renders new views, with an upload budget equivalent to 64 views at 960 px. If you want to feel what "the scene is a cache of views" means today, that is the one to try.

**Camera-driven generation** elsewhere on the site: [Atlas Camera Studio](/articles/marble-camera-studio) turns one photo into a depth point cloud, lets you draw a camera path over it and has a model paint a frame at each camera, and [Cosmos](/articles/cosmos-world-model) is a general world model. AVM's tables are mostly an argument that metric poses plus retrieval beat world models on control and revisits. And [Déjà View](/articles/deja-view) or the [streaming reconstruction](/articles/da3-streaming-reconstruction) systems go the other way entirely: from views to poses and depth, which is what AVM assumes it is given.

## What I think

The idea I will remember from this paper is the indexing, not the model. Keying generated frames by metric pose and fetching them back by frustum coverage is a cheap, explicit memory that any video world model can bolt on, and the ablation (28.04 with it, 11.13 without) makes the case better than any of the demos. The bounded-context argument is also clean: the pool grows, the attention does not, and shrinking the window barely matters once retrieval is doing the work.

What I don't buy yet is the framing that this replaces 3D. It replaces 3D storage, at the price of a 14B forward pass per chunk and consistency that is statistical. For exploring, previewing or directing a virtual camera through a scene you only half photographed, that trade is attractive. For anything where two looks at the same corner must agree to the millimetre, a splat or a mesh still wins. AVM's own retrieval also depends on metric poses that something explicit, an SfM or SLAM system, had to produce.

And until there are weights, a speed number and a benchmark someone else has run, the numbers above are the authors' on their own protocol. The paper and the page are both clear about the artifacts that remain. I would watch this one, not plan around it.
