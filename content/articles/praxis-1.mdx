On 2026-11-11 Runway released Praxis-1, which it calls a world action model, with open weights under
Apache 2.0. There are three policies on Hugging Face, a
[base](https://huggingface.co/runwayml/praxis-1-base), a single-arm
[DROID policy](https://huggingface.co/runwayml/praxis-1-droid) and a bimanual
[YAM policy](https://huggingface.co/runwayml/praxis-1-yam), plus NVFP4 and LeRobot variants. Inference and
fine-tuning code is at [`runwayml/praxis-1-oss`](https://github.com/runwayml/praxis-1-oss), and a long
[technical report](https://runway.com/research/praxis-1-video-pretraining-to-robot-control) stands in for a
paper. The launch thread on X made four claims: first place on NVIDIA's RoboLab-120 leaderboard, ahead of
every open VLA including π0.5 and GR00T N1.6; 15 of 15 simulated trials on a Noble Machines robot after
under 30 minutes of teleoperated demonstrations; and a run 15.6x faster than GPT-6 Astra driving the same
arm, at 51x lower cost per completed task.

For this piece I read every safetensors header of the DROID checkpoint by HTTP range request and summed
the shapes. I read the sampler, the model and the server code at commit `68f4d7a` (2026-11-11), the
configs, and NVIDIA's NeMo fine-tuning tutorial. I also read the live RoboLab leaderboard, and I went back
to the launch thread's videos. I ran no model. Nothing here was served on a GPU.

The finding that changed how I read the whole release is in the sampler, so the mechanism comes first.

## Three ways to turn pixels into motor commands

A robot **policy** maps what the robot sees and feels to what it does next. Modern ones emit an
**action chunk**, a short trajectory of future commands executed before the policy looks again. One
expensive network call is then spread over many control ticks.

Three families compete for that job.

A **VLA** (vision-language-action model) starts from a vision-language model and adds a head that
outputs actions. π0.5 and GR00T N1.6 are the open reference points. The language model brings
semantics: it knows what "the purple comb" is. It brings nothing about how a comb moves when pushed,
because no text corpus teaches contact dynamics.

A **world model** predicts the future of a scene given an action. It is a simulator you can roll
policies through. Runway uses one that way for policy evaluation, and its
[September post](https://runway.com/research/introducing-praxis-1) claims a 0.95 correlation between success inside its world
model and success on hardware. A world model does not pick actions.

A **world action model** (WAM) predicts the future frames and the actions together. The bet is that the
same weights that must imagine what the scene looks like after the gripper closes will learn which
motion gets it there. Video is cheap and abundant; teleoperation is neither. Runway's September post
puts a number on the bet: fine-tuned placement error of 16.1 cm after pretraining on web video against
16.0 cm after pretraining on the same hours of teleoperated robot video, inside one standard error over
93 evaluation pairs. If that holds, the bottleneck on a policy becomes how much ordinary video a model
can digest. This site has covered three other WAMs: [FLUX 3 Action](/articles/flux-3-action),
NVIDIA's [Cosmos 3](/articles/cosmos-world-model) policy variants and [Flex-π](/articles/flex-pi).

The cost of a WAM is tokens. Video is thousands of them per second, actions a few dozen. Every design in
this family is an answer to the question of how much imagined video you pay for per action, and when.

## What is in the 54.4 GB

Praxis-1 has three parts. A video backbone predicts the next stretch of video. A small **action expert**
reads the backbone's hidden states and predicts the motion. Thin per-robot projections translate each
robot's poses and actions into the shared format. Runway's architecture figure:

The weights tell the same story in more detail. Summing all 567 tensor shapes in the 17 shards of
`praxis-1-droid` gives 27,202,371,308 parameters. Hugging Face's API says 27,202,371,288. The gap of 20
is the twenty scalar tensors of shape `[]`, one per backbone block (I counted them), which the API skips. The file

|---|---:|

| **Total** | **27.202B** |

The backbone is a diffusion transformer split into a condition encoder and a velocity decoder, the DDT
layout of [Wang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05741). It works on latents of a frozen Wan2.2 VAE
and reads instructions through a frozen Qwen3.5-2B. Seventeen of its 20 feed-forward layers are
mixture-of-experts. Each MoE layer has one shared expert and 128 routed experts, and every token takes
the shared one plus eight routed ones, balanced the [DeepSeek-V3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437) way.

The config shows why the report can say per-token compute "stays about the same as a dense block". Every expert has a
hidden width of 1,280, and nine of them make 11,520, which is exactly `dense_ffn_hidden`, the width of the
three dense layers. A token pays for one dense FFN's worth of matrix multiplies while the layer stores 129
experts. Applying 8 of 128 to my routed total gives 2.823B active parameters in the backbone, which
matches the report's 2.8B.

### Tokens per chunk

Time is cut into **chunks** of 1.6 s: six latent frames, 24 frames at 15 fps, 24 action rows at 15 Hz.
The DROID config fixes the shapes, and multiplying them out shows where the compute goes. Two cameras,
exterior and wrist, are resized to 224×416 tiles and placed side by side on a 224×832 canvas. The VAE
compresses 16× in each spatial direction, giving a 14×52 latent with 48 channels, and the backbone
patchifies 2×2. That is 7×26 = 182 tokens per latent frame and **1,092 video tokens per chunk**. On the
action side, the expert holds one pose token, eight learned "future" tokens and six action tokens per
chunk. Each action token packs four rows of the 10-D action (`action_patch_dim: 40`). Fifteen expert
tokens against 1,092 video tokens: the arm's motion is a rounding error in the sequence.

An action row for the Franka is 10 numbers: xyz, a 6-D rotation and the gripper. Position and rotation
are relative to the pose at the start of the chunk. Position and gripper are rescaled to [-1, 1] by the
1st and 99th percentiles of DROID, and the exact bounds are written into `config.json`, so no dataset
statistics are needed at inference.

### Chunk-causal attention

The base video model generated whole clips with every frame seeing every other, including later ones. A
robot cannot see its own future, so mid-training switches the attention to **chunk-causal**: chunk $k$
attends to itself and to clean copies of chunks before it. In training, those earlier chunks are real
video (teacher forcing), which lets Runway train four chunks in one parallel pass over a 93-frame window.
The action expert gets a mask of its own:

That last column is the whole idea. The actions for the next 1.6 s are computed by reading eight layers
of the backbone while it denoises its picture of the same 1.6 s.

## Joint denoising, and what "jointly" means here

Both streams are trained with rectified flow. Take a clean sample $x_0$ (a chunk of video latents, or a
chunk of action rows), Gaussian noise $\varepsilon$ and a time $t \in [0, 1]$, and mix them along a
straight line:

$$
x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,\varepsilon, \qquad v = \varepsilon - x_0 .
$$

The network $v_\theta(x_t, t, c)$ learns the velocity $v$ given conditioning $c$: the instruction, the
clean context frames, the pose. Runway's loss is an equal-weight sum of the video and action velocity
losses. Each chunk draws its own video noise level, weighted toward high noise, and its own action noise
level, uniform and independent. The independence matters: the expert learns to read the backbone when the
imagined video is nearly clean, when it is nearly pure noise, and everywhere between, regardless of how
noisy its own actions are.

At inference the sampler walks $t$ from 1 to 0 with Euler steps,

$$
x_{t'} = x_t + (t' - t)\,v_\theta(x_t, t, c),
$$

and the clean-sample prediction the network implies at any point is $\hat{x}_0 = x_t - t\,v_\theta$.

In the released code, "jointly" runs in one direction only. The docstring of
`plan_window_joint` says it outright: "No trunk pass reads the actions or the proprio: the video Euler
state moves with the video velocity only." The video stream is denoised as if no actions existed. The
action stream is denoised by the expert, which reads the backbone's taps at each step. Video shapes
actions, and never the reverse. During training, the action loss does push gradients into the backbone
through those cross-attention reads, so the backbone learns features the expert finds useful. At
inference the backbone never sees the expert's output.

## Two steps, and a video that never finishes

The shipped settings are in `configs/serve_droid.yaml` and `configs/sim_droid.yaml`: `num_steps: 2`,
`t_shift: 12.5`, `cfg_video_scale: 4.0`, `cfg_scale: 1.0`. The time grid comes from
`window_time_grid` in `praxis/sampler.py`:

$$
u_j = 1 - \frac{j}{N}, \qquad t_j = \frac{s\,u_j}{1 + (s-1)\,u_j}.
$$

With $N = 2$ and $s = 12.5$, $u = (1, 0.5, 0)$ becomes $t = (1, 0.926, 0)$. So the model is evaluated
twice: once on pure noise, once at $t = 0.926$, where the latent is 92.6% noise by mixing weight. The
first Euler step moves the state 7.4% of the way. The second covers the remaining 92.6% in one jump.

That jump has a useful property. The last Euler step goes from $t$ to 0, so

$$
x_0 = x_t - t\,v_\theta(x_t, t, c) = \hat{x}_0(x_t, t, c).
$$

The output of the sampler is exactly the network's clean prediction made at its last evaluation. For
Praxis-1 that means the 24 action rows the robot executes are the action expert's one-shot guess, made
while it reads a backbone whose video latent is still 92.6% noise. Runway's report describes the knob in
softer words: "A larger shift leaves more of the denoising to the second step."

Then there is `skip_video_decoder: on`, set in both serve configs. The server code explains it: the last
step "moves only the actions, which read only the encoder taps", so the video decoder is skipped and "the
plan has no video (None)". Counting the passes in `plan_window_joint` for one served DROID call:

1. Step 1 at $t = 1$: encoder and decoder with the instruction, encoder and decoder with a blank caption
   (video guidance 4 needs both), one Euler step on the video latent.
2. Step 2 at $t = 0.926$: the encoder only, with the instruction. No decoder, no unguided pass.
3. Two passes of the action expert, one on each step's taps.

Three encoder passes, two decoder passes, two expert passes. The robot moves on actions read from a
future that the model starts imagining and abandons 7.4% of the way in. The "predicted chunk of
future video" in the report's prose exists on the server only when you turn the flag off; it is not
in the default response. The README is plain about this, so it is not a hidden trick. It does mean the
"world" half of this world action model is, at serving time, a set of hidden states.

The video guidance still matters, through a narrow channel. At step 1 the backbone sees only noise plus
context, so its taps depend on the context and the instruction. Guidance acts on the video velocity of
step 1, and that guided velocity moves the latent the 7.4% that the step-2 encoder pass then reads. Runway's
guidance search (section 9 of the report) found that adding video guidance and raising the shift
together ended 4.0 points above its start of no guidance at shift 8. Pushing either further scored lower.
The report says the search ran on "a fixed set of simulated environments" and does not say which; I could
not tell from the repository whether those were RoboLab tasks.

With 8 steps and no shift, the sampler resolves the choice by itself: by $t \approx 0.25$ the noise is
small enough that the partial chunk already looks like one reach or the other, and the posterior
collapses. With Praxis-1's 2 steps at shift 12.5, the last read is at 0.926, the partial chunk is almost
all noise, the posterior still equals the context prior, and the output is the prior-weighted average of
the two reaches. At 50/50 that is a reach for the empty space between the mugs. The toy can't speak for
the learned model, whose conditioning makes most scenes unambiguous. What it shows is where the choice has
to come from at these settings: the context frames, the instruction and the barely started imagination,
not the iteration.

Runway's own error analysis points the same way. On 32 held-out DROID windows, the mean end-effector error
is 5.71 cm when the model samples its own video and actions, and 1.14 cm when it is handed the true future
video, noised to the sampler's level, and samples only the actions.

## The control loop, 1.6 seconds at a time

Each call returns 24 rows (21 on the first call of a context window, whose first latent frame covers one
frame instead of four). In the default **chunked** schedule the robot executes all of them, sends back the
frames it saw over one WebSocket, and calls again. The report gives p50 server times on one H100 of 522 ms
for the DROID policy and 545 ms for the bimanual one, and about 0.55 to 0.6 s round trip.

Waiting is the price. If a call takes $L$ seconds and a chunk lasts $H = 1.6$ s, a synchronous loop holds
still for $L / (L + H)$ of the time. The report measured that on its bimanual station: 32% holding still
when calls took 0.8 to 0.9 s, and 16% at about 0.3 s. The formula gives 33.3 to 36.0% and 15.8%. Close
enough that the loop is doing what it says.

**Real-time chunking** (RTC), opt-in in the repo, asks for the next chunk before the current one ends. The
hard part is the junction. Runway's blended plans made the arm oscillate, so the new plan starts from
where the arm will be. On the bimanual station RTC cut holding still to 5% and 3%. On a five-object
pick-and-place into a bag it finished faster (median 9.1 s against 15.2 s) at 24 of 30 successes against
28 of 30 chunked. With streaming optimizations the p50 call drops to 124 ms (DROID) and 128 ms (YAM).

<ChunkClock />

The slider exposes the other cost of chunking: reaction time. A cube that slips just after a call took its
frames is first seen by the next call, a full chunk plus one call later, and acted on one call after that.
At 545 ms that is 2.69 s in the worst case. RTC shortens it by one call, not by a chunk; the chunk length
itself is what bounds how fast this policy can notice anything.

## Four stages of training

Runway trains the world before the actions. Each stage starts from the one before, and the video loss stays
on throughout.

Stage one is Runway's video foundation model, trained on twelve generation tasks with full-sequence
attention. Stage two switches to chunk-causal attention on embodied video at 15 fps: 58% egocentric human
video, 20% general video, 18% real-robot video and under 1% gameplay, by tokens. Stage three attaches the
expert with fresh weights and adds action targets for single arms, arm pairs, handheld grippers and human
hands, ramping the action gradient into the backbone from zero over 1,500 updates and keeping about 30% of
tasks video-only. Stage four fine-tunes for one robot; for DROID that is about 277 hours of successful
episodes, 128 H100s and 50k steps.

The fine-tune is a full fine-tune of 27.2B parameters. The per-robot projections are the only new weights,
but every backbone matrix moves. NVIDIA's NeMo tutorial does the same on one 8-GPU node, with the 128
routed experts split 16 per GPU so the FP32 optimizer state fits.

Runway's compute-scaling test is the strongest evidence that mid-training matters. Five DROID fine-tunes
from successive mid-training checkpoints scored 25.5%, 35.2%, 39.2%, 41.2% and 41.2% on RoboLab-120.
On RT-1, a robot absent from all mid-training, five fine-tunes rose from 56.3% to 80.0% on SimplerEnv,
though not monotonically (65.6%, 66.9%, 64.1% between). Runway calls its fitted line "a guide rather than
a law", which is the right amount of confidence for five points. The released DROID policy scores 46.2%,
not 41.2%; the guidance and shift search accounts for some of the gap, and the report does not reconcile
the rest.

- **It is one run.** There is no seed table. The FLUX 3 Action entry it displaces was the best of six seeds

The ratios from the rounded table come out at 15.8x and 46.75x, not 15.6x and 51x. That fits rounding:
\$0.04 could be anything from \$0.035 to \$0.045. At \$3.95 an hour, \$0.04 buys about 36 seconds of H100
per completed task, and \$0.08 on the pen cap about 73 seconds, against a 15-second average success. So
either Praxis-1 failed a fair share of pen-cap attempts or the GPU was billed for time outside the episode.
The report doesn't say which.

The bigger issue is what is being compared. The YAM fine-tunes learn from roughly 1,400 to 2,600 hours
of bimanual YAM recordings, per the report. Astra ran zero-shot through a
tool interface, and the fine-tuning bill is not in Praxis-1's \$0.04. The report is fair about this in
prose: it calls the two approaches complementary and notes that frontier-model agents currently hold the
top two places on RoboLab. The speed gap is real and large. The tweet's "dramatically outperforms frontier
LLMs" is a statement about latency and cost per task on trained tasks, not about capability.

### Open, and how open

The Apache 2.0 claim checks out. All eight repositories carry `license: apache-2.0`, none is gated, and the
frozen dependencies, Qwen3.5-2B (also Apache 2.0) and the Wan2.2 VAE, download from their own repos. That
makes Praxis-1 the most permissively licensed WAM this site has looked at; FLUX 3 Action's weights carry a
non-commercial licence with a revenue cap.

The launch post's hardware list, "from data center GPUs like H100 and B200, to workstation GPUs like RTX
5090", needs a footnote. The BF16 checkpoint is 54.4 GB, plus 4.55 GB of text
encoder and a 2.82 GB VAE file, and the README asks for one 80 GB GPU. A 32 GB card needs the NVFP4 path.
Its repos are bf16 QAT master weights, 54.4 GB to download either way; the routed experts are packed to
NVFP4 at load time, shrinking the policy in memory to 17.5 GB. My own arithmetic agrees: 25.669B routed
weights at 4.5 bits each (4-bit values plus one 8-bit scale per 16) is 14.4 GB, the size the card gives
for the packed experts, and the other 1.53B parameters in BF16 add 3.1 GB. The NVFP4 card says the native
path runs on SM120 GPUs only, while the code README lists SM100, SM110 and SM120. Runway's quantization test
found NVFP4 at 279 of 600 RoboLab episodes against 287 for BF16, and 26 of 30 against 28 of 30 on the YAM
bag task, neither difference significant.

NVIDIA's [NeMo tutorial](https://github.com/NVIDIA-NeMo/Automodel/blob/main/tutorials/praxis-1-libero/README.md)
is the most useful independent number in the release. On LIBERO-Spatial, the DROID checkpoint zero-shot
fails all 500 episodes; it "moves smoothly and purposefully, but towards the wrong things". A full
fine-tune of 600 steps on 8 H100s (1 h 52 min) reaches 451 of 500, and a LoRA run of the same length 457 of
500. Two caveats on the tutorial. Those are NVIDIA's numbers, which I did not reproduce. And it says the
evaluation used text guidance 3.0, while `sim_droid.yaml` at the commit I read says 4.0.

## Where it breaks

- **The imagined video is not returned by default.** With `skip_video_decoder: on`, the server returns
  actions and no video. Turning it off adds three passes to the last step (the guided decoder, and an
  unguided encoder and decoder for video guidance), and the actions come out bit-identical. Anyone who
  wants the predictions for monitoring pays for them.
- **Ambiguous targets.** Runway's own limitations: look-alike objects and vague instructions are the common
  failures. The toy above suggests why few sampler steps leave that choice to the context, though it
  is a toy.
- **Long tasks.** Multi-step tasks work when each step gets its own instruction. One prompt for a chain of
  steps does not. Runway suggests pairing it with a language model planner.
- **Reaction time.** Chunked execution at 1.6 s per chunk means up to 2.69 s before a disturbance changes
  the motion, at the default latency.
- **Every new robot is a full fine-tune of 27.2B parameters,** or LoRA on one 80 GB GPU. The success
  predictor in section 10.3 (AUROC 0.77 on 272 real episodes) was trained on an earlier unreleased model
  and is not in the release.

## What I think of it

Praxis-1 is the best open-weight world action model I have read, and the licence is the most important part
of the release. Apache 2.0 weights, code, a MuJoCo sim, LoRA recipes and an NVIDIA fine-tuning tutorial
mean a lab can actually build on it, which was not true of FLUX 3 Action.

The benchmark story is good and overstated in the usual ways. Fifth overall, first in its class, from one
verified run; a real lead on complex tasks that the launch didn't mention; a Noble Machines number with
nothing behind it; and an Astra comparison that measures the price of zero-shot generality, not a
capability gap.

What I will take away is the sampler. The marketing image of a WAM is a model that watches a movie of the
future and then acts it out. The shipped Praxis-1 starts the movie, runs it 7.4% of the way, and reads the
motion off the backbone's hidden states while the frames are still almost pure noise. Finishing the movie
would not help: the decoder is skipped because the actions never read it, and they come out identical with
it on. How noisy the last read is does not look like the bottleneck either. Runway's guidance search ran at two
steps and moved toward a higher shift, 12.5 from a start of 8, which makes the last read noisier, not
cleaner. And the oracle test reads a true future noised to the same level and gets 1.14 cm instead of 5.71. What matters is which
future the backbone starts imagining.

The knob nobody has turned in public is the step count. Every number in the report is at two steps. If I
had an 80 GB card for an afternoon, I would run the simulated `hard` suite with `num_steps` at 4 and 8.
More evaluations give the backbone more chances to commit to one future before the expert reads it, which
is the look-alike failure Runway names first. If that buys nothing, the 5.71 cm is a pretraining problem,
and the answer is more video, which is Runway's bet anyway.
