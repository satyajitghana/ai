Three text-to-speech models crossed my feed within a day of each other, and every one of them sold itself as small.

A Japanese account [posted Kitten TTS 2 Mini](https://x.com/aiaicreate/status/2121793930358563024): 82M parameters, 16 languages, 118 voices, an 82.7 MB int8 ONNX file, no PyTorch needed. [TeksEdge posted](https://x.com/TeksEdge/status/2121756314682708184) a "1.8GB Local AI model" that clones voices and narrates whole audiobooks on a Ryzen CPU: TontaubeV1.1, with the link in a reply. And Muhammed Nazeem [posted tiny-voca](https://x.com/n4ze3m/status/2121799813679419515): "a text-to-speech model that has never heard a real human voice. 4.5M parameters. 18 MB. Runs on a CPU."

Small means three different things there. For Kitten it means bytes: a model the size of Kokoro, stored at one byte per weight. For Tontaube it means 4-bit: a 2.9B-parameter speech language model squeezed until a desktop CPU can almost keep up. For tiny-voca it means parameters, honestly few of them, and a waveform generator that is barely a neural network at all. I read each one's config, safetensors headers, inference code and card, checked the posts against them, and then ran all three on the same five texts on one machine.

## Kitten TTS 2 Mini is Kokoro's graph, retrained

The [model card](https://huggingface.co/KittenML/kitten-tts-2-mini) never says what the network is. The `config.json` does. Its `architecture` block reads `hidden_dim 512`, `n_layer 3`, `n_token 178`, `style_dim 128`, `max_dur 50`, an ALBERT with hidden size 768 and 12 layers sharing one group, and an `istftnet` decoder with upsample rates `[10, 6]`, `gen_istft_n_fft 20` and hop 5. I put it next to [Kokoro-82M's](https://huggingface.co/hexgrad/Kokoro-82M) `config.json` and every one of those values matches. The safetensors header agrees: 548 float32 tensors, 81,763,410 elements, a 327,115,192-byte file against Kokoro's 327,212,226-byte `.pth`. The class names in [KittenML's package](https://github.com/KittenML/KittenTTS) (`ProsodyPredictor`, `AdainResBlk1d`, `DurationEncoder`, and a comment that the style encoders, the diffusion model and the discriminators were left out because synthesis reads the style from the voice pack) are StyleTTS 2's.

So this is the architecture the site already took apart in [Paradee](/articles/paradee-tiny-tts). Phonemes (from a normaliser, the misaki lexicon, CMUdict and a 0.75M-parameter BART for unknown words; espeak-ng for other languages) go through PL-BERT and a text encoder. A prosody predictor, conditioned on a style vector, gives each phoneme its frames, pitch and energy. The ISTFTNet decoder predicts a short-time spectrum and an inverse STFT makes the 24 kHz waveform. The header's grouping shows where the weight is: 53,276,190 parameters in the decoder, 16,194,612 in the prosody predictor, 6,686,208 in PL-BERT and 5,606,400 in the text encoder. Two thirds of an "82M TTS model" is its vocoder.

### What a voice is here

There is no speaker encoder at inference. A voice is a float32 array of shape `[510, 256]` in `voices_<lang>.npz`, and the code picks the row matching the chunk's phoneme count: two 128-dimensional style vectors, one for the decoder and one for the prosody predictor. That is Kokoro's design too.

That changes what "118 voices" means. I counted the `voices` block of the config: 118 entries, 73 distinct names. English has 11 voices, and nine of those characters appear again in German, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese and Russian, each with its own pack. The other languages get "Female 1" and "Male 2"; Turkish gets two voices, both female.

The card says the English voices "carry the names of the same characters in KittenTTS 2," the company's 1.7B voice-cloning speech language model. The KittenTTS 2 repository's `voices.json` does list `55_somber_historian_female` as Eleanor, `63_rumbling_blues_musician_male` as Otis, and so on, each with an 11-second reference clip. That hints the Mini's English voices were learned from the big model's speech, the same teacher-student pattern as tiny-voca. The card does not say so, and I found no statement anywhere of what the Mini was trained on.

### The int8 part is done carefully

The config's `quantization` field reads "int8, symmetric, per output channel (DequantizeLinear)", float32 activations, and "post-training quantization + quantization-aware distillation from the fp32 model." The package's docs give the reasoning. Dynamic int8 activation quantization (`MatMulInteger`, `ConvInteger`), which the older Kitten 0.8 models used, "was measured and rejected: on these vocoders it gives audible spectral error and non-finite audio on part of the inputs." So every weight is stored as int8 and expanded to float32 before the arithmetic. The text side and the prosody predictor were then distilled through the exact int8 rounding the graph uses, then the decoder, so the shipped weights are the ones that were trained. The docs claim the same Whisper character error rate as fp32 and a speaker-embedding similarity of about 0.999, though the Mini's card carries no per-model numbers.

Weight-only int8 suits a vocoder, whose activations carry phase; phase errors are what Paradee's author chased as a buzz. Rounding weights still moves the output, hence the distillation through the rounding.

### Checking the post

- 82M parameters, 16 languages, 24 kHz, an 82.7 MB int8 ONNX file: all hold (81,763,410 parameters; the `.onnx` is 82,711,386 bytes).
- 118 voices: 118 voice packs under 73 names.
- No PyTorch needed: true of the ONNX graph. But the `kittenml` package's `setup.py` installs `torch>=2.6`, `torchaudio`, `transformers`, `diffusers` and spaCy, because one package also serves the 1.7B KittenTTS 2. To deploy without PyTorch you carry the graph, vocab, voice pack and front end yourself.
- Licence: Apache-2.0, models, voice packs and G2P files included.

The Mini's siblings, Nano (17M) and Pico (7.2M), ship one model per language for eight languages; the Mini is one graph for all 16.

## TontaubeV1.1: four transformers, one per codebook

Tontaube is a different kind of model, a speech language model in the family the site covered with [Breeze TTS 2](/articles/breeze-tts-2) and [Qwen Audio 3.0 TTS](/articles/qwen-audio-3-tts). It comes from Fritz and Jonathan Cremer in Berlin, with a [TontaubeV1 technical report](https://tontaube.ai/papers/tontaube-v1-technical-report.pdf), [full-precision weights](https://huggingface.co/TontaubeAI/TontaubeV1.1), the [int4 release](https://huggingface.co/TontaubeAI/TontaubeV1.1-int4) the post is about, and an [inference server](https://github.com/craitech/tontaube).

### Speech as four rows of codes

Speech is encoded by [DualCodec](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2025/li25e_interspeech.html) at 12.5 Hz: eight residual vector-quantized streams, one code each every 80 ms. The first, quantized from w2v-BERT 2.0 features, carries phonetic content and timing (16,384 entries); each of the others encodes what the streams before it missed (4,096 entries). Tontaube keeps the semantic stream and three acoustic ones, 625 bit/s.

Most codec language models either interleave the codebooks into one stream or give the residuals to one small shared module. Tontaube gives each codebook its own model:

| stage | predicts | backbone | blocks | width | stored parameters |
|---|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| CB0 | semantic codes, and therefore duration | Qwen3-1.7B | 28 | 2,048 | 1,854,352,386 |
| CB1 | first acoustic refinement | Qwen3-0.6B, truncated | 16 | 1,024 | 448,960,512 |
| CB2 | second | Qwen3-0.6B, truncated | 8 | 1,024 | 327,307,264 |
| CB3 | third | Qwen3-0.6B, truncated | 4 | 1,024 | 268,577,792 |

I summed every tensor in the four full-precision safetensors headers and got 2,899,197,954, the server README's figure, so "2.9B" holds. CB0 reads the text and writes semantic codes until it emits an end token. That length, L, is the utterance's duration, fixed once. CB1, CB2 and CB3 then each write exactly L codes for their row, conditioned on the text and every completed row below them, at temperature zero by default. No stage ever revises a lower one. The bet is that prosody is settled in the semantic stream, so that is where 64% of the parameters go.

Three details make this work for long text.

- **One character, one token.** Pronunciation is learned over a few hundred well-observed character IDs instead of tens of thousands of sparse subwords. The report's price: digits are characters too, so it asks for "1984" already spelled out, by a separate Qwen3-1.7B verbalizer that needs a GPU and is off by default.
- **Positions by time, not by order.** In the flattened input, frame 10 of the semantic row and frame 10 of an acoustic row can sit hundreds of tokens apart. Tontaube gives them the same RoPE coordinate, so their relative offset is zero, and text shares that timeline. Paired `<|text_split|>` and `<|audio_split|>` markers re-align text and audio at each chunk boundary.
- **Bounded context.** Text is cut into chunks of at most 350 characters. CB0 sees the previous chunk, the current one and up to 50 characters of the next. The acoustic stages carry no state across chunks, so voice drift cannot build up through them.

DualCodec's decoder is not causal, so Tontaube decodes overlapping windows, re-encodes them with VibeVoice's acoustic tokenizer and decodes the stable interiors with VibeVoice's causal decoder. A non-causal codec streams without retraining, at the cost of running a second codec.

### What runs on a CPU, and why it is about real time

The int4 bundle quantizes the transformer layers with GPTQ at 4 bits in groups of 64, keeps the last layer's MLP down-projection and the audio heads at 8 bits, and the CPU path runs hand-written AVX2 kernels (`app/native/lm_kernels.cpp`) that keep weights packed and quantize activations to int16. Decoding one token means reading every weight of the model once, so the speed limit is memory bandwidth, and the int4 headers say how much has to move. I summed the packed layers and heads: 876.5 MB for CB0 (798.6 MB of layers, 77.9 MB of head), 164.6 MB for CB1, 93.9 MB for CB2 and 58.5 MB for CB3. That comes to 1,193.6 MB of weights per 80 ms frame if every stage decodes its frame on its own.

For real time that is 12.5 frames a second, so about 14.9 GB/s of weight traffic before DualCodec, VibeVoice or the attention over the KV cache. CB0 alone is 11 GB/s, and within one stream it cannot be batched, since each semantic code depends on the last. Dual-channel DDR4-3200 peaks at 51.2 GB/s on paper. The README's line, "Speed is bound by memory bandwidth: a 12-core desktop CPU with dual-channel DDR4 synthesizes at about real time," is consistent with that arithmetic.

Bandwidth is the floor, though, not the whole bill. On beast2's i7-6850K, six Broadwell-E cores from 2016, Tontaube ran at 0.45x real time across five texts (a real-time factor of 2.22), and 0.43x on a 75-second passage. That platform has four memory channels, so bandwidth should not be the wall; on six older cores the int4 arithmetic is. Its peak resident memory was 8,072 MB.

### A FastSpeech 2 front, a source-filter back

The front half is FastSpeech 2 at a small width. Phonemes and their stress marks (from dizione, the author's own phonemizer) go into a 4-block transformer encoder. A duration predictor gives each phoneme a length in frames of 256 samples (10.7 ms at 24 kHz). The encoder states are repeated by those durations, and two frame-level transformers follow: one of 2 blocks that predicts pitch, voicing and energy, and one of 5 blocks that predicts the parameters of the waveform generator. Everything is 192 channels, 4 heads and rotary positions.

The back half is what makes 4.5M parameters possible. There is no neural vocoder. `SourceFilter` in `model.py` synthesises speech the way a 1970s vocoder did, with learned controls:

- A band-limited pulse train at the predicted pitch, computed in closed form as the sum of all harmonics below 0.47 of the sample rate, $\frac{\sin((K + \tfrac12)\theta)}{2\sin(\theta/2)} - \tfrac12$, with the top harmonic faded in and out so the signal stays continuous while pitch glides.
- White noise for the unvoiced parts: breath, `s`, `f`, the release of a `t`.
- Each is shaped by a filter that changes over time. Once per frame, a linear head predicts a cepstrum: 80 causal coefficients and 16 anti-causal ones for the harmonic branch (a minimum-phase vocal tract plus a maximum-phase part for the glottal pulse), 32 for the noise branch. The cepstrum becomes a complex frequency response through $H = \exp(\mathrm{FFT}(c))$ and multiplies the source's short-time spectrum.

The two linear heads that do all this have 25,862 parameters together. The rest of the network decides what the spectral envelope should be every 10.7 ms; turning that into samples is fixed signal processing. It is also why the card admits the voice "can sound slightly synthetic": a pulse-plus-noise source cannot express creaky voice or irregular glottal pulses.

### Where the voices came from

The training data is synthetic, and the pipeline is published in full: the [dataset](https://huggingface.co/datasets/n4ze3m/tiny-voca), its generation scripts and every rejected clip. Step through it:

<SyntheticCorpus />

The two voices began as sentences (F2: "an adult American woman in her mid twenties with a bright, clear, slightly higher-pitched voice"), rendered by Qwen3-TTS 1.7B VoiceDesign into 12-second reference clips. Qwen3-TTS 1.7B Base then read 8,984 sentences in both voices, 17,968 readings by my sum. Whisper small.en screened every clip; failures over 15% word error rate were regenerated, and 597 that failed twice were dropped, leaving 17,371 clips and 19.1 hours. That is the post's "19 hours". The model card's 17.6 hours is the training split after forced alignment with `facebook/wav2vec2-lv-60-espeak-cv-ft`, which accepted 17,326 of the 17,371 clips.

Now tick "where recorded speech entered" in the widget. The claim is precise and true about tiny-voca's weights: no recording of any person is in its training data, and it has no pretrained components. But every stage that decides what tiny-voca learns ran through a model trained on recorded human speech. The teacher made every waveform, Whisper decided which waveforms were correct, and the aligner decided how long each phoneme lasts. tiny-voca never heard a person. It learned from a model that heard many. The card says so: "the model cannot be more natural than the model that generated it." What the route buys is concrete: voices that belong to nobody, Apache-2.0 weights, a CC BY 4.0 dataset, and a recipe anyone with a voice-design model and ten dollars can repeat.

The rest checks out against the card:

- 4.5M parameters: 4,537,291 at inference.
- 18 MB: `tiny-voca.pt` is 18.2 MB of float32. The ONNX export is 21.4 MB, and the GGUF builds go down to 8.7 MB at `q4_0`.
- Runs on a CPU, about 70x real time on four threads: I measured 66.5x through the ONNX graph on four threads and 104.6x through PyTorch, 44.2x on one thread. The same ONNX file runs in the browser, which the [demo](https://tiny-voca.n4ze3m.com) does.

The evaluation is thin, and the card says so: Whisper small.en WER on 8 validation clips, 8 test clips and 16 new sentences, called "a sanity check, not a benchmark." There is no listening test. It is English only, two voices, no cloning, no streaming and no sampling.

## Verba-TTS-Indic-Zero: 1.8M parameters and Opus's vocoder

Surya TK [posted Verba-TTS-Indic-Zero](https://x.com/isuryatk/status/2123347241993986246) on 19 November: "1.8M parameters (7.6 MB)", "12 languages (11 Indic + English)", "24 voices", no GPU, about 100 ms to first audio in a Cloudflare Durable Object, and "even on an ESP32-S3 (+PRAM) chip" (PSRAM, presumably). There is no repository, model card or licence. There is a [demo](https://verba-tts.suryatk.in/), which the post's [page](https://suryatk.in/verba-tts) embeds, and the demo's `manifest.json` names the model `PiVo-Multi-1.8M`, dated 7 November, at 16 kHz, with four ONNX files in `model/` that the server serves to anyone who asks.

I downloaded the four graphs and counted their initializers:

| graph | parameters | bytes | what it does |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| `encoder.onnx` | 733,889 | 3,055,034 | phoneme tokens, voice id and language id in; encodings and log durations out |
| `decoder.onnx` | 298,812 | 1,241,193 | autoregressive, two transformer layers with a KV cache; 20 acoustic features per frame out |
| `conditioning.onnx` | 70,272 | 282,407 | features and pitch period in; 320 conditioning values per frame out |
| `vocoder_step.onnx` | 746,325 | 3,001,580 | 40 samples of waveform per call, carrying GRU state between calls |
| total | 1,849,298 | 7,580,214 | |

The manifest's own figures are 1,849,258 parameters and 7,580,214 bytes. The 40 I count beyond the manifest are the decoder's `feature_mean` and `feature_std`, two 20-value normalisation tables that are buffers, not weights. So the post's numbers are the files' numbers. Everything is float32, about 4.1 bytes a parameter. There is no int8 trick in the 7.6 MB. A 3.3 MB CMUdict lexicon ships beside the graphs for English and is not counted in it.

### A tiny acoustic model in front of FARGAN

The front half is a small FastSpeech-style model. The encoder is 128 wide: a 160-entry token table, a 24-entry voice table and a 12-entry language table, then six blocks alternating attention (four query heads, two key-value heads, 32-dimensional heads) with 5-tap convolutions, and a convolutional duration predictor. The decoder takes the encodings already repeated by those durations (its input is named `expanded`) and runs two more 128-wide layers autoregressively, with a key-value cache passed in and out, predicting 20 features per frame.

The back half I could name. `conditioning.onnx` has a pitch embedding of shape `[224, 12]`, a `[32, 64]` projection, a 3-tap convolution from 64 to 128 channels and a `[128, 320]` output layer. `vocoder_step.onnx` has GRUs of 160, 128 and 128 units, a gain head of `[4, 192]` and a skip layer from 688 inputs. Those are the layer shapes of `FARGANCond` and `FARGANSub` in [Opus's `fargan.py`](https://github.com/xiph/opus/blob/main/dnn/torch/fargan/fargan.py), every one of them: 224 pitch periods into 12 dimensions, 4 subframes of 80 conditioning values, 40-sample subframes. FARGAN is Jean-Marc Valin's framewise autoregressive vocoder ([arXiv 2405.21069](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.21069)), built for Opus to synthesise 16 kHz speech in 40-sample subframes using long-term pitch prediction, at a complexity its abstract puts at 600 MFLOPS. Here the vocoder and its conditioning network hold 816,597 parameters, 44% of the model.

That is the design choice worth taking from this. tiny-voca made its waveform with a 26k-parameter source-filter head; Kitten spends two thirds of its 82M on an ISTFTNet decoder. Verba spends its 816k on a vocoder that a codec team already squeezed for phones. Each call of `vocoder_step.onnx` returns 40 samples, 2.5 ms at 16 kHz, with the GRU state handed back to the caller, which is how a CPU worker can stream audio as it goes.

### Checking the post

- 1.8M parameters and 7.6 MB: true, from the graphs.
- 12 languages and 24 voices: the manifest lists English, Kannada, Hindi, Telugu, Tamil, Bengali, Marathi, Gujarati, Punjabi, Malayalam, Odia and Assamese, one female and one male voice each.
- 100 ms to first audio, "2,000× cheaper than ElevenLabs" and the ESP32-S3 run: stated, with nothing published to check them against. The cost comparison gives no prices.
- Quality: the demo page itself says "A research model, so voices can sound robotic." There is no evaluation of any kind.

At 16 kHz it is a telephone-band voice, and with no licence it is a demo, not something to build on. The architecture is the useful part: if I wanted a multilingual voice on a microcontroller, FARGAN plus a 1M-parameter front end is the shape I would start from.

Whisper small.en got every word of all five Kitten clips. tiny-voca's long passage came back with one inserted word, and every short-passage clip from tiny-voca and Tontaube came back with "beams" for "beam", which I put down to Whisper or a soft final consonant. ## Which one I would use

If I needed many languages on a CPU with no server, I would take **Kitten TTS 2 Mini**. It is Kokoro's architecture in one 83 MB graph, 3.3x real time on six old cores, with an Apache licence. I would still want to know its training data before shipping it, and the card does not say.

If I needed one clean English voice inside a browser tab or on a phone, I would take **tiny-voca**, or more likely its recipe. A 26k-parameter synthesizer at a hundred times real time, and a \$10 recipe for a voice that belongs to nobody. Its ceiling is the teacher and the source-filter model.

**Tontaube** is the only one of the three that clones a voice and is built for whole chapters, and it is the only one that isn't small. It is a 2.9B-parameter model with three more models behind it, about 8 GB on disk, under half real time on the 6-core CPU I tried and about 5x on a 3090. The licence is free below EUR 200,000 of revenue and never free for a TTS service. For an audiobook rendered overnight on my own machine, that is fine. For anything I would sell, I would read the licence twice.

For eleven Indian languages on almost nothing, **Verba-TTS-Indic-Zero** shows the shape, at 1.8M parameters, but it has no weights, licence or evaluation yet.

Related reading: [Paradee](/articles/paradee-tiny-tts) for distilling the same Kokoro architecture Kitten uses down to 8M parameters, [Pocket TTS with drifting](/articles/pocket-tts-drifting) for a 100M on-device model with a one-step sampler, [Breeze TTS 2](/articles/breeze-tts-2) for another Qwen3-backbone codec model, and [Nar TTS](/articles/nar-tts).
