In October I wrote this article from other people's pipelines. A dam flown once with a 360 drone, a two-kilometre shrine walk, a phone streaming GPS into a camera. Every capture number in it was the poster's, and the rules it ended on were theirs too: solve poses on the equirectangular panorama, train on cube faces, drop to SH2 when VRAM runs out, give it scale on day one.

Those rules sit in the middle of the pipeline I care about most: Insta360 dual-fisheye walks turned into metric splats, meshes and point clouds, then into BIM. So this update tests them. I took one public 360 capture that comes with a laser scan of the same room, ran structure from motion on it five ways, trained the splat at every SH degree, and measured the room off the splat with one dimension as a tape measure and the LiDAR as the answer key. The practitioners' material is still here, shorter, after the measurements.

## The room

[FIORD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.01732) (Tampere University, CC BY 4.0 on [Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/communities/fiord)) is a fisheye dataset built for this kind of question: ten scenes shot with an Insta360 One RS 1-inch on a tripod, two 200° lenses back to back, and a Faro Focus LiDAR scan of each scene as reference geometry. The authors posed the fisheye images in COLMAP with the `OPENCV_FISHEYE` model and registered the LiDAR to that model by hand, picking 7 to 10 corresponding points, with an alignment error of up to 25 cm on their largest indoor scene. That alignment is theirs, not ground truth, so I never measure against it: below, "the authors' poses" means their COLMAP solve, and the LiDAR is used only for lengths inside itself.

## Three ways to store one sphere

A 360 camera records a sphere of rays from one point (nearly: the two lenses sit a few centimetres apart). There are three common ways to lay that sphere onto pixels, and each samples it differently.

- **Dual fisheye**, what the sensor actually records: two circles, one per lens. FIORD's lenses are close to equidistant, so image radius grows linearly with the angle from the lens axis, $r \approx f\theta$, and sampling is nearly even out to the rim: radially $f$ pixels per radian everywhere, tangentially $f\theta/\sin\theta$, which rises slowly to $\pi/2 \cdot f$ at 90°.
- **Equirectangular (ERP)**: longitude across, latitude down, a $2{:}1$ image. North-south it is $H/180$ pixels per degree everywhere; east-west it is $W/360$ divided by $\cos(\text{latitude})$, so a 4096-pixel-wide ERP gives 11.4 px per degree at the horizon and twice that at 60° up. The poles are a single point smeared across a whole row. It is one image per instant, and that is its appeal for SfM: the mapper sees one camera.
- **Cube faces**: six 90° pinhole images. A pinhole of focal length $f$ samples $f/\cos^2\theta$ radially at angle $\theta$ off its axis, so a 1024-pixel face (f = 512) gives 8.9 px per degree at its centre, 17.9 at the middle of an edge and 26.8 in a corner. Trainers want these, because their rasterisers assume pinhole cameras.

Pick a direction below and a ring of rays ten degrees across it is drawn in all three layouts at the sizes my run used. Push the pitch towards a pole to watch the ERP ring smear, and turn the yaw to 90° to put the ring on the stitch seam, where the fisheye ring splits across both lenses.

<RayGeometry />

The seam is where an ERP lies. FIORD ships no panorama, and the paper says why: stitching causes ghosting. To get an ERP I stitched one from each fisheye pair myself, with the lens intrinsics and the lens-to-lens rotation from the authors' COLMAP model (two `OPENCV_FISHEYE` lenses with focal lengths near 999 px on 3,264-pixel frames, 179.8° apart), a 4° feather and no parallax correction. Two lenses that sit apart see a near object from two places, so along the seam the halves disagree by roughly the lens separation over the distance, in radians. I masked a band 6° wide around the seam for feature extraction in every arm that starts from my ERP, the way the Kotohibi guide masks its seam.

One thing went wrong in a way worth repeating. FIORD's fisheye frames are stored upside down (the dataset page says so about the raw files, and the split frames follow them), and my stitcher took the first lens's frame as the panorama's. So every ERP came out with the floor at the top, and the mask I wrote for the tripod (everything more than 60° below the horizon) blanked the ceiling instead. The tripod stayed in. For structure from motion that is mostly harmless, since the tripod moves with the camera and its features fail geometric verification; I note it below where it matters.

## Five ways to pose the same 56 positions

The job (one script on an RTX 3090, pycolmap 4.2.1 with CUDA) took 56 of the 106 tripod positions, evenly spaced through the capture, and posed them five ways with COLMAP's incremental mapper. Every arm got the same positions, exhaustive matching and the same SIFT budget per position (16,384 features, split across however many images a position became), and the same nadir mask.

1. **ERP, spherical model.** My stitched 4096 × 2048 panoramas with COLMAP's `EQUIRECTANGULAR` camera, added to COLMAP in June and shipped in 4.2.1. One image per position.
2. **Cube faces with the rig.** Four sides and the top face cut from each ERP (the bottom face is the tripod), 1024 px, with COLMAP's rig constraint: the five faces of one position share one pose and fixed relative rotations. This is what `pycolmap.panorama` does.
3. **Cube faces without the rig.** The same 280 images, each posed on its own: what you get from feeding cube faces to a mapper as a folder of photos.
4. **Dual fisheye without the rig.** The original circles at half size, `OPENCV_FISHEYE` started from the authors' calibration, one camera per lens, each image posed on its own.
5. **Dual fisheye with the rig.** The same, with the lens-to-lens pose as a rig constraint.

Then all five again on a thinned capture, every fourth position (14 positions), because a dense tripod capture forgives a lot and a sparse walk does not.

On the dense capture everything registers, which is what a tripod every few feet buys you. The thinned capture is where the arms separate, and it separates exactly along the line the practitioners drew. Cube faces with no rig fall apart: 42 of 70 images in the largest of three models, and not one position with all five faces in the same model. The mapper has to discover from image content alone that five pinhole views share a centre, and with 14 positions it cannot. Give it the rig, or give it the panorama as one camera, and every position registers. That is Spirula's note ("it is one camera, so the connection between what the six directions see is not something the mapper has to discover"), reproduced on a dataset with no forest in it.

The dual fisheye without a rig is the surprise. It held: 27 of 28 images, 13 of 14 positions whole. A 200° lens is not a cube face. Each circle sees more than a hemisphere, so the two lenses of one position share a band of overlap around the seam, and neighbouring positions share most of a hemisphere. DuckbillStudio's `.osv` failure came from a 40-minute walk through cedar foliage; on a 14-position tripod capture of a room the fisheye route is fine. What I would not conclude is that fisheye is safe on a long handheld walk. I did not test that, and the rig arm (the fix) was the slowest thing in the job: rig-verified matching on 28 fisheye images took 687 s, against 3 s without the rig, and the full 112-image run hit its 25-minute timeout.

### Do the poses agree?

Registration says an image got a pose, not that the pose is right. Two checks.

**Against the authors' solve.** For every arm I fitted a similarity transform from its camera centres to the centres of the authors' COLMAP model (all 203 fisheye images, their own run) and took the residual. In the authors' units, all four dense arms agree with it to between 0.024 and 0.029, about 0.2% of the span of the camera path. ERP and cubes-with-rig are not measurably better or worse than the fisheye; on a tripod capture this good, the projection does not decide the poses.

**Against the rig itself.** The arms that posed each image independently let me measure how well a mapper rediscovers a rig it was not told about.

- Cube faces without the rig: the five face centres of one position, which should coincide, spread by a median of 0.030 units (0.058 at the 90th percentile), and their relative rotations miss the cube's exact 90° steps by a median 0.60° (1.01° at P90). Half a degree is a lot of error to put into a splat.
- Fisheye without the rig: the relative rotation between the two lenses lands within a median 0.20° of the authors' calibrated rig (0.30° at P90). The lens-to-lens distance is less stable: a median 0.068 units, with outliers that push its standard deviation to 0.64 units, so a handful of positions put one lens far from the other while every image still counted as registered. The authors' own model has the same problem in miniature: its 97 posed pairs give a baseline of 0.038 units with a spread of 0.021.

Structure from motion has no scale, so all of that is in units of nothing. To put it in centimetres I fitted a horizontal slice through the walls of the ERP arm's sparse cloud to the same slice of the LiDAR, scale free, by trimmed ICP over a grid of scales. The fit is coarse (the sparse cloud's median distance to the scan's walls is 13 cm, because it is sparse and some of it lies beyond the glass), but it says one ERP-arm unit is 0.605 m and puts the camera 1.65 m above the floor, which is a tripod. On that scale:

- every dense arm agrees with the authors' poses to between 1.4 and 1.7 cm RMS;
- a rigless cube position's five face centres spread by a median 1.8 cm (3.5 cm at P90);
- the authors' calibrated lens-to-lens distance is 2.2 cm, and the rigless fisheye arm's median is 4.2 cm.

Two centimetres between the two lenses is also why my zero-baseline stitch is tolerable here: for a wall 1.5 m away, 2.2 cm is under a degree of seam parallax, about ten pixels in a 4096-wide panorama. Halve the distance to a desk edge and it doubles.

The room itself, measured in the scan: a horizontal slice from 0.67 m to 1.56 m above the floor gives walls 5.37 m and 6.73 m apart, each wall a band of points with an RMS thickness of 4 to 16 mm. That is roughly 36 m², not the 15 m² the paper's table lists for this scene. I report what the scan contains.

## Why SH2: the splat budget

The dam's "VRAM limitation" answer is checkable arithmetic. A splat's view-dependent colour is a spherical-harmonic expansion with $(d+1)^2$ coefficients per channel: 1, 4, 9 and 16 for degrees 0 to 3. A standard 3DGS PLY stores position, normals, the DC colour, the rest of the SH, opacity, scale and a quaternion, all float32: 68, 104, 164 and 248 bytes a splat. At 15M splats an SH2 PLY is about 2.46 GB and an SH3 one about 3.72 GB. Training holds each trainable float four times over (value, gradient, two Adam moments): 9.12 GB for 15M splats at SH2 and 14.16 GB at SH3, before any image buffer. On a 24 GB card with thousands of 1,920 px images resident, dropping a degree is the obvious lever.

## Training the splat, at four SH degrees

From the ERP arm's poses I trained gsplat 1.5.3 with its MCMC strategy: 12,000 iterations, a cap of 245,000 splats, the same seed every time, at spherical-harmonic degree 0, 1, 2 and 3. A fifth run trains SH0 with 890,000 splats, about as many bytes in a PLY as 245,000 at SH3. The training images are not my stitch. Each lens's fisheye frame is cut into four 768-pixel, 90° pinhole crops with that lens's own centre, so the stitcher's seam never reaches the loss. Every eighth position is held out entirely: 49 positions train (392 crops) and 7 test (56 crops). The upside-down frames cost something here too. The crop meant to look 45° up looked 45° down, so a quarter of the views include the tripod's legs, in training and in test alike.

Held-out PSNR goes from 24.73 dB at SH0 to 24.79, 24.97 and 25.08 dB at SH3, and SSIM from 0.809 to 0.815. The PLY goes 16.7, 25.5, 40.2 and 60.8 MB: 68, 104, 164 and 248 bytes a splat, exactly the arithmetic above. So three extra degrees of colour buy 0.35 dB for 3.6 times the file, and the spread across the 56 held-out views is about 2 dB, several times the whole gain. Spending the same bytes on more splats instead does not help either: SH0 with 890,000 splats is a 60.5 MB PLY and scores 24.62 dB, slightly below SH0 at 245,000, though its SSIM (0.816) is the best of the five. Each run took 131 to 162 s and at most 2.4 GiB of VRAM on the 3090, so the dam's "VRAM limitation" is a 15-million-splat problem, not a degree problem in a room this size.

This is one room, lit evenly, with few shiny surfaces, which is where view-dependent colour matters least. The blinds and the glass are where SH3 should earn its bytes, and 0.35 dB over 56 views says it mostly does not here. For a room headed for BIM, SH0 or SH1 is the size to keep.

## A tape measure on the splat

The last test is the one that matters for BIM: is the splat's geometry good enough to measure off? I took the SH0 splat's centres (those with opacity above 0.3, within the room), cut the same horizontal slice I cut through the scan, and looked for the walls.

Where the splat sees a solid wall, it puts the wall in a band of centres with an RMS thickness of 2.6 to 3.0 cm, at the 0.605 m-per-unit scale from the pose fit. The scan's walls are 0.4 to 1.6 cm thick. So splat centres place a painted wall within about three centimetres, 1.6 to 7.5 times looser than the scanner: good enough to find a wall, not to dimension one. That clean pair of walls comes out 9.23 units apart, 5.58 m at the fitted scale, against the scan's 5.37 m. That 4% is the scale fit's error showing, since the fit itself has a 13 cm median residual; it says nothing about the splat.

The tape-measure test itself failed on this room, and the failure is instructive. The plan was to take one wall-to-wall distance from the scan as the known length, scale the splat by it, and check the other. One pair of walls shows up cleanly in the splat. The other pair is glazed partitions with blinds, and behind glass the splat puts centres wherever the reflections and the corridor beyond fit the photos: along that axis the slice has a dozen peaks spread over 11.8 units, about seven metres, with no pair that stands out as the walls. A splat is a model of what the camera saw, and glass shows it the wrong room. For scan-to-BIM that rules out measuring glazed walls off a splat at all; their position has to come from the frames around them, or from a scanner.

## What the practitioners do, and what holds up

The rest of this piece is the October material: five practitioners' pipelines, read from their posts, threads and the source of the tools they name. I kept what still holds and marked where my run agrees or doesn't.

### A dam from one drone flight

[@kotohibi_3d's Miho dam](https://x.com/kotohibi_3d/status/2079907663895482456) is the most fully documented capture in the set. The post lists the steps and the replies add the training cost: a DJI Avata 360, one flight of 14:34 of 8K D-Log M video, 875 masked frames, spherical SfM in Metashape Standard with the masks, cubemap conversion and "parallax-based reduction", then LichtFeld Studio at 15M splats, SH2, strategy MRNF, PPISP on. Training took "around 14 hours with RTX 4090 24GB" for 481,300 iterations. Asked why SH2 and not SH3, the author wrote "VRAM limitation".

The author also maintains a [CC BY 4.0 pipeline guide](https://github.com/Kotohibi/3DGS_pipeline_guide), still the best written description of a 360-to-splat workflow I know of. Frames come from a tool that keeps the sharpest frame per chunk. Masks come from YOLO classes or a SAM 3 text prompt, plus a seam mask for the stitch line between the two lenses, which only works with in-camera horizon levelling off, because levelling moves the seam. In Metashape the camera type is *Spherical*, masks apply to key points, and tie points are cleaned in three passes (reprojection error, reconstruction uncertainty, projection accuracy, about 5% each), re-optimising after each, twice round.


The guide's converter drops whole source frames whose neighbours already cover them. Its example keeps 237 of 251 source frames, taking 1,506 cube faces to 1,422, under a 3.0 maximum camera distance, 4.0° maximum foreground parallax and at least 100 shared visible points.

I can back out roughly how many images trained the dam. LichtFeld Studio's GUI scales the step count by image count: `autoScaleSteps` sets the scaler to `image_count / 300` above 300 images (`src/visualizer/core/parameter_manager.cpp`), on a base of 30,000 iterations. The guide's screenshot shows 346,800 iterations with a steps scaler of 11.56, exactly 30,000 × 11.56. So 481,300 iterations means about 4,813 training images, against 5,250 for six faces of 875 frames: the reduction removed about 8%, if the default auto-scale was left on. That is about 9.5 iterations a second on the 4090.

The follow-up post fixes scale with printed AprilTags on the ground, detected in a downward cube face, each marked `USED` with a corner reprojection error of about 0.2 px on the image. With the tag's printed size known and its corners triangulated from several views, the ratio of the two lengths is the scale. It is the cheapest metric reference there is: a sheet of paper.

MRNF and PPISP are LichtFeld Studio's. Neither acronym is expanded anywhere in the repository; the UI calls MRNF the "Default 3DGS strategy with MRNF refinement". In `src/training/strategies/mrnf.cpp`, each refine window soft-prunes splats with near-zero opacity, a degenerate rotation, a collapsed scale or an absurd extent, and parks their rows in a free list; then a fraction of candidates (`grow_fraction` 0.07) is picked by Gumbel top-k sampling weighted by an SSIM error map and an edge score and split into children that reuse the freed rows. Opacity decay and noise on the means sit on top, ideas recognisable from MCMC-style 3DGS. The default `max_cap` is 5,000,000 splats; the dam raised it to 15M. PPISP is NVIDIA's [Physically-Plausible Image Signal Processing](https://github.com/nv-tlabs/ppisp), vendored under Apache-2.0: a learned per-camera ISP (exposure in EV stops, vignetting, a colour correction, a response curve) that keeps exposure drift over a 14-minute flight out of the splats' colours.

### A shrine approach on foot, and the fisheye file that would not connect

[DuckbillStudio walked the approach to Togakushi Shrine's Okusha](https://x.com/DuckbillStudio/status/2106231790025531608) with a DJI Osmo 360: 2 km in 40 minutes, built in Spirula Studio from 360 video converted in DJI Studio. The thread adds that 10 clips were merged into 3 ERP videos with a LUT applied, just under 400 extra stills were shot around the gate, and that with the dual-fisheye `.osv` files "parts that would not connect always appeared, whatever extraction interval or alignment method I used, but with ERP every frame connected" (my translation). Training was about 5,000 images at 8K equirectangular, all at once, on an RTX 4090.


Spirula's own notes say the same about a GoPro MAX unwrapped into ten views per frame: without a rig constraint a 78 s handheld walk reconstructed as 645 of 930 images over 3 components (`docs/notes/sfm-rig-constraints.md`), and an ERP aligns more easily because "it is one camera, so the connection between what the six directions see is not something the mapper has to discover." That is the claim my SfM arms above test, on a tripod instead of a forest. The SfM result in the thread is one unbroken strand two kilometres long, 2,777,150 points on the counter in the screenshot.

### Putting the splat on a map

[DuckbillStudio's third post](https://x.com/DuckbillStudio/status/2106704257835839830) closes the loop from camera to map: an Android app sends the phone's GPS and shutter over Bluetooth Low Energy to one or more Osmo 360s, each camera writes the position into its video, Spirula Studio runs SfM with a weak position constraint, the splat is cleaned in SuperSplat and converted to geo-referenced 3D Tiles. The camera-to-scaled-splat half took a day to build.

"Weak" matters. Spirula's notes put consumer GPS at a DOP around 2, which "with a typical 3-5 m range error means 6-10 m horizontal" (`docs/notes/imu-gps-for-sfm.md`), correlated over minutes. So GPS enters bundle adjustment as a per-frame position factor with a Huber loss, so that "one wrong prior -- a synchronisation glitch, a wild GPS fix -- bends nothing" (`docs/notes/sensor-priors.md`). Vision decides local geometry; GPS decides scale, drift and placement, and the note reports 0.2 percent scale error over a 100 m walk. Spirula's survey of the `.OSV` format also found the Osmo 360 files carry a 1 kHz attitude quaternion and no GPS of their own, and the Insta360 X5's GPS log "is the phone's position pushed over Bluetooth". A 360 camera's GPS is usually a phone's.

### Rendering without sorting

<RepoCard repo="MrNeRF/LichtFeld-Studio" />

[Donovan Hutchence (@slimbuck7), who works on PlayCanvas and SuperSplat](https://x.com/slimbuck7/status/2107023202832527738), posted "7.5 ms a frame on a Pixel 7 Pro in Chrome/WebGPU, where sorting takes 19.5". At 60 Hz the budget is 16.7 ms: the sort alone overruns it. The trick is one line of probability. Sorted blending gives splat $i$ the weight $\alpha_i \prod_{j \text{ in front}} (1-\alpha_j)$. Draw every splat opaque with depth testing, but let each fragment survive with probability $\alpha_i$; a fragment is the visible one when it survives and everything in front does not, which happens with exactly that weight. The depth buffer orders for free, one frame is a noisy sample, and temporal anti-aliasing averages it out. PlayCanvas ships this as `GSplatParams#stochastic` on its WebGPU renderer ("Splats are drawn without sorting, using dithered coverage, opaque blending and depth writes", `src/scene/gsplat-unified/gsplat-params.js` at `9f0f464`). The opaque fragments also give the splats a depth buffer, which is what shadows and lighting need.

### The LiDAR route, and what its "5 mm" means

XGRIDS sells the other route: a handheld scanner whose LiDAR is metric from the start, with cameras for colour and a splat. On 12 November it [posted](https://x.com/XGRIDS_OFFICIAL/status/2120919328950198780) a Lixel L3 walk through a factory's MEP piping: "One scan of dense MEP piping, three outputs for factory digital twins: a true-color point cloud (5 mm relative accuracy), photorealistic 3DGS, and a textured mesh."

XGRIDS publishes how it got the number, in an [accuracy white paper](https://cdn-bukbb1.xgrids.cloud/ep/dc/officialWebsite/privacyPolicy/lixell3-accuracy-validation-white-paper-en.pdf). Thirteen circular targets, 4 cm across, on the walls of an office floor of about 507 m², measured by a Leica TS60 total station to 1.5 mm. Twenty scans by five operators with seven units, no ground control. Every pair of target centres in a scan gives a distance, compared with the total station's: 1,524 comparisons from 5.40 m to 44.22 m. The 68th percentile of the absolute deviations is 4.2 mm, reported as within "5 mm @ 1σ (typical)"; P85 is 6.4 mm and P97 is 9.9 mm.

So "relative" means the distance between two well-defined points in one scan, over corridor lengths: how much SLAM drift bends the cloud. It says nothing about where the cloud sits in the world, and nothing directly about the noise on a single surface, which is what my wall-flatness number below is about. The end card carries the condition in small type.

  src="/articles/3dgs-capture-pipelines/fig14.jpg"

  caption="The footnote at the bottom of the end card: 'Tested in a designated 500 m² environment; accuracy reported at 1σ.' The factory in the video is not the test site (@XGRIDS_OFFICIAL on X, frame from the post's video)."

For scan-to-BIM on MEP the work is fitting cylinders, and a diameter error is a local error on a curved, often shiny surface. Before trusting 5 mm on a pipe I would ask for a cylinder-fit test on known diameters, by pipe size and material (bare stainless steel returns LiDAR badly at grazing angles), with the fraction of each circumference the walk actually saw, and the tail percentile, since a prefabricated spool cares about P97, not P68. The white paper also lists "automatic alignment on high-reflectivity targets" in its setup. A rigid alignment cannot change a distance between two points, so if that is all it is, the result stands; if the targets also constrained the SLAM solution, the check points were not independent. The paper does not say which. 4.2 mm at P68 with a published method is a good number for drift. It is not a diameter accuracy.

## Where this sits in the scan-to-BIM chain

This piece is the capture end of a chain the site keeps returning to. [KOLC+ loads splats into BIM](/articles/kolc-3dgs-bim) and has to compute sections and earthworks on Gaussians that are not a surface; [3D and BIM tools for agents](/articles/agent-3d-bim-tools) maps which editors take a scan at all; [Mutiny BIM](/articles/mutiny-bim) is where I modelled a scan-sized room and exported IFC 4.3; [debim](/articles/debim) and the [floor-plan-to-3D roundup](/articles/floor-plan-to-3d-roundup) are the drawing side. On the reconstruction side, [GOFish](/articles/gofish-fisheye-gaussian-splatting) trains splats on the same FIORD lenses without cutting them into pinholes, the [fifth 3D reconstruction roundup](/articles/3d-reconstruction-roundup-5) is where learned pose models stand against bundle adjustment, and [Depth Pro 2](/articles/depth-pro-2-single-image-splats) gets metres from a single photo's focal length. [Accord](/articles/accord-metric-mapping) gets them live from a camera's own IMU, through a visual-inertial filter. The splat format itself is in [SOG](/articles/sog-splat-format), and Spirula Studio's internals in [its own article](/articles/spirula-studio).

## What I would do with a 360 camera now

Solve on the ERP or give the mapper the rig; never hand it loose cube faces. That rule held exactly where the practitioners said it would, on a sparse capture, and on a dense tripod capture it made no difference. Mask the seam and the operator, and check which way up your frames are before you trust a mask. Expect the poses to be good to a centimetre or two in a room, and a painted wall in the splat to be a band about three centimetres thick: fine for looking, the floor for anything you measure. Keep SH0 or SH1 for a room; SH3 bought 0.35 dB here for 3.6 times the file. And measure nothing behind glass. The fisheye-without-a-rig result surprised me, and I would not generalise it from 14 tripod positions to a forest walk. Scale stays the open problem: SfM gives none, and the cheapest honest source is still a tag or a tape measure, not a fitted guess like the one I used above.
