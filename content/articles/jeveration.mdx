Two months ago I wrote about [jev-paint](/articles/jev-as-substrate), which asked Jev one
question per pixel and painted the answers. A 32×32 heart came back as 1,024 forward
passes that never saw each other, with a mean top-1 probability of 0.627. Thresholded, the
heart was there. Averaged, it was a grey rectangle. My conclusion then was that the
marginals carry a coarse picture, and that structure, the part where one pixel has to agree
with another, is "the thing a decision model provably cannot represent across independent
questions."

[Jeveration](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.24738), from Yuhe Liu, Qi Li, Shihua Zhang and
Xinchao Wang at the National University of Singapore, is the same move made properly. It
asks a decision model for 128 Choices at once, each over an 8,192-entry codebook, and gets
back one coherent 512 px image in one forward pass. The paper's claim is in the title:
System One models now generate. AK posted it on X with the project page's headline numbers.

## What "decision" means here

Jeveration is not built on Jev, and nothing from TypeSafe is involved. It is
[Qwen3.5-4B](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.5-4B) kept frozen, with rank-256 LoRA
adapters on every linear layer, some new input embeddings and two kinds of output head. The
"decision" vocabulary comes from how the outputs are typed:

- **An image is a joint Choice.** The image tokenizer is a frozen TA-TiTok with 128 codes
  per image from an 8,192-entry codebook. Jeveration appends 128 *decision tokens* to the
  prompt, runs the backbone once, and reads a distribution over the 8,192 entries at each
  of the 128 positions. All 128 are sampled in parallel.
- **Segmentation is a Choice per region.** For dense tasks the decision tokens become 432
  spatial queries (an 18 × 24 grid over a 576 × 768 picture), each choosing one of 13 NYUv2
  classes for its 32-pixel cell.
- **Depth and surface normals are Scores**: a real number, or three, per position.

## Why 128 independent choices make a monster

The obvious way to make a decision model generate is what jev-paint did and what
WindTunnel's `answerFromSpans` did with its eight evidence slots: ask every position its
own question and take each answer. The paper writes that model down as

$$
q_{\mathrm{ind}}(c \mid y) = \prod_{i=1}^{N} q_i(c_i \mid y)
$$

and then proves the line that matters. For a target distribution $p(c \mid y)$ over whole
images, the best any such factorised model can do is

$$
\min_{q_{\mathrm{ind}}} \mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q_{\mathrm{ind}}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} H(c_i \mid y) - H(c \mid y).
$$

The right side is the total correlation of the codes: the entropy of the positions one at a
time, minus that of the whole image. With one right answer, as in dense prediction, it is
zero and independence is free. "A snow leopard on a rock" has thousands of right pictures,
each position's marginal blends all of them, and independent sampling gives you the left
ear of one leopard and the right ear of another.

The widget is the smallest version I could build. One prompt admits four pictures (day or
night, island left or right), each an 8 × 8 grid of six codes.

<SharedState />

The 64 cells hold 57.0 bits one at a time; the picture holds 2.0. The total correlation is
55.0 bits, and an independent draw is a valid picture about one time in $10^{17}$. A shared
state the model fully follows makes every draw valid; at 90% use the rate is 7.0%, at 80%
it is 0.4%. Coordination has to be nearly total before it pays.

The paper's Figure 2 shows the same failure at full size. The "no joint decision" images
are the first-phase masked model sampled in one pass, every position from its own
distribution.

## One random state for every position

The fix is to give every position the same source of randomness. Jeveration draws one
random state $\xi$ per image and conditions all positions on it:

$$
q_\theta(c \mid y) = \mathbb{E}_{\xi \sim p_0}\Big[\prod_{i=1}^{N} q_{\theta,i}(c_i \mid y, \xi)\Big].
$$

Given $(y, \xi)$ the codes are still sampled independently. Averaged over $\xi$ they are
not, because every position read the same $\xi$. That is the design: the randomness that
decides "this leopard, facing that way" is meant to move out of the 128 per-position draws
and into one shared input, which the network can use to agree with itself before anything
is sampled. On the released checkpoint it moves only part of the way, as the run further
down shows.

In the code, $\xi$ is a 128 × 64 tensor shaped like the codes it will be replaced by:

```python
# jeveration/random_state.py @ e0cc08b
masked = torch.rand(batch, n, generator=generator, device=dev) < rho
codes = torch.randint(0, codebook_std.shape[0], (batch, n), generator=generator, device=dev)
state = torch.where(masked.unsqueeze(-1), mask_emb.view(1, 1, -1).float().expand(batch, n, -1), codebook_std[codes])
state = math.sqrt(1 - sigma ** 2) * state + sigma * torch.randn(state.shape, generator=generator, device=dev)
```

Each position is a learned mask embedding with probability $\rho = 0.6$, otherwise a random
codebook entry, plus Gaussian noise at $\sigma = 0.1$ (the code also scales the state by
$\sqrt{1-\sigma^2}$, which the paper does not mention). The state enters the decision-token
embeddings and, again, the image head.

The backbone is mostly recurrent. Qwen3.5-4B's config marks 24 of its 32 layers as linear
attention (Gated DeltaNet) and 8 as full attention. Decision tokens "attend to each other
bidirectionally" only in those 8, through a block mask in `backbone.py`; in the other 24 a
decision token sees only the tokens before it. Much of the mutual agreement is left to the
image head, whose eight blocks use plain bidirectional attention over the 128 positions.

TA-TiTok's codes are also not patches. TiTok is a one-dimensional tokenizer: each code is a
latent the decoder attends to while building every patch. Two disagreeing codes do not make
two halves of an image. They make a double exposure, which is what Figure 2 shows.

## Why the model ignores the state, and how it is made to use it

Train this network the obvious way (draw $\xi$, predict the target image's codes) and it
learns to ignore $\xi$, because $\xi$ is drawn independently of the target and says nothing
about it. The loss is then minimised by each position's marginal: the independent model
again. So training has two phases, with the VLM frozen throughout.

**Phase 1 learns the image distribution.** The architecture is trained as a masked
generative model, MaskGIT-style ([arXiv 2202.04200](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.04200)):
hide a random subset $M$ of a real image's codes and predict them from the visible ones and
the prompt,

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mask}}(\phi) = \mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{|M|}\sum_{i \in M} -\log p_{\phi,i}(c_i \mid c_{\bar M}, y)\Big].
$$

Because the visible codes are given, this model learns how codes depend on each other. Run
over many rounds, a model like this samples good images. Run in one round from all-masked,
it is the independent model, and the paper reports it at **0.32 GenEval** that way.

**Phase 2 teaches one pass to produce a whole image.** The decision model $\theta$ starts
as a copy of $\phi$. From $(y, \xi)$ it samples a full code sequence $\hat c$ in one pass.
Then a random subset of $\hat c$ is re-masked and two masked models predict the hidden
codes from the same visible ones: the frozen $\phi$, which knows what real images look
like, and an auxiliary $\psi$, which starts as a copy of $\phi$ and keeps training on
$\theta$'s own samples, so it knows what $\theta$'s images look like. The update is

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\Big[\frac{1}{|M|}\sum_{i \in M} \mathrm{sg}\big(p_{\psi,i} - p_{\phi,i}\big)^{\top} \ell_{\theta,i}\Big].
$$

Read it as: at every hidden position, push $\theta$'s logits away from what "an image like
mine" predicts and toward what "a real image" predicts. The two predictions are conditioned
on the rest of $\hat c$, so they agree only when $\theta$'s codes fit together. The paper's
argument is that routing the choice through $\xi$ is how $\theta$ achieves that. This is Di\[M\]O's distribution
matching for masked models, applied with the frozen VLM as the backbone.

**A correction term finishes it.** For some samples, the half of the positions with the
lowest confidence $q_{\theta,i}(\hat c_i)$ are re-masked, the frozen $\phi$ fills them in
over four rounds at CFG 3, and $\theta$ is trained with cross-entropy to produce that
corrected sequence from the same $(y, \xi)$, weighted $\lambda = 1$ against the matching
loss.

$\phi$, $\psi$ and the correction exist only in training, which took about four days on
four RTX PRO 6000 GPUs. At inference the path is `sample_random_state`, one
`backbone_forward`, the image head, one `torch.multinomial` over all 128 positions, and
the decoder.

## What the ablations measure

Table 4 holds three separate experiments, each comparable only within itself:

- **Random state against a fixed one**, on a text-to-image-only model: 0.73 GenEval and
  28.27 HPSv2.1 with a fresh $\xi$ per image, 0.70 and 26.63 with one fixed $\xi$.
- **Correction target**, on an earlier all-task run: 0.74 and 28.03 with it, 0.73 and
  27.92 without.
- **Head branches**, on the first-phase masked model: 0.64, 0.60 and 0.59.

The number I find most informative is the fixed-state row. With $\xi$ frozen, every image
for a prompt comes from the same state, and the only randomness left is the 128
independent per-position draws, yet GenEval stays at 0.70. Distribution matching is what
moved the model from 0.32 to 0.73; the randomness of $\xi$ adds 0.03 GenEval and 1.64
HPSv2.1 on top. The correction term adds 0.11 HPSv2.1, from one run each with no spread
reported. My first reading was that the per-position distributions must be close to
one-hot for the fixed-state row to work. The 3090 run says they are not.

Table 5: training on all tasks instead of text-to-image alone moves GenEval from 0.73 to
0.74 and HPSv2.1 from 28.28 to 28.06. The extra tasks cost generation almost nothing.

## On an RTX 3090

I ran the released checkpoint on an RTX 3090 (repository at `e0cc08b`, its pinned PyTorch
2.7.1 and Transformers 5.12.1, 16 prompts of my own). It peaks at 14.23 GB of VRAM. A
counter on `backbone_forward` fires once per text-to-image request, and once for a dense
request covering all three tasks. The same seed gave identical codes twice.

**Latency.** With the paper's protocol, the medians were 145.2, 154.2 and 144.6 ms on three
prompts, about 1.9x the H200's 77.8 ms. Split by stage: backbone 102.7 ms, image head
7.1 ms, decoder 29.9 ms. A batch of eight took 57.7 ms an image. The three dense tasks in
one request took 652.3 ms, and segmentation alone 250.1 ms.

**The per-position distributions are wide.** Over 16 prompts and 8 random states each,
16,384 positions in all: The mean top-1
probability is 0.149 (median 0.104). Only 3.0% of positions put more than half their mass
on one code, and none put more than 90%. The mean entropy is 6.46 bits out of a possible
13; averaging $2^H$ per position instead gives about 189 effective choices. Holding the state fixed and drawing twice
changes 93.5% of the codes; changing the state changes 97.3%. The shared state does not pick the image and leave the final draw a formality; at the
level of code ids, the final draw carries most of the randomness. So I scored images with
CLIP ViT-L/14-336 against each prompt, 64 per condition:

| Codes from | CLIP score (mean ± sd) |
|---|---|
| the model's joint draw | 29.81 ± 1.88 |
| argmax at every position | 29.31 ± 2.19 |
| each position's marginal over 8 states | 29.49 ± 1.96 |

The marginal row is the Eq. 1 counterfactual on the final model: each position sampled from
its distribution averaged over eight states. It scores 0.32 below the joint draw, about one
standard error of the difference with 64 images a side. Two readings fit, and my run cannot
separate them: many codes may decode to nearly the same thing, or CLIP cannot see the
double exposures of Figure 2. No images left the box, so I have not looked. The narrower
claim stands: the released model's coherence is not carried by confident per-position
decisions.

**The decoder's prompt read matters a little.** The same codes decoded with the right
prompt, an empty one and a different one scored 29.81, 28.72 and 27.98, and right versus
empty differ by 6.67 grey levels per pixel on average.

## What this does to the earlier System One pieces

This site has about thirty pieces on decision models, most from September, when the only
working examples were Jev, its reproductions and the harnesses around them. Seven of their
claims bear on Jeveration.

**"It cannot write a string that is not already an option. Forced. True of every family, no
exceptions."** ([What a decision model cannot do](/articles/what-decision-models-cannot-do))
Holds for strings: Jeveration writes images. But the piece defined a decision model's output
space as "a list you sent in the request", and that no longer covers the category. Here the
list is a codebook the caller never sees, which is the shape a text version would need too.
Nobody has shown one, and I no longer think it impossible in principle.

**"One call, eight bits, and nothing else comes back."** (same piece) Ruled out as a
general ceiling. A 255-option Choice carries at most $\log_2 255 \approx 7.99$ bits. One
Jeveration call returns 128 choices over 8,192 entries, $128 \times 13$ bits, which is 1,664
bits in one call. The eight bits were Jev's interface, not a law of parallel readout.

**"Structure — the thing a decision model provably cannot represent across independent
questions."** ([Jev as a substrate](/articles/jev-as-substrate)) The proof part holds, and
the paper's Eq. 2 is the formal version of it: independent questions miss by the total
correlation, whatever the model. What I got wrong was treating "independent" as part of
being a decision model. jev-paint's questions never saw each other, by that piece's own
account, and I generalised from it. Jeveration lets its 128 questions read each other (in
eight backbone layers and the whole image head) and conditions them on one shared random
input, and the paper's evidence (0.32 GenEval for independent one-pass sampling, 0.73 after
decision training, and Figure 2) says structure comes back inside one pass. The limit
belongs to independent questions, and decision models do not have to ask them. My 3090 run
neither confirms nor refutes this: its CLIP comparison is too blunt to see a double
exposure.

**Gate 3: "can every option be judged without reading another?"**
([Where to use Jev](/articles/where-to-use-jev)) Unchanged. Gate 3 is about options inside
one question, the 0 of 100 on [relational choice](/articles/jev-scores-zero). Jeveration
couples *questions*, not options: within a position the 8,192 entries are still scored by a
linear layer that knows nothing of the others.

**"The bounded model decides, something else composes."** (What a decision model cannot
do) Survives in a new form. A 305M-parameter decoder turns the 128 codes into pixels and reads
the prompt again through CLIP while doing it: the WindTunnel and json-render split, with
the composer learned instead of written in JavaScript. On the 3090 its prompt read was
worth about one CLIP point (29.81 against 28.72 with no prompt).

**"A decision model cannot emit a string, so somebody has to."**
([Five Jev harnesses](/articles/system-one-harnesses)) Holds. Jeveration emits codebook ids,
and nothing in it writes text. The nearest thing that piece covered is djev, which seeds a
DiffusionGemma canvas, pins the template and reads every label slot in one denoise step.
Jeveration is the same canvas idea with nothing pinned, trained so that one step is enough.

**"System One model" is "a serving discipline plus a training objective."**
([Any model can be Jev](/articles/any-model-can-be-jev)) Holds, and Jeveration is the
clearest case for the second half. The readout is ordinary: logits at 128 positions, one
pass. Everything that makes it work is the phase-2 objective, and no serving flag on an
untrained Qwen3.5-4B would produce it. Its objective is coherent joint samples, though,
where the piece meant calibration.

Two September themes do not transfer. The category was sold on **calibrated probabilities
you can branch on**; Jeveration samples from its distributions and the paper measures no
calibration. And the speed
pattern repeats: [Where to use Jev](/articles/where-to-use-jev) found that "the two headline
multipliers measure 4.57x and 336x when somebody runs both arms on one task". Here the
headline survives as arithmetic and shrinks with the choice of row.

## Where I land

Jeveration is a good paper that is mostly named after the wrong ancestor. Its mechanism is
masked generative modelling (MaskGIT) distilled into one step by distribution matching
(Di\[M\]O), with a frozen VLM as the backbone and the outputs called Choices and Scores.
What is new is that a frozen 4B VLM with adapters gets to 0.74 GenEval in one pass, and that
one checkpoint does generation, editing and NYUv2-grade segmentation together.

The "System One" frame does earn one thing. It forces the question this site had been
answering wrong: why parallel decisions cannot agree. The answer is not the architecture.
It is the training objective, and the fix is a shared random input plus a teacher that
knows the joint distribution. That is reusable well beyond pictures. WindTunnel's eight
evidence slots, decided in parallel and deduplicated afterwards, are a small instance of
the same factorisation problem.

The released model is also less decisive than the story suggests: its per-position
answers average 6.46 bits of entropy, and sampling each position from its marginal cost
little on my CLIP comparison. If you want fast images, compare it with SANA-Sprint before switching. If you build on
decision models, read Section 3.1 and the ablation table, and stop telling people that a
decision model cannot produce structure.
