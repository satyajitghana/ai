On 2 November 2026 four MIT CSAIL researchers (Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang and Omar Khattab) published
[Sparks of Weight-Space Programming](https://rikiyat.github.io/blog/weight-space-programming/). The claim is that a
frontier model, GPT-6 Astra, used as it is, can read and write neural network weights: name the game a policy was
trained on from its raw weights, repair a stuck car controller, write game-playing networks from zero, and, in the
experiment that went viral, make Qwen3.5-0.8B better at math by editing its weights directly. **Reported:** MATH-500
went from 7.9% to 48.1%, GSM8K from 13.8% to 67.1%, IFEval from 21.0% to 62.3%, by changing "only 0.016% of
weights", with no gradients and no training.

Within a day the replies split. One reply called it a loop fix dressed up: Qwen3.5-0.8B runs in non-thinking mode by
default, and in thinking mode it loops, so of course a fix for looping helps. Another said Gaussian noise on the last
four layers doubles the score too. Hugging Face's Ahsen Khaliq rebuilt the checkpoint from the published logs and
re-ran MATH-500 at 51.0%.

Everything needed to check the core claim is public: both checkpoints, the edit as a diff, and a log of every
message, every Python run and every forward pass of the agent's 20-hour session. So I checked it. This page labels
each claim **measured** (I ran it), **reported** (a source says so) or **reasoned** (my inference).

## What the experiment was

**Reported, from the post and `exp8_math/README.md` in the logs.** Astra got a persistent Python kernel over
Qwen3.5-0.8B's weights (CPU only, no GPU, no internet), practice problems with answers (7,469 from GSM8K train, 7,275
from MATH train), and a budget of 10, 25 or 50 forward passes. A pass is either a generation (the model answers up to
512 practice problems with the evaluation's sampling, original and edited side by side if asked) or a teacher-forced
read of up to 1,024 texts that returns log-probs, hidden states and MLP activations. Gradient descent, autograd and
any iterative fitting were forbidden. So were edits to the embedding, the output head and the final norm, and any
edit that just multiplies a whole tensor by a constant. Astra never saw the benchmarks.

The evaluation is fixed: thinking on, temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, top-k 20, presence penalty 1.5, at most 32,768 new
tokens, last `\boxed{}` graded with math-verify. Those are the thinking-mode settings Qwen's own card uses for its
benchmarks.

The three runs ended with three different edits:

| Budget | Turns | Hours | Final edit (from each run's `final_answer.md`) | MATH-500 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| none | | | the base model | 7.9% |
| 10 passes | 74 | 11.6 | subtract 1.5x a loop direction from 30 output matrices in layers 8-22 | 12.3% |
| 25 passes | 86 | 8.5 | zero attention head 7 of layer 15 | 14.1% |
| 50 passes | 158 | 20.3 | subtract a loop direction from 64 MLP output columns in layers 15 and 19 | 48.1% |

Only the 50-pass edit is published as a checkpoint, so that is the one I took apart.

## The model and the edit, term by term

Before the measurements, the object. **Measured, from the base model's `config.json`:** Qwen3.5-0.8B's language model
is 24 decoder layers over a 1,024-wide residual stream. Three of every four layers use Gated DeltaNet, a linear
attention with 16 heads of width 128; layers 3, 7, 11, 15, 19 and 23 use ordinary softmax attention with 8 query heads
and 2 key-value heads of width 256. Every layer has the same gated MLP:

$$
\mathrm{MLP}(x) = W_{\text{down}}\,\big(\mathrm{silu}(W_{\text{gate}}\,x) \odot W_{\text{up}}\,x\big),
\qquad W_{\text{gate}}, W_{\text{up}} \in \mathbb{R}^{3584 \times 1024},\;
W_{\text{down}} \in \mathbb{R}^{1024 \times 3584}
$$

The vocabulary is 248,320 tokens, and the output head reuses the embedding table. Both layers the edit touches, 15
and 19, are softmax-attention layers; the 25-pass run's edit, a zeroed attention head, is in layer 15 too.

Read $W_{\text{down}}$ by columns. The MLP's hidden vector $a = \mathrm{silu}(W_{\text{gate}}x) \odot W_{\text{up}}x$
has 3,584 entries, one per neuron, and the output is $\sum_j a_j\, w_j$ where $w_j$ is column $j$. So column $j$ is
the fixed vector neuron $j$ adds to the residual stream, scaled by how strongly it fires. Editing a column changes
what a neuron says, never when it fires.

Now the edit, with every symbol:

$$
W' = W - \alpha\, u\,\big(u^\top W\big)\,M
$$

- $u \in \mathbb{R}^{1024}$, unit length: the loop direction. Astra got it by teacher-forcing matched pairs of texts,
  the same problem continuing productively and continuing into repetition, and taking the difference of what layers
  15 and 19 write on the two branches.
- $u^\top W \in \mathbb{R}^{1 \times 3584}$: for each neuron, how far its write $w_j$ points along $u$. Call it $c_j$.
- $M$: a 3,584 x 3,584 diagonal 0/1 mask with 64 ones. It keeps the update to the chosen neurons.
- $u\,(u^\top W)\,M$: a rank-one matrix whose column $j$ is $c_j u$ for a chosen neuron and zero otherwise.
- $\alpha$: the dose. Column $j$ becomes $w_j - \alpha c_j u$, so its component along $u$ goes from $c_j$ to
  $(1 - \alpha)\,c_j$. At $\alpha = 1$ the neuron stops writing along $u$; at $\alpha = 6$ it writes $-5 c_j$.

Everything orthogonal to $u$ in those 64 columns is untouched, and so is every other column. **Reasoned:** that is why
this is cheap to search over. Once $u$ and the 64 indices are fixed, there is one number left to choose, and the
whole forward pass up to layer 15 does not depend on it.

What the rules allowed shaped it as much as anything. **Reported, from the task prompt:** no gradients, no
optimisers, no iterative fitting, no edits to the embedding, the output head or the final norm, nothing that just
multiplies a tensor by a constant. Closed-form linear algebra on activations Astra had read was allowed. A
projection like this one is about the most expressive thing those rules leave: it uses a direction estimated from
data and puts it into weights without a training loop.

## The edit, measured from the two checkpoints

**Measured.** I streamed both safetensors files over HTTP range requests (base `Qwen/Qwen3.5-0.8B` at commit
`2fc0636`, edited at `d1671a1`) and compared all 488 tensors byte for byte. Exactly two differ:
`model.language_model.layers.15.mlp.down_proj.weight` and `…layers.19.mlp.down_proj.weight`, both `[1024, 3584]` in
bfloat16. Every other tensor, vision tower included, is identical.

Inside each, 64 of the 3,584 columns changed. A column of `down_proj` is what one MLP neuron writes into the
1,024-wide residual stream. The bits differ in 65,260 values in layer 15 and 64,928 in layer 19, 130,188 in all. That is
a little under the 131,072 values the edit touched, because some updated values round back to the same bfloat16
number.

The change in each layer is one outer product. I took an SVD of the difference restricted to the 64 columns:
99.9995% of its energy is in the first singular value in layer 15 (99.9991% in layer 19), and the next singular values
(0.001) are the size of bfloat16 rounding. So the edit is

$$
W' = W - \alpha\, u\,(u^\top W)\,M, \qquad \alpha = 6
$$

where $u$ is a unit "loop direction" in the residual stream and $M$ keeps only the 64 chosen columns. That matches the
formula in Astra's final report, `W = W0 - 6*outer(u, mask64*(u@W0))`. I measured what it does to each changed column:
its component along $u$ goes from $c$ to $-5c$ (the median ratio is -5.00 in both layers). Removing the component
would be $\alpha = 1$. This edit overshoots it, so those 64 neurons now write *against* the loop direction, five times
as hard as they used to write along it. The relative Frobenius change is 10.64% of layer 15's matrix and 8.11% of
layer 19's. The two directions are not the same vector: their cosine is 0.85.

<EditAnatomy />

The "0.016%" is honest arithmetic, with a choice of denominator. **Measured:** the release has 873,438,784
parameters, the language model 752,393,024 of them, and 254,279,680 of those are the tied embedding table. 131,072
touched weights is 0.0150% of the release, 0.0174% of the language model and 0.0263% of the language model without
its embedding. The post's 0.016% is 131,072 over a round 0.8 billion. Any of these is small. The more useful way to
say it is that the edit is described by two 1,024-number directions and 128 column indices; the strength is one
constant.

One more measured detail. The 64 columns are not simply the 64 with the largest $|u^\top W|$: only 31 of them in layer
15, and 18 in layer 19, are in that top 64. **Reported, from `work_notes/experiment_history.md`:** they were chosen
from activations on a pass of the original model, as the neurons that carry most of the difference between what is
written on looping and on productive text ("~91%/98% of transfer repetitive-minus-productive writing contrast, but
50%/40% of full directional weight norm"). **Measured:** the second half of that checks out. The chosen columns hold
25.2% of $\lVert u^\top W \rVert^2$ in layer 15 and 16.0% in layer 19; the square roots are 50% and 40%.

How big is the change per weight? **Measured, from the diff:** the median changed entry moves by 0.0020 in layer 15
and 0.0017 in layer 19, against a median weight of 0.0052 and 0.0058 in those matrices, and the largest single change
is 0.146 and 0.215. Per column it is large: the median chosen column changes by 57% of its own norm in layer 15 and
43% in layer 19. So this is not a small nudge to many weights. It is a large change to a few neurons' outputs, along
one axis.

One pattern I did not expect. **Measured:** 40 of layer 15's 64 neurons, and 35 of layer 19's, have an index below
512. If the choice ignored the index, about 9 would. The low-index neurons do not have larger write vectors (their
mean column norm is 0.236 against 0.277 for the rest in layer 15); their mean $|c_j|$ is only about 25% higher. I come
back to this below, where I recompute the selection from activations.

## How Astra found it

**Reported, from the p50c run's log.** The log reads like an ordinary interpretability workflow, run by a model with a
tight forward-pass budget.

1. **Diagnose.** Pass 1 ran the original model on 96 practice problems: 11 right, 85 cut at the token limit, no
   finished wrong answers. Astra wrote that the dominant failure was "pathological long/repetitive reasoning", not math.
2. **Find a direction.** Teacher-forced reads of matched contexts, the same problem continuing productively or into
   repetition, gave a common difference vector in the residual stream. A first try ablated a single neuron in layer 19
   (+6.25 points on 32 problems, within noise).
3. **Turn the dose up.** Removing the direction from both layers' full `down_proj` at α = 1 took 128 fresh problems from
   8.59% to 32.81%. α = 2 on a 256-answer set: 29 right to 92. Restricting it to 64 neurons per layer at α = 4: 13 of
   128 to 47 of 128. At α = 6 with the full 32,768 tokens: 7 of 64 to 35 of 64, with the share cut at the limit falling
   from 89% to 8%.
4. **Control.** A norm-matched random direction, orthogonal to the loop direction, at the same dose: +1.56 points
   with a standard error of 3.52, on 128 answers. Not the effect.
5. **Confirm on held-out problems.** Frozen before this step, the edit was run against the original on 1,152 practice
   problems not used to choose it: 10.6% to 60.4%, 579 rescues and 5 losses. Of the 579 rescues, 576 were answers the
   original had not finished. Accuracy on answers the original did finish did not change.

Astra's own reading of that last line is the honest one: the edit lets the model finish answers it could already work
out. The post says the same thing in its Gemma follow-up: on Gemma 3 270M, which reaches its token limit on only 8.5%
of answers, the same procedure found ordinary reasoning errors and no component to switch off, and MATH-500 stayed
between 6.8% and 7.7%.

The task prompt matters for judging how much was discovered. **Reported, from `turns/t001/prompt.txt`:** it names the
target as an example, "if the model often fails because it repeats itself and never finishes, finding and fixing what
drives that repetition counts", and tells Astra to "go after the largest category you can explain". It also rules out
the cheap fix: "making the model skip its thinking or close it early regardless of the problem" is not wanted. So
Astra did not have to find out *that* the model loops. It had to find *where*, in weights, and do it within 50 passes.

### The dose ledger

**Reported, from `work_notes/experiment_history.md`.** Pulled out of the narrative, the generation passes that tested
a candidate read like a dose-response table. Each is paired: original and edited model on the same problems in the
same pass.

| Pass | Edit | Problems x samples, cap | Original → edited | Gain (SE) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | ablate one neuron (layer 19, #11) | 32 x 1, 32,768 | 3 → 5 | +6.25 (4.35) |
| 5 | remove $u$, layer 19 only, α = 1 | 32 x 1, 32,768 | 4 → 6 | +6.25 (4.35) |
| 10 | remove $u$, layers 15 + 19, all columns, α = 1 | 128 x 1, 32,768 | 11 → 42 | +24.22 (3.96) |
| 12 | same, α = 2 | 64 x 4, 32,768 | 29 → 92 | +24.61 (3.96) |
| 19 | same, α = 2, fresh problems | 128 x 2, 32,768 | 21 → 110 | +34.77 (3.53) |
| 20 | same, α = 3 | 128 x 2, 32,768 | 21 → 123 | +39.84 (3.82) |
| 23 | norm-matched random direction | 64 x 2, 32,768 | 14 → 16 | +1.56 (3.52) |
| 27 | 64 chosen columns, α = 4 | 64 x 2, 16,384 | 13 → 47 | +26.56 (4.45) |
| 28 | all columns, α = 4 | 64 x 2, 16,384 | 14 → 48 | +26.56 (4.59) |
| 30 | 64 chosen columns, α = 6 | 64 x 1, 32,768 | 7 → 35 | +43.75 (6.63) |
| 34 | same, frozen, fresh problems | 64 x 1, 32,768 | 2 → 34 | +50.00 (6.30) |

Two things stand out. The gain grows with the dose all the way from α = 1 to α = 6, with no sign of a peak; Astra
stopped raising it because it had to freeze an edit and confirm it, not because a higher dose hurt. And pass 28 is
the ablation for the 64-neuron mask: at α = 4, editing all 3,584 columns and editing the 64 chosen ones gave the same
gain on the same problems. The mask halves the size of the change (relative Frobenius change 7.09% against 14.11% in
layer 15, from the same notes); it was not needed for the effect. **Reasoned:** that makes "0.016% of weights" partly
a presentation choice. The full-column version of the same edit changes 7.3 million weights across the two
matrices and, at that dose, does the same thing.

### Three budgets, three edits

The two smaller budgets ended somewhere else. **Reported, from each run's `edit/manifest.json`:** the 10-pass run
shipped 30 rank-one edits, one per attention or MLP output matrix in layers 8-22, at α = 1.5, which changed the bits
of 72,986,982 weights. The 25-pass run zeroed the 256 output columns of attention head 7 in layer 15, 262,144
weights. Its own notes call the head a retriever of "repeated continuations": weakening it reduced loops, amplifying
it made them worse.

The logs keep, for every run, the grade of all 2,000 MATH-500 answers and the full text of 200 of them (the first
sample of the first 20 problems in each of ten evaluation shards). That is enough to compare the three edits by
difficulty and by loop rate, which the post does not do. **Measured,** with the loop rule from the samples section
below, re-implemented and applied to all 800 texts. (On the 20 published samples per model the re-implementation
marks 19 base and 7 edited answers as looping, against 19 and 9 for the first version, so if anything it
undercounts the edited model's loops.)

<RunLevels />

The base model's failure is the same at every level. Even on level 1, 16 of 21 first samples loop and never close.
It solves what it can solve before it loops: the 13 base answers that close their thinking are all right. The two
small-budget edits move that a little: 41 and 29 closed answers of 200, of which 33 and 25 are right. The 50-pass
edit changes the picture: 152 of 200 close, and of the 71 that still loop, 54 recover and close. Its closed answers
are now wrong 40% of the time (92 right of 152), which is what happens when a model starts finishing problems it
cannot do.

Two paired facts settle what kind of gain this is. **Measured, from the per-problem grades:** the base model solves
77 problems at least once in four samples. The 50-pass edit solves every one of those 77 at least once, and on them
its accuracy is 96.4% against the base's 51.3%. It solves 320 problems at least once in all. Nothing the base could
do was lost. The gain is largest in the middle: level 3 goes from 7.6% to 56.7%, level 5 only from 0.9% to 23.1%.

## What the edit does to the answers

**Measured, from the 20 published samples per model** in the edited repository's `eval/` folder (the first sample of
each of the first 20 problems in the re-run's smoke test, thinking on, 32,768-token cap). I counted closed `</think>`
tags, right `\boxed{}` answers and occurrences of "wait", and marked a loop onset where at least 90% of the next 3,000
characters' lines repeat a line already written.

<LoopStrip />

The base model closes its thinking in 1 of 20 answers and gets that one right. The other 19 loop: their tails are
lines like `Wait, $f(-1) = 5/3$.` repeated until the cap, and the median answer says "wait" 764.5 times. The edited
model closes in 18 of 20 and is right in 10. It still falls into a loop in 9 of 20, with a later onset, and all 9 of those
eventually close. Its two capped answers fail in a new way: each ends in a run of more than three million whitespace characters.
The logs flag the same thing ("whitespace degeneration").

Twenty answers per model are an illustration. The re-run behind them is the real check. **Reported, from the model
repository:** 500 problems x 4 samples, the post's protocol on vLLM, scored 51.0% with 31.6% of answers hitting the
token limit. Its 25-problem smoke test of the base model scored 5.0% with 95% at the limit.

### Scoring the loop under both models (CPU)

**Measured.** To see the mechanism rather than its outcome, I took base-model answers from those samples, split each at
its loop onset, and scored the text before and after the onset under both sets of weights, teacher-forced, on a CPU
(bfloat16, PyTorch 2.14, transformers 5.20.2, two threads). If the edit works the way the log says, the looping tail
should become much less likely and the productive head should barely change.

Five base answers, mean log-probability per token (nats) of the text before the onset and of about 3,000 characters
after it:

| Problem | Tokens before onset | Tokens after | Before onset: base → edited | Loop: base → edited |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `precalculus/807` | 7,068 | 1,367 | -0.346 → -0.362 (-0.016) | -0.030 → -0.076 (-0.045) |
| `intermediate_algebra/1994` | 9,046 | 1,461 | -0.277 → -0.294 (-0.017) | -0.018 → -0.061 (-0.043) |
| `algebra/2584` | 7,778 | 1,285 | -0.309 → -0.325 (-0.016) | -0.072 → -0.129 (-0.056) |
| `number_theory/572` | 8,232 | 1,083 | -0.169 → -0.201 (-0.032) | -0.031 → -0.121 (-0.090) |
| `algebra/1349` | 4,130 | 1,609 | -0.367 → -0.382 (-0.015) | -0.056 → -0.086 (-0.030) |

The loop tail is cheap for the base model: -0.03 nats per token means each next token of the loop has about a 97%
chance. The edit makes every loop token less likely in all five answers, by 0.053 nats on average, and costs the
productive text before the onset 0.019 nats per token (about 2% in perplexity). That is selective, but less than
Astra's own measurement on its chosen cases (-0.103 against -0.009 at the same dose, from `final_report.md`).
**Reasoned:** a per-token penalty of 0.05 looks small, but it compounds. Summed over 1,000 tokens of loop, the edited model gives that exact
continuation about $e^{-50}$ times the probability the base model gave it. Each step of the loop is a little more
likely to be left, sampling gets many steps to leave it, and presence penalty 1.5 pushes the same way. That is why a change too small to notice on productive text moves the share of answers cut at the limit from
93% to 33%.

## Turning the one knob

The published checkpoint is one point on a curve. Because the edit is $u$, 64 indices and $\alpha$, and I recovered all
three from the diff, I can rebuild it at any dose and with any part swapped out. **Measured.** I rebuilt the two
matrices from the base weights (at $\alpha = 6$ this reproduces the published tensors to within bfloat16 rounding:
the largest difference is 0.0005), and scored six base answers teacher-forced under each version: 43,214 tokens of
text before the loops and 7,327 inside them. The edit cannot change anything below layer 15, so I cached the
residual stream entering layer 15 once per answer and re-ran only layers 15-23 for each setting, about 30 seconds
each on two threads.

<DoseCurve />

What the sweep shows, in order of how much it changed my reading:

1. **The random-direction control is flat.** A random unit vector orthogonal to $u$, in the same 64 columns at
   $\alpha = 6$, changes the loop tail by -0.0001 nats per token and the text before it by -0.0003. Astra's logged
   random control (+1.56 points, SE 3.52) agrees. The effect is the direction, not the act of disturbing 64 neurons.
2. **Selectivity rises with the dose.** At $\alpha = 1$ the edit costs the productive text almost as much as the loop
   (-0.0023 against -0.0026). At 6 the loop pays 2.3 times as much, at 8 it pays 3.7 times, at 12 it pays 7.5 times.
   But the cost to normal text rises too: -0.021 nats per token at 6, -0.035 at 8, -0.084 at 12. The last is about
   9% on perplexity across all reasoning, which I would not ship without the accuracy run Astra had and I do not.
3. **The activation-chosen mask matters on real text.** Swapping the 64 neurons for the 64 with the largest
   $|u^\top W|$, same dose, halves the loop penalty (-0.024 against -0.050) and is less selective (1.5x against
   2.3x). Removing $u$ from every column at $\alpha = 1$ is not selective at all here (-0.0044 on the productive
   text, -0.0037 on the loop), although in Astra's generations that edit gained 24 points. **Reasoned:** a small,
   unselective change can still break loops in generation, because a loop is a fixed point that any perturbation
   makes a little less stable; the teacher-forced number measures selectivity, not that.
4. **The two layers are not additive.** Layer 15 alone does -0.027 on the loop, layer 19 alone -0.012, both together
   -0.050, more than the sum. Layer 19 reads a stream layer 15 has already changed. Astra's own single-layer probes at
   $\alpha = 4$ ranked them the same way (-0.081 against -0.034 on its short pairs).

There is also a gap between Astra's teacher-forced numbers and mine. On its hand-written pairs, about 200 tokens each,
the full-column edit at $\alpha = 1$ raised the productive branch's log-prob by 0.002 and lowered the repetitive one
by 0.024. On real 7,000-token answers I get no selectivity at that setting. **Reasoned:** short clean pairs make a
contrast look sharper than it is inside a long, half-repetitive answer.

### Why six

The probe that explains the dose is the residual stream itself. **Measured,** on the same six answers with the base
weights: projected onto $u$, the residual stream after layer 15 averages 0.93 on productive text and 2.10 inside a
loop. The 64 chosen neurons write 0.11 of that on productive text and 0.34 in a loop, which is 80% of what all 3,584
neurons together write along $u$ there (0.43). They are mostly silent along $u$ until the model loops.

Now apply the edit. Each chosen neuron's contribution goes from $c$ to $-5c$, a change of $-6 \times 0.343 = -2.06$
inside a loop, and the stream along $u$ falls from 2.10 to 0.04, measured. On productive text it falls from 0.93 to
0.30. After layer 19 the same thing happens: 3.82 to 0.29 in the loop, 1.86 to 0.64 outside it.

So $\alpha = 6$ is close to the dose that cancels the loop direction exactly when the model is looping:
$2.10 / 0.343 \approx 6.1$ would zero it on average, and 6 leaves 0.04. **Reasoned:** the "minus five times"
overshoot that looked strange in the diff is not the neurons pushing against loops. It is the amount needed to cancel
what the whole residual stream carries along $u$ at that point, using the 64 neurons whose activity rises when it
does. Astra did not compute it this way; it found 6 by raising the dose until the results said stop.

What does $u$ mean in tokens? **Measured:** pushed through the final norm and the tied unembedding, neither layer's
$u$ reads as anything about repetition. The tokens it promotes most are fragments (`...*`, `urada`, `ossid`) and
Chinese administrative titles; the ones it suppresses lean towards connectives (` first`, ` thus`, ` however`,
` rather`, ` accordingly`, ` using`). "Wait" is in neither top 25. That is the expected null result for a mid-stack
direction, which feeds later layers rather than the output, and it is a warning against naming the direction from
its logit-lens reading. What it does is clear from the probe above; what it represents is not.

### Recomputing the selection

Last, the low-index pattern from the diff. **Measured:** on four of the same answers I recomputed Astra's selection
rule myself: for every neuron, its mean write along $u$ inside the loop minus its mean write before it. The 64 chosen
neurons carry 97% of that net contrast in layer 15 and 102% in layer 19 (some unchosen neurons push the other way),
close to the 91% and 98% Astra logged on its own texts. My own top 64 by contrast share 38 neurons with Astra's set in
layer 15 and 27 in layer 19, from different texts, which is reasonable agreement for a choice made on one forward
pass.

My top 64 are low-index too: 38 below 512 in layer 15, 52 in layer 19. The reason is in the activations. Neurons
0-511 fire about twice as hard as the rest on these texts (mean $|a_j|$ 0.126 against 0.058 in layer 15, 0.152
against 0.060 in layer 19). So the skew is a property of Qwen3.5-0.8B's MLPs, not of the agent's tooling. Why the
first 512 neurons of a layer should be the busy ones I can only guess; the checkpoint's index order is whatever
training left it.

## The criticism: thinking mode, and noise

**Reported, from Qwen's model card:** "Qwen3.5-0.8B operates in non-thinking mode by default", and in thinking mode
"Qwen3.5-0.8B is more prone to entering thinking loops compared to other Qwen3.5 models". The card's own IFEval for
this model is 44.0 in thinking mode and 52.1 in non-thinking mode. The post's base IFEval is 21.0 with thinking on and
a 32,768-token cap; 74.9% of those answers were cut off (`eval/unedited/merged.json`) and an answer cut inside its
thinking scores zero. **Reasoned:** Qwen probably ran its card with a larger budget, so a cut-off loop hurt it less;
the card does not say.

The sharpest reply, from @dummerspast39, says that in default non-thinking mode the unedited model scores 47% on
MATH-500 against 48% for the edit. **Reported, unverified:** the post gives no sample count, prompt or sampling
settings, and I found no files behind it.

**Measured, small.** I could not run MATH-500 with thinking off at full size on a CPU, but I could look for loops. I
sampled 12 MATH-500 problems (seed 7) and ran both checkpoints twice on each with `enable_thinking=False` and Qwen's
non-thinking settings (temperature 0.7, top-p 0.8, top-k 20, presence penalty 1.5), capped at 1,536 new tokens:
48 answers, about 1.4 hours on three CPU threads. Neither model loops. Across 24 answers each, the base said "wait" 16
times and the edit 18; no answer from either repeats a line more than 8 times, against hundreds in the thinking-mode
samples above. The answers that hit the cap (12 of 24 for the base, 9 for the edit) were still working through the
problem, not repeating it. Right answers, by last `\boxed{}` and counting a vector written as a tuple as right: 10 of 24 for the base, 8 for the edit. That is too small a sample to separate
the two, and the short cap makes both scores low; it says only that the edit neither breaks non-thinking mode nor has
a loop there to fix, which is what @dummerspast39's numbers would predict.

The authors' own harness was ready to answer this. **Measured, by reading `exp8_math/code/evaluate.py`:** it
defines a `nothink35` protocol, Qwen's non-thinking setting with presence penalty 1.5 and an 8,192-token cap, under
the comment "but thinking looped at 0.8B; the evaluation uses the model card's NON-thinking benchmark setting
instead". The file's docstring still describes a non-thinking evaluation of a Qwen3-1.7B checkpoint, left over from
an earlier experiment. Every MATH-500 shard behind the published numbers, though, records `"protocol": "think35"`
and `"thinking": true` in its `scores.json`, for the base and all three edits. So the published numbers are what the
post says they are, thinking on; the non-thinking comparison was planned in code and never published.

The other reply, from @yavuzbakman, shows MATH-500 going from 8.8% to 16.2% after adding Gaussian noise to the last
four layers ("seed 11, 20%"), one sample per problem over all 500. **Reported.** One seed, named, out of an unknown
number tried; at one sample per problem the base's own run-to-run spread is not small. Set it beside Astra's logged
control: a norm-matched random direction at the edit's dose gained +1.56 points (SE 3.52) on paired practice
problems. **Reasoned:** both can be true. Noise that big perturbs the late layers enough to knock some answers out of a
loop; a single random direction in two layers does not. Neither is close to the targeted edit's 40-point gain under
the same protocol. The arXiv paper the reply points to, [Evolution Strategies at Scale](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.24372)
by Qiu and colleagues, is about fine-tuning with many such perturbations and a reward, not about one random draw.

So both sides are right about something. **Reasoned:**

- The edit is real, small and specific. It is the same two tensors in both public checkpoints, it is rank one per
  layer, it was found by contrasting activations and confirmed on held-out problems against a random control, and it

- The "up to 6x" is a fix for a failure that this model's default mode does not have. The relevant comparison is not

- Some of what is new is the operator, not the operation. Finding a direction from contrasting activations and

  with a 50-pass budget, and picked the layers, the neurons and the dose.

## The background paper: the other way to change weights without gradients

The noise reply cited [Evolution Strategies at Scale: LLM Fine-Tuning Beyond Reinforcement
Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.24372) (Qiu, Gan, Hayes, Liang, Xu, Dailey, Meyerson, Hodjat and Miikkulainen;
Cognizant AI Lab, MIT, UCLA and UT Austin). It is worth reading next to Astra's edit, because it also changes an
LLM's weights with no backpropagation, and it does it in the opposite way.

**Reported, from the paper's Algorithm 1 and 2.** Each iteration samples $N$ Gaussian noise vectors the size of the
whole model and scores each perturbed copy with a reward:

$$
R_n = R(\theta_{t-1} + \sigma \varepsilon_n), \quad \varepsilon_n \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I), \qquad
\theta_t = \theta_{t-1} + \alpha \cdot \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} Z_n\, \varepsilon_n
$$

- $\theta$ is every parameter of the model. Nothing is masked.
- $\sigma$ is the perturbation size; the paper uses $\sigma = 0.001$ per parameter.
- $R_n$ is the task reward of the $n$-th perturbed model, a full generation and grading.
- $Z_n$ is $R_n$ z-scored across the population, so the update is a reward-weighted average of the noise: directions
  that scored above average are added, those below are subtracted.
- $\alpha$ is the step size, $5 \times 10^{-4}$. The usual $1/\sigma$ factor is folded into it.

The engineering is in Algorithm 2: noise is never stored. Each perturbation is regenerated from its random seed, added
layer by layer in place, scored, and subtracted again, so a population of 30 costs one model's memory. $N = 30$ is
small next to the 10,000 or more of earlier ES work, and it is the paper's main surprise that this works on billions
of parameters. On the Countdown arithmetic task, **reported from its Table 1**, ES took Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct from
0.1% to 14.4% (best RL baseline 13.5%) and Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct from 10.0% to 60.5% (best RL 43.8%), with one set of
hyperparameters for every model.

So ES spreads its change over every parameter, and the change looks like noise even though it is not. Astra's edit
is the other extreme: 131,072 weights, one direction, and a change you can name. **Measured, to put them on one
scale:** spread evenly over all 3.67 million entries of layer 15's `down_proj`, the edit's Frobenius norm (1.75) would
be a per-entry change of $9.1 \times 10^{-4}$, about $0.9\sigma$ of a single ES perturbation; in layer 19 it is
$7.3 \times 10^{-4}$. The two methods move these matrices by about the same total amount. ES points it in a random
direction and keeps it if the reward goes up; Astra pointed all of it along one direction it had measured.

**Reasoned:** that also frames the noise reply. Adding one Gaussian draw to four layers is ES with a population of one
and no update rule. In a model whose main failure is a self-reinforcing loop, a random push can break enough loops to
double a score near 8%; ES selects among such pushes with a reward, and Astra skipped the search by reading where the
loop lives. None of the three, as run here, shows the base model learning math it did not already know.

## The rest of the week's weight-space programming

**Reported.** Three other things were filed under the same name, and they are different kinds of object.

**TensorSlay's fact writer** ([areu01or00/qwen0.8B-wsp](https://github.com/areu01or00/qwen0.8B-wsp)) writes a short
profile of its author into Qwen3.5-0.8B: five `down_proj` tensors (layers 17-21), each changed by at most 25% of its
norm. I read its README and `wsp.py`. The update is a closed-form, MEMIT-style least-squares solve,
`ΔW = R Kₚᵀ (λ·Kₙ Kₙᵀ + Kₚ Kₚᵀ + μI)⁻¹`, with protected keys from about 40 everyday questions. Its own results table
is candid: on five unseen phrasings, two return the bio word for word, one starts right and then loops, one gets a
clause, one misses. It is a known editing algorithm that an agent set up, not weights the agent reasoned out.

**Chandan Singh's toy programs** ([report](https://csinva.io/weight-space-programming-autoresearch/analyze/toy_examples/report.html))
gave Claude, as a coding agent, a blank transformer and the rule "set every weight by hand". Across 13 tasks it wrote
959 programs; the ten algorithmic ones (addition, multiplication, sorting, parity, GCD and more) reached 100%
accuracy within three attempts. The parity model has 174 weights, 35 of them nonzero. This is the forward direction
the post's title promises, at a scale where every weight can be checked by hand.

**Weight-space learning**, which Boris Knyazev pointed to, is the other route: train a model whose outputs are
weights, "Stable Diffusion for weights" in his phrase. Weight-space programming asks a general model to reason its way
to an edit; weight-space learning trains a generator for one.

The closest page on this site is [Program-as-Weights](/articles/program-as-weights), where a compiler model turns a
spec into a LoRA, trained for that job. The edit here is also what the neuron pages do by hand:
[neuron steering](/articles/neuron-steering) scales a single neuron's write, [neuron finding](/articles/neuron-finding)
searches for which one, and [neuron lens](/articles/neuron-lens) reads what a neuron's weights say. An agent that picks
64 writer neurons by their activation contrast and rescales their write along one direction is doing the same thing,
in two layers at once.
[Neuron tuning](/articles/neuron-tuning) is the trained version of the same idea: it picks neurons that fire in the
target context and are quiet elsewhere, and trains only their output columns. Astra's selection rule is that, done
with one activation read instead of a training run, and its update is that, restricted to one direction.

### Why the edit is a linear program

The starting point is the formula in "The model and the edit, term by term" above. Every changed column moves by a multiple of the same
$u$, so the edit is $\Delta W = u\,v^\top$ with one number $v_j$ per neuron, and at token $t$ it adds $(v \cdot h_t)\,u$ to the
residual stream, where $h_t$ is the MLP's 3,584-entry activation. That push is linear in $v$. Barry labels tokens in the base model's own
traces with a repeat detector: looping tokens $R$ deep inside a loop, productive tokens $P$ well before one. Then choosing $v$ is

$$
\min_v \sum_j |v_j| + C \sum \text{slack}
\quad \text{s.t.} \quad v \cdot h_t \le -\delta \;\; (t \in R), \qquad |v \cdot h_t| \le \varepsilon \;\; (t \in P).
$$

The L1 objective makes $v$ sparse, so the solve picks the neurons as well as the amounts, which is the job Astra's mask and its $\alpha$
did. HiGHS took over 20 minutes per solve on these dense rows, so the repo uses PDHG on the GPU, which `docs/WRITEUP.md` says reaches
HiGHS's optimum to within 0.2% in about a minute. The layers (15 and 19) and, in the best version, the direction $u$ are still Astra's.

### What the post got right, and what it stretched

The "eight hours and about thirty passes" is not the whole run. The write-up reconstructs the p50c log's timeline: 15 minutes to find
$u$, about two hours to settle on layers 15 and 19, then 7.7 hours and 28 counted passes (6 to 33) choosing columns and dose, the
edit frozen at about ten hours, and 17 more passes confirming it. The 20.3 hours in the table near the top of this page is the full
run, 4.7 hours of it an outage. The LP replaces the 7.7-hour phase. "A minute" is the solve itself; it also needs one GPU generation
run on 512 problems for the labelled activations and a 14-edit dev sweep to pick $\varepsilon$ and the neuron count. The whole project, benchmark
runs included, took about 18 H100-hours and 1.5 A100-hours, which `results/spend.md` puts at \$63.

The weight count holds. The chosen edit uses 8 neurons per layer, and `edits/shift_k8.json` counts 8,149 changed bf16 values in layer 15
and 8,142 in layer 19, 16,291 in all. Against the 130,188 I counted in the diff above (Barry counts the same 130,188, since 884 of
the 131,072 touched values round back), that is 8.0 times fewer. The size of the change is another matter. The same file gives a
relative Frobenius change ($\lVert \Delta W \rVert / \lVert W \rVert$, the measure I used above) of 8.5% in layer 15 and 34.0% in layer 19, against 10.64% and 8.11% for Astra's edit. In layer 19 the LP
edit moves the matrix about four times as far, through eight columns. Fewer weights is not a smaller edit, which is the point of
"[Weights touched is the wrong headline](/articles/weight-edits-without-finetuning#weights-touched-is-the-wrong-headline)" on
the Deletion Tuning page.

The scores are within noise of Astra's, with more samples than any check on this page. From `results/benchmarks/README.md`, paired
per problem against Astra's edit:

| Benchmark (problems x samples) | Base | Astra's edit | LP, Astra's direction | LP minus Astra (SE) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GSM8K test (1,319 x 2) | 14.1 | 68.2 | 69.6 | +1.4 (0.9) |
| MATH-500 (500 x 4) | 7.0 | 50.9 | 51.7 | +0.8 (1.1) |

second LP edit, with $u$ taken from the model's own loops instead of Astra's hand-written pairs (cosine about 0.6 with Astra's), gets

"Fires on nearly 90% of tokens with a near-constant push" describes the LP edit in one layer, not Astra's weights. In
`results/analysis/overlap.json`, the LP's 8 neurons in layer 15 are active on a median 89.3% of tokens (a typical neuron: 15.7%), and
their push along $u$ has a coefficient of variation of 0.17. In layer 19 the same edit's neurons are active on 27.2% of tokens with a
coefficient of variation of 0.81. Astra's 64 neurons are active on about 35% in both layers, with coefficients of variation of 1.11 and
1.38: a push that varies a lot from token to token.

The steering vector is the strongest claim, and the thinnest. Barry took the LP edit's average push, $\mathrm{mean}_t(v \cdot h_t)\,u$
(norm 0.89 in layer 15 and 0.67 in layer 19), and added it as a constant to the unedited model through a vLLM plugin. At that strength
it scores 57.8 on the 128-problem dev set and 50.8 on MATH-500 with one sample per problem, against 50.0 for Astra's edit and 53.2 for
the LP edit on the same seed. Half the strength drops the dev score to 51.6, and double it gives 31.2: the loops stop and the reasoning
breaks. So a fixed vector does the job on one seed, inside a narrow window. "The effect is identical" is stronger than one seed allows,
and the window is the part that matters.

### What this changes on this page

Barry's findings hit my sweep where it was weakest. In "Turning the one knob" I measured selectivity: how much more the edit penalizes
loop tokens than productive ones under teacher forcing, and I read the rise in that ratio with dose as part of how the edit works. In
generation that did not matter. Asking the LP to push only on loop-like tokens did no better than pushing on every token, fewer neurons
beat more (8 over 32 over 128), and the write-up reports that Astra's own teacher-forced metric ranked several edits wrongly. A probe on
layer 15 detects a loop once it is under way (AUC 0.93) but not where it starts (0.73), so an edit that waits for a loop acts too late.
My numbers describe the edit correctly; selectivity just isn't what makes it work.

The rest holds. "Why six" found that $\alpha = 6$ is about the dose that cancels the stream's component along $u$ inside a loop, and
Barry's narrow steering window says the same thing in generation: the fix is a direction and an amount. The random-direction control
agrees: his random direction scores 10.2 on the dev set against the base model's 10.9. Pass 28, where 64 columns and all 3,584 gave the same gain, fits his
finding that many sparse solutions work: his 8 neurons share 3 with Astra's 64 in layer 15 and 1 in layer 19.

Where I land: the LP result takes most of the mystique out of the agent's search, and I think it is right to. Astra did the
diagnosis fast (the direction in 15 minutes) and then spent most of its time tuning one amount that a solver or a dose sweep finds
directly. That is still useful work for an agent, and it is the work a linear program does better. The non-thinking question in
"Limits" above is untouched: every number here is in thinking mode, where the base loops.
