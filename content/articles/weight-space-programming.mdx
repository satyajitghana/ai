On 2 November 2026 four MIT CSAIL researchers (Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang and Omar Khattab) published
[Sparks of Weight-Space Programming](https://rikiyat.github.io/blog/weight-space-programming/). The claim is that a
frontier model, GPT-6 Astra, used as it is, can read and write neural network weights: name the game a policy was
trained on from its raw weights, repair a stuck car controller, write game-playing networks from zero, and, in the
experiment that went viral, make Qwen3.5-0.8B better at math by editing its weights directly. **Reported:** MATH-500
went from 7.9% to 48.1%, GSM8K from 13.8% to 67.1%, IFEval from 21.0% to 62.3%, by changing "only 0.016% of
weights", with no gradients and no training.

Within a day the replies split. One reply called it a loop fix dressed up: Qwen3.5-0.8B runs in non-thinking mode by
default, and in thinking mode it loops, so of course a fix for looping helps. Another said Gaussian noise on the last
four layers doubles the score too. Hugging Face's Ahsen Khaliq rebuilt the checkpoint from the published logs and
re-ran MATH-500 at 51.0%.

Everything needed to check the core claim is public: both checkpoints, the edit as a diff, and a log of every
message, every Python run and every forward pass of the agent's 20-hour session. So I checked it. This page labels
each claim **measured** (I ran it), **reported** (a source says so) or **reasoned** (my inference).

## What the experiment was

**Reported, from the post and `exp8_math/README.md` in the logs.** Astra got a persistent Python kernel over
Qwen3.5-0.8B's weights (CPU only, no GPU, no internet), practice problems with answers (7,469 from GSM8K train, 7,275
from MATH train), and a budget of 10, 25 or 50 forward passes. A pass is either a generation (the model answers up to
512 practice problems with the evaluation's sampling, original and edited side by side if asked) or a teacher-forced
read of up to 1,024 texts that returns log-probs, hidden states and MLP activations. Gradient descent, autograd and
any iterative fitting were forbidden. So were edits to the embedding, the output head and the final norm, and any
edit that just multiplies a whole tensor by a constant. Astra never saw the benchmarks.

The evaluation is fixed: thinking on, temperature 0.6, top-p 0.95, top-k 20, presence penalty 1.5, at most 32,768 new
tokens, last `\boxed{}` graded with math-verify. Those are the thinking-mode settings Qwen's own card uses for its
benchmarks.

The three runs ended with three different edits:

| Budget | Turns | Hours | Final edit (from each run's `final_answer.md`) | MATH-500 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| none | | | the base model | 7.9% |
| 10 passes | 74 | 11.6 | subtract 1.5x a loop direction from 30 output matrices in layers 8-22 | 12.3% |
| 25 passes | 86 | 8.5 | zero attention head 7 of layer 15 | 14.1% |
| 50 passes | 158 | 20.3 | subtract a loop direction from 64 MLP output columns in layers 15 and 19 | 48.1% |

Only the 50-pass edit is published as a checkpoint, so that is the one I took apart.

## The edit, measured from the two checkpoints

**Measured.** I streamed both safetensors files over HTTP range requests (base `Qwen/Qwen3.5-0.8B` at commit
`2fc0636`, edited at `d1671a1`) and compared all 488 tensors byte for byte. Exactly two differ:
`model.language_model.layers.15.mlp.down_proj.weight` and `…layers.19.mlp.down_proj.weight`, both `[1024, 3584]` in
bfloat16. Every other tensor, vision tower included, is identical.

Inside each, 64 of the 3,584 columns changed. A column of `down_proj` is what one MLP neuron writes into the
1,024-wide residual stream. The bits differ in 65,260 values in layer 15 and 64,928 in layer 19, 130,188 in all. That is
a little under the 131,072 values the edit touched, because some updated values round back to the same bfloat16
number.

The change in each layer is one outer product. I took an SVD of the difference restricted to the 64 columns:
99.9995% of its energy is in the first singular value in layer 15 (99.9991% in layer 19), and the next singular values
(0.001) are the size of bfloat16 rounding. So the edit is

$$
W' = W - \alpha\, u\,(u^\top W)\,M, \qquad \alpha = 6
$$

where $u$ is a unit "loop direction" in the residual stream and $M$ keeps only the 64 chosen columns. That matches the
formula in Astra's final report, `W = W0 - 6*outer(u, mask64*(u@W0))`. I measured what it does to each changed column:
its component along $u$ goes from $c$ to $-5c$ (the median ratio is -5.00 in both layers). Removing the component
would be $\alpha = 1$. This edit overshoots it, so those 64 neurons now write *against* the loop direction, five times
as hard as they used to write along it. The relative Frobenius change is 10.64% of layer 15's matrix and 8.11% of
layer 19's. The two directions are not the same vector: their cosine is 0.85.

<EditAnatomy />

The "0.016%" is honest arithmetic, with a choice of denominator. **Measured:** the release has 873,438,784
parameters, the language model 752,393,024 of them, and 254,279,680 of those are the tied embedding table. 131,072
touched weights is 0.0150% of the release, 0.0174% of the language model and 0.0263% of the language model without
its embedding. The post's 0.016% is 131,072 over a round 0.8 billion. Any of these is small. The more useful way to
say it is that the edit is described by two 1,024-number directions and 128 column indices; the strength is one
constant.

One more measured detail. The 64 columns are not simply the 64 with the largest $|u^\top W|$: only 31 of them in layer
15, and 18 in layer 19, are in that top 64. **Reported, from `work_notes/experiment_history.md`:** they were chosen
from activations on a pass of the original model, as the neurons that carry most of the difference between what is
written on looping and on productive text ("~91%/98% of transfer repetitive-minus-productive writing contrast, but
50%/40% of full directional weight norm").

## How Astra found it

**Reported, from the p50c run's log.** The log reads like an ordinary interpretability workflow, run by a model with a
tight forward-pass budget.

1. **Diagnose.** Pass 1 ran the original model on 96 practice problems: 11 right, 85 cut at the token limit, no
   finished wrong answers. Astra wrote that the dominant failure was "pathological long/repetitive reasoning", not math.
2. **Find a direction.** Teacher-forced reads of matched contexts, the same problem continuing productively or into
   repetition, gave a common difference vector in the residual stream. A first try ablated a single neuron in layer 19
   (+6.25 points on 32 problems, within noise).
3. **Turn the dose up.** Removing the direction from both layers' full `down_proj` at α = 1 took 128 fresh problems from
   8.59% to 32.81%. α = 2 on a 256-answer set: 29 right to 92. Restricting it to 64 neurons per layer at α = 4: 13 of
   128 to 47 of 128. At α = 6 with the full 32,768 tokens: 7 of 64 to 35 of 64, with the share cut at the limit falling
   from 89% to 8%.
4. **Control.** A norm-matched random direction, orthogonal to the loop direction, at the same dose: +1.56 points
   with a standard error of 3.52, on 128 answers. Not the effect.
5. **Confirm on held-out problems.** Frozen before this step, the edit was run against the original on 1,152 practice
   problems not used to choose it: 10.6% to 60.4%, 579 rescues and 5 losses. Of the 579 rescues, 576 were answers the
   original had not finished. Accuracy on answers the original did finish did not change.

Astra's own reading of that last line is the honest one: the edit lets the model finish answers it could already work
out. The post says the same thing in its Gemma follow-up: on Gemma 3 270M, which reaches its token limit on only 8.5%
of answers, the same procedure found ordinary reasoning errors and no component to switch off, and MATH-500 stayed
between 6.8% and 7.7%.

The task prompt matters for judging how much was discovered. **Reported, from `turns/t001/prompt.txt`:** it names the
target as an example, "if the model often fails because it repeats itself and never finishes, finding and fixing what
drives that repetition counts", and tells Astra to "go after the largest category you can explain". It also rules out
the cheap fix: "making the model skip its thinking or close it early regardless of the problem" is not wanted. So
Astra did not have to find out *that* the model loops. It had to find *where*, in weights, and do it within 50 passes.

## What the edit does to the answers

**Measured, from the 20 published samples per model** in the edited repository's `eval/` folder (the first sample of
each of the first 20 problems in the re-run's smoke test, thinking on, 32,768-token cap). I counted closed `</think>`
tags, right `\boxed{}` answers and occurrences of "wait", and marked a loop onset where at least 90% of the next 3,000
characters' lines repeat a line already written.

<LoopStrip />

The base model closes its thinking in 1 of 20 answers and gets that one right. The other 19 loop: their tails are
lines like `Wait, $f(-1) = 5/3$.` repeated until the cap, and the median answer says "wait" 764.5 times. The edited
model closes in 18 of 20 and is right in 10. It still falls into a loop in 9 of 20, with a later onset, and all 9 of those
eventually close. Its two capped answers fail in a new way: each ends in a run of more than three million whitespace characters.
The logs flag the same thing ("whitespace degeneration").

Twenty answers per model are an illustration. The re-run behind them is the real check. **Reported, from the model
repository:** 500 problems x 4 samples, the post's protocol on vLLM, scored 51.0% with 31.6% of answers hitting the
token limit. Its 25-problem smoke test of the base model scored 5.0% with 95% at the limit.

### Scoring the loop under both models (CPU)

**Measured.** To see the mechanism rather than its outcome, I took base-model answers from those samples, split each at
its loop onset, and scored the text before and after the onset under both sets of weights, teacher-forced, on a CPU
(bfloat16, PyTorch 2.14, transformers 5.20.2, two threads). If the edit works the way the log says, the looping tail
should become much less likely and the productive head should barely change.

Five base answers, mean log-probability per token (nats) of the text before the onset and of about 3,000 characters
after it:

| Problem | Tokens before onset | Tokens after | Before onset: base → edited | Loop: base → edited |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `precalculus/807` | 7,068 | 1,367 | -0.346 → -0.362 (-0.016) | -0.030 → -0.076 (-0.045) |
| `intermediate_algebra/1994` | 9,046 | 1,461 | -0.277 → -0.294 (-0.017) | -0.018 → -0.061 (-0.043) |
| `algebra/2584` | 7,778 | 1,285 | -0.309 → -0.325 (-0.016) | -0.072 → -0.129 (-0.056) |
| `number_theory/572` | 8,232 | 1,083 | -0.169 → -0.201 (-0.032) | -0.031 → -0.121 (-0.090) |
| `algebra/1349` | 4,130 | 1,609 | -0.367 → -0.382 (-0.015) | -0.056 → -0.086 (-0.030) |

The loop tail is cheap for the base model: -0.03 nats per token means each next token of the loop has about a 97%
chance. The edit makes every loop token less likely in all five answers, by 0.053 nats on average, and costs the
productive text before the onset 0.019 nats per token (about 2% in perplexity). That is selective, but less than
Astra's own measurement on its chosen cases (-0.103 against -0.009 at the same dose, from `final_report.md`).
**Reasoned:** a per-token penalty of 0.05 looks small, but it compounds. Summed over 1,000 tokens of loop, the edited model gives that exact
continuation about $e^{-50}$ times the probability the base model gave it. Each step of the loop is a little more
likely to be left, sampling gets many steps to leave it, and presence penalty 1.5 pushes the same way. That is why a change too small to notice on productive text moves the share of answers cut at the limit from
93% to 33%.

## The criticism: thinking mode, and noise

**Reported, from Qwen's model card:** "Qwen3.5-0.8B operates in non-thinking mode by default", and in thinking mode
"Qwen3.5-0.8B is more prone to entering thinking loops compared to other Qwen3.5 models". The card's own IFEval for
this model is 44.0 in thinking mode and 52.1 in non-thinking mode. The post's base IFEval is 21.0 with thinking on and
a 32,768-token cap; 74.9% of those answers were cut off (`eval/unedited/merged.json`) and an answer cut inside its
thinking scores zero. **Reasoned:** Qwen probably ran its card with a larger budget, so a cut-off loop hurt it less;
the card does not say.

The sharpest reply, from @dummerspast39, says that in default non-thinking mode the unedited model scores 47% on
MATH-500 against 48% for the edit. **Reported, unverified:** the post gives no sample count, prompt or sampling
settings, and I found no files behind it.

NOTHINK_PARA

The other reply, from @yavuzbakman, shows MATH-500 going from 8.8% to 16.2% after adding Gaussian noise to the last
four layers ("seed 11, 20%"), one sample per problem over all 500. **Reported.** One seed, named, out of an unknown
number tried; at one sample per problem the base's own run-to-run spread is not small. Set it beside Astra's logged
control: a norm-matched random direction at the edit's dose gained +1.56 points (SE 3.52) on paired practice
problems. **Reasoned:** both can be true. Noise that big perturbs the late layers enough to knock some answers out of a
loop; a single random direction in two layers does not. Neither is close to the targeted edit's 40-point gain under
the same protocol. The arXiv paper the reply points to, [Evolution Strategies at Scale](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.24372)
by Qiu and colleagues, is about fine-tuning with many such perturbations and a reward, not about one random draw.

<ScoreBoard />

So both sides are right about something. **Reasoned:**

- The edit is real, small and specific. It is the same two tensors in both public checkpoints, it is rank one per
  layer, it was found by contrasting activations and confirmed on held-out problems against a random control, and it
  generalizes past math because loops are not specific to math (IFEval, GPQA Diamond and MMLU-Pro all went up).
- The "up to 6x" is a fix for a failure that this model's default mode does not have. The relevant comparison is not
  7.9% but the base model with thinking off, and there the reply's numbers say the edit adds about one point. Nothing
  here shows Astra made the model better at math than it already is; the post's own Gemma result says it could not.
- Some of what is new is the operator, not the operation. Finding a direction from contrasting activations and
  projecting it out of a weight matrix is an established interpretability edit; the agent ran that workflow on its own,
  with a 50-pass budget, and picked the layers, the neurons and the dose.

## The rest of the week's weight-space programming

**Reported.** Three other things were filed under the same name, and they are different kinds of object.

**TensorSlay's fact writer** ([areu01or00/qwen0.8B-wsp](https://github.com/areu01or00/qwen0.8B-wsp)) writes a short
profile of its author into Qwen3.5-0.8B: five `down_proj` tensors (layers 17-21), each changed by at most 25% of its
norm. I read its README and `wsp.py`. The update is a closed-form, MEMIT-style least-squares solve,
`ΔW = R Kₚᵀ (λ·Kₙ Kₙᵀ + Kₚ Kₚᵀ + μI)⁻¹`, with protected keys from about 40 everyday questions. Its own results table
is candid: on five unseen phrasings, two return the bio word for word, one starts right and then loops, one gets a
clause, one misses. It is a known editing algorithm that an agent set up, not weights the agent reasoned out.

**Chandan Singh's toy programs** ([report](https://csinva.io/weight-space-programming-autoresearch/analyze/toy_examples/report.html))
gave Claude, as a coding agent, a blank transformer and the rule "set every weight by hand". Across 13 tasks it wrote
959 programs; the ten algorithmic ones (addition, multiplication, sorting, parity, GCD and more) reached 100%
accuracy within three attempts. The parity model has 174 weights, 35 of them nonzero. This is the forward direction
the post's title promises, at a scale where every weight can be checked by hand.

**Weight-space learning**, which Boris Knyazev pointed to, is the other route: train a model whose outputs are
weights, "Stable Diffusion for weights" in his phrase. Weight-space programming asks a general model to reason its way
to an edit; weight-space learning trains a generator for one.

The closest page on this site is [Program-as-Weights](/articles/program-as-weights), where a compiler model turns a
spec into a LoRA, trained for that job. The edit here is also what the neuron pages do by hand:
[neuron steering](/articles/neuron-steering) scales a single neuron's write, [neuron finding](/articles/neuron-finding)
searches for which one, and [neuron lens](/articles/neuron-lens) reads what a neuron's weights say. An agent that picks
64 writer neurons by their activation contrast and rescales their write along one direction is doing the same thing,
in two layers at once.

## What I ran, and what I did not

**Ran:** a byte-for-byte diff of all 488 tensors of both checkpoints over HTTP range requests; an SVD of each changed
matrix's difference; counts on the 20 published samples per model; teacher-forced scoring of base-model loops under
both weights; NOTHINK_RAN. **Read:** the post, the edited model's card and `eval/` files, the logs' `exp8_math`
README, the p50c run's final answer, history notes, manifest and first prompt, TensorSlay's README, Singh's report,
and Qwen's model card.

**Did not run:** the full MATH-500 protocol in either mode. At 32,768 tokens per answer the re-run took 43.4 million
generated tokens on an H200; on this CPU that is out of reach, so the 7.9%, 48.1%, 51.0%, 47% and 16.2% are all
reported. I did not re-run Astra's search, the noise experiment or TensorSlay's writer.
