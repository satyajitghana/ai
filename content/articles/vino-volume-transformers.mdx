Every 3D self-supervised model I have tried on a real scan has the same tell.
Freeze the encoder, fit a linear head, and the chair legs come out the same
colour as the table legs. They are both thin vertical cylinders. The features
learned geometry, not objects. In 2D, a frozen DINOv2 with a linear head gets
close to a supervised model. In 3D it has not.

[VINO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07176) (Adrian Kruse, Kadir Yilmaz, Daan de
Geus, Francis Engelmann and Bastian Leibe; RWTH Aachen, TU Eindhoven and USI
Lugano; arXiv 4 November 2026) is a paper about why. Its authors posted it as
"DINO for 3D/volumetric data". It takes Sonata, the current reference recipe
for 3D self-distillation, and changes five things: the backbone, how the
teacher sees the scene, where its targets come from, what the student sees when
part of the scene is masked, and how 2D image features are distilled in. Each
change is small. Together they move ScanNet200 linear probing from 28.3 to
40.4 mIoU on the paper's own ablation path.

What you can check today is limited. The [VINO repository](https://github.com/AdrianKruse/VINO)
is a README: "Code and pretrained weights are coming soon." The backbone it
builds on, [Volt](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19609) (same group, April 2026),
is [open under MIT](https://github.com/YilmazKadir/Volt). So I read the VINO
paper and the Volt code, re-implemented Volt's tokenizer and encoder, and ran
them on CPU on Pointcept's demo scenes. Labels below: **measured** is a number
I produced, **reported** is the paper's, **reasoned** is my inference.

## The backbone change: from a U-Net to a ViT

Sonata and almost every recent 3D self-supervised method
(Concerto, Utonia, DOS, PointINS, LAM3C) use Point Transformer V3. PTv3 is
hierarchical: it serialises points along space-filling curves, attends inside
local windows, pools, and repeats at coarser resolutions, like a U-Net. Dense
self-distillation on a hierarchy needs a feature at every point, so Sonata
upsamples the outputs of several stages and concatenates them before the
loss.

Volt does what ViT did to images. From the Volt code
(`pointcept/models/volt/volt_base.py`, commit `8dcd608`):

1. Voxelise the cloud on a 2 cm grid indoors, 5 cm outdoors, one point per
   occupied voxel.
2. Cut space into non-overlapping cubes of P x P x P voxels, P = 5. That is a
   10 cm cube indoors and 25 cm outdoors.
3. Each non-empty cube becomes one token: scatter its voxels' features into a
   dense `[125, C]` block, zero the empty slots, flatten, and apply one
   `nn.Linear(125 * C, D)`.
4. Run a plain pre-norm Transformer over all tokens with full global attention
   (FlashAttention varlen, one sequence per scene) and 3D RoPE on the integer
   patch coordinates.
5. A single transposed-convolution-like layer (a linear to `125 * D'` and a
   gather) puts features back on the voxels.

No windows, no serialisation, no pooling. The interesting question for a
ViT on 3D is how long the sequence gets, because attention is quadratic in it.
Volt's paper says "on the order of 5,000 tokens" for a ScanNet scene. I
counted.

<TokenBudget />

**Measured.** Pointcept's `sample1` is an indoor room, 273,530 points over
7.2 x 8.0 x 2.5 m. At 2 cm it has 187,899 occupied voxels. With P = 5 those
collapse to 12,092 tokens, about 240 times fewer attention pairs than one token
per voxel. The room is larger than a typical ScanNet scan, which is why it
lands above the 5,000 figure; VINO crops indoor views to at most 65,536
voxels during pre-training (reported), which on this room's density is about
4,200 tokens (reasoned, by proportion).

The number I did not expect is the fill. A token has 125 slots and the room
fills 15.5 of them on average, median 15. One token in three has ten or fewer
occupied voxels. Surfaces are 2D, so a 10 cm cube cut through a wall holds one
sheet of voxels, about 25 of 125, and a cube that clips the edge of a chair
leg holds three. Outdoors it is starker: the LiDAR sweep at 5 cm gives 32,700
tokens with a median of 2 occupied voxels each, 3.1% fill. VINO caps outdoor
crops at 6,000 tokens (reported). That sparsity is the reason two of VINO's
four fixes exist, as we will see.

I also ran a forward pass. The sandbox would not execute the downloaded Volt
code, so I wrote the encoder again from the source: the same tokenizer, RoPE
cache (theta 100, frequency split 12/12/8), QK-norm attention blocks and
detokenizer, with PyTorch's `scaled_dot_product_attention` in place of
FlashAttention. **Measured** (PyTorch 2.14.1, 2 CPU threads, random weights,
fp32): Volt-S (384 wide, 12 blocks, 6 heads) has 23.68M parameters and takes
19.1 s for the 12,092-token room; Volt-B (768 wide, 12 heads) has 85.63M
parameters without its decoder, 87.78M with it, and takes 42.1 s. Peak RSS was
under 2 GB. The 85.6M matches the "Volt-B (85.7 M)" in VINO's tables, so the
architecture the paper describes is the one in the Volt repository. The CPU
times say nothing about GPU speed; the Volt paper reports Volt-B as twice as
fast as PTv3-S on A100 and H100 (reported).

Swapping the backbone alone, Sonata's recipe goes from 28.3 to 29.6 on
ScanNet200 linear probing (reported, Table 1). A 1.3-point gain is real but
modest. The bigger effect is downstream: with one flat sequence of tokens, every
trick from 2D self-distillation (registers, cross-attention predictors,
dropping masked tokens) applies without adapting it to a multi-stage pyramid.

## The four leaks

Self-distillation has two objectives. Local prediction: a student sees a small
crop and must predict, at each token, what the teacher said about the same
location in a big crop. Masked prediction: the student sees a big crop with
blocks hidden and must predict the teacher's output at the hidden blocks. Both
work when the only way to solve them is to understand the scene. VINO's
argument is that, in 3D, each objective as Sonata built it has a shortcut.

### Leak one: the teacher sees what the student sees

Sonata applies the same augmentation set to every view: elastic distortion,
dropout and jitter on the teacher's global crops as well as on the student's
local crops. The teacher's targets are then computed from warped geometry. VINO
keeps the teacher's two global views clean (standard rotations, flips,
scaling and coordinate jitter only) and puts the strong corruptions only on the student's four local
views: three of elastic distortion, point dropout, colour jitter and chromatic
translation indoors (reported). This alone is +2.4 mIoU (Table 2).

### Leak two: the target head drifts toward the student

In DINO, the teacher's projection head is an exponential moving average of the
student's. Once the two see different input distributions, that coupling makes
the teacher's head track one adapted to corrupted inputs. VINO replaces it with
a target head that belongs only to the teacher, borrowed from CAPI: 4,096 learned
prototypes $c_k$, cosine similarities to the teacher's token features $x_i$,
sharpened by a temperature and balanced with three Sinkhorn-Knopp iterations:

$$
\ell_{i,k} = \frac{c_k^\top x_i}{\lVert c_k\rVert\,\lVert x_i\rVert},\qquad P_t = \mathrm{SK}\!\left(\ell / \tau_t\right),\quad \tau_t = 0.05
$$

The prototypes cannot be trained by the student's loss without collapsing, so
they get their own: for each pair of corresponding tokens in the two global
views (nearest token centres, at most 2 tokens apart), the balanced assignment
of one is the target for the other's softmax over prototypes, and vice versa.
That swapped clustering loss updates only the prototypes. The student's head is
a separate MLP trained with cross-entropy against $P_t$. **Reported:** +1.5
mIoU (Table 2), and it is what makes leak three's fix possible, because masked
prediction reuses the same targets.

### Leak three: the mask keeps the shape

This is the change I find most convincing, and the one where 3D differs from
2D for a real reason. In an image every patch exists; masking a patch with a
learned embedding hides its content and nothing else. In a sparse voxel grid,
which tokens exist is itself the geometry. Sonata and other scene-level methods
replace masked features with a mask embedding but leave the tokens where they
are. The student then sees the exact outline of every masked region and can
predict "table" from "flat slab at 75 cm" without looking at any context.

How much does that outline tell you? I measured it on the demo room with
VINO's masking settings: 8-token blocks (80 cm) with 55% of blocks masked, the
token label being the majority ScanNet-20 class of its voxels. For each masked
token I built a lookup from its own geometry to a label, fitted on a random
half of the masked tokens and scored on the other half, over 10 random masks.

| What the predictor sees about a masked token | Accuracy (measured) |
|---|---|
| nothing (most common class) | 29.7% |
| its height in the room | 57.2% |
| its occupied-neighbour pattern (in-plane and above/below counts) | 54.6% |
| height and neighbour pattern | 62.2% |

A two-key lookup table, with no learning and no context, gets 62% of masked
tokens right in a room where guessing gets 30%. A Transformer with the whole
masked outline available can do much better than a lookup table. That is the
shortcut. The demo room has only ten classes present and 12,092 tokens, so
treat these as an illustration of scale, not a benchmark.

VINO deletes masked tokens before the student encoder, as MAE does for images
and Point-MAE does for objects. A small decoder of four cross-attention layers
then places one query, a learned mask embedding, at each masked position and
lets it attend to the encoded visible tokens with the same 3D RoPE. Queries do
not attend to each other, so no query can see where the other masked tokens
are. **Reported** (Table 11): cross-attention only 35.9, adding query-to-query
attention 34.2, a full self-attention decoder 33.0.

My measurement also shows what is left (reasoned). Each query still carries
its own position, and through RoPE its offset from the visible floor and
walls. The height row above, 57%, is information VINO does not remove, and
probably should not: knowing where you are is part of the context. What VINO
removes is the neighbour pattern, the shape of the hole. Adding masked
prediction this way is worth +2.8 mIoU (Table 3), the largest single step in
the self-supervised stage.

### Leak four: the tokenizer counts voxels

Volt's tokenizer is a weighted sum over a patch's occupied slots. A patch with
40 voxels sums 40 contributions; a patch with 3 sums 3. The paper traces
high-norm token artifacts that survive register tokens to exactly this: early in
training, before semantic features exist, the token's magnitude encodes how full
its patch is, and the student can match the teacher on that. VINO divides the
sum by the number of occupied voxels: an average.

I wanted to see the effect in numbers, so I measured token norms at
initialisation on the demo room, 12,092 tokens, Volt's init (truncated normal,
standard deviation 0.02). There is a twist.

<TokenizerNorm />

**Measured.** With Volt's tokenizer as built, every slot of the 125 has its own
block of weights. At random init, the contributions of different voxels point
in independent directions, so the norm of their sum grows as the square root
of the count: the log-log slope is +0.50, and the correlation of norm with
occupancy is 0.88. Averaging does not make that flat. It divides by n instead of
the square root of n, and the slope becomes −0.50: now sparse patches have the
largest tokens, with correlation −0.68. A divide-by-√n control is flat (slope
0.00).

Tie every slot to the same weights and the picture inverts. Now each voxel
pushes the token the same way, the sum grows almost linearly (slope +0.94), and
averaging is the flat one (slope −0.06).

What this means (reasoned): averaging removes the occupancy signal only to the
extent that a patch's voxel contributions agree, as they do on a flat surface
whose voxels share a colour and a normal once the tokenizer has learned
something smooth. At step zero it replaces "big tokens are full patches" with
"big tokens are sparse patches". The paper measures what happens after
training, and there the fix works: fewer outliers (its Figure 7 histogram) and
+0.3 mIoU (Table 3). My numbers say nothing against that. They say the cure is
a property of the trained tokenizer, not of the arithmetic, and that if you
copy the idea into a model whose slots stay decorrelated, a √n normaliser is
the one that is flat by construction. I did not train anything to test which
is better.

## Distilling 2D without the view baked in

The second training stage adds DINOv2-g/14 as a frozen teacher, for 30,000
more steps. The usual recipe (Concerto, DITR) projects each 3D point into
posed images and regresses the 2D patch feature with a cosine loss. VINO's
objection: a ViT patch feature also encodes where the patch sits in the image,
so the same table corner seen from two cameras gives two different targets.

The fix reuses leak two's machinery. Learn 4,096 prototypes in DINOv2's feature
space with the swapped clustering loss, on pairs of image patches from
different views that observe the same valid 3D point.
A component of the feature that differs between the two views cannot help one
predict the other's assignment, so the prototypes keep the view-consistent
part. The 3D student averages its point features per image patch and predicts
that patch's soft assignment. **Reported:** prototypes beat raw-embedding
regression by 1.8 mIoU and training 2D-3D from step 0 is 1.4 worse than adding
it as a second stage (Table 4).

The stage also adds colour dropout and arbitrary SO(3) rotations to the
student's local views only, so the encoder works on uncoloured, non-upright
clouds. That matters for anyone feeding it LiDAR or a scan that was not
gravity-aligned.

| Sonata | PTv3-B, 108.4M | 29.3 | 72.5 | 38.9 | 66.1 | 62.0 | 60.5 |
| DOS | PTv3-B, 108.4M | 30.7 | 73.9 | – | 74.8 | 68.3 | 67.0 |

Three things to read carefully.

The "+5.2 on ScanNet200" is against DOS's 30.7, not Sonata. The ablation's
Sonata baseline is a reproduction at 28.3; the table quotes Sonata's own 29.3.
Neither changes the direction.

"VINO even surpasses the fully supervised Volt-B" holds on three of six
benchmarks: ScanNet200 by 4.2 points, ScanNet by 0.2 and SemanticKITTI by 0.5.
On ScanNet++, nuScenes and Waymo the supervised model is still ahead. On a
long-tailed label set like ScanNet200 a frozen self-supervised encoder beating
a supervised one is a genuinely good sign; on ScanNet a 0.2-point gap is a
tie.

The registration result (Table 9: 84.1% success on 3DMatch and 44.6% on
3DLoMatch, against FPFH's 77.5% and 36.4%) uses baselines the authors had to
fix first. Their appendix says Utonia and Concerto scored 62.0/18.5% and
54.4/13.4% with plain concatenated PTv3 features, and 76.5/36.3% and
65.0/22.7% once each stage was normalised separately. That is fair practice,
and it also shows how much of a "frozen features" comparison is the
read-out rather than the encoder. VINO reads out the last three layers of a
single-resolution stack; PTv3 methods have to upsample and concatenate a
pyramid.

Fine-tuned (Table 6), the gaps shrink, as they always do: 42.7 against Utonia's
39.6 on ScanNet200 and 52.6 against Concerto's 50.7 on ScanNet++. Waymo is the one
benchmark where VINO does not lead; it ties Sonata at 72.9 and DOS has 73.9.

## What it costs

**Reported** (Appendix A.2): stage 1 is 200,000 steps at batch 32, 24 hours on
8 H100s indoors and 24 hours on 16 H100s outdoors; stage 2 adds about 9 and 8
hours on the same counts. That is roughly 260 H100-hours for the indoor model
and 510 for the outdoor one (reasoned, multiplying). The indoor stage 1 uses
about 18,000 real scans; stage 2 grows that to about 90,000 by adding
RealEstate10K videos reconstructed with Depth Anything 3. Outdoor uses about
110,000 LiDAR sweeps from nuScenes, SemanticKITTI and Waymo. Separate models
for indoor and outdoor; there is no single checkpoint.

  no config and no checkpoint, so every mIoU in this article is the paper's.

## Should you care

If you train 3D encoders, three of VINO's changes are portable today and none
needs its code. Keep the teacher's views clean. Drop masked tokens from the
encoder and predict them with cross-attention only, when your data is sparse.
Normalise your patch tokenizer so its norm cannot count points, and check the
slope on your own data, as I did here, before trusting that averaging does it.

If you use frozen 3D features (registration, open-vocabulary lookup, probing a
LiDAR scene), the point is that a plain ViT over 10 cm cubes is now the
strongest frozen 3D feature extractor on paper, at 85.7M parameters against
PTv3-B's 108.4M. Whether that holds in your pipeline waits on the weights.

For the other half of the point-cloud robustness problem, what happens when
the grid size you deploy at differs from the one you trained at, see
[Invaria](/articles/invaria-point-clouds). Volt fixes the voxel size and the
patch size in its config, so the problem that article describes applies to it
unchanged.
