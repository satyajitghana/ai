A month after [Bonsai 2 27B](/articles/bonsai-2-27b), PrismML has shipped the rest of the
family: **Ternary Bonsai 2 2B, 4B and 9B**, built on Qwen3.5-2B, 4B and 9B, Apache 2.0, with
headline sizes of **0.43 GB, 0.95 GB and 2.0 GB**. The format is the 27B's: every weight
matrix stored as `{−1, 0, +1}` times an FP16 scale per 128 weights, in a basis rotated by a fixed
Walsh–Hadamard transform that the runtime has to undo on the activations. I covered how that
works, and why a stock runtime produces garbage without it, in the
[27B write-up](/articles/bonsai-2-27b); the gen-1 lineage is in [Bonsai 27B](/articles/bonsai-27b),
and the from-scratch version of the idea is in [Ternary15M](/articles/ternary15m). This piece is
about what is different when the model is small, and about four claims that went around on
release day:

- PrismML: "2B: 0.43GB, 4B: 0.95GB, 9B: 2.0GB", and in the launch post "more than 9× smaller
  than their full-precision counterparts."
- Atomic Chat, reposting: "PrismML shrank Qwen3.5 7x so the 9B goes from 17.9 GB to just 2.42 GB
  and it keeps up or outperforms the original model on benchmarks."
- PrismML: the 9B "uses roughly 40% fewer tokens on standard instruction-following and everyday
  math tasks."
- PrismML: "On iPhone 18 Pro Max, Bonsai 2 9B reaches up to 34 toks/sec, while the 2B model
  reaches up to 120 toks/sec."

I pulled the file listing of every repo in the
[Bonsai 2 Small collection](https://huggingface.co/collections/prism-ml/bonsai-2-small), parsed the
GGUF header of all nine language-model packs (the 2B locally, the 4B and 9B through HTTP range
requests, never downloading their weights), read the
[whitepaper](https://github.com/PrismML-Eng/Bonsai-demo/blob/main/bonsai-2-small-whitepaper.pdf)
and the [docs](https://docs.prismml.com), and built PrismML's
[llama.cpp fork](https://github.com/PrismML-Eng/llama.cpp) to run the 2B and 4B. Labels below:
**measured** is something I computed from a file or a run, **reported** is PrismML's or Atomic
Chat's figure, **reasoned** is my arithmetic on the other two.

## The sizes are exact, and they are decimal gigabytes

Start with the easy part. The three PTQ1_0 files on the Hub are **429,183,456**, **949,549,920**
and **1,997,486,976** bytes (measured, from the Hub tree API; the 2B and 4B files I downloaded
match the repos' `SHA256SUMS`). In decimal gigabytes that is 0.429, 0.950 and 1.997: the headline
0.43 / 0.95 / 2.0 GB, rounded honestly. In GiB, the unit your OS and `llama-bench` print, the same
files are 0.400, 0.884 and 1.860 GiB. `llama-bench` reports the 2B as `398.81 MiB` because it counts
only the tensor data, without the 11 MB header (measured).

What the number covers is stated plainly in the model card and the docs: the **language model
only**. The vision tower ships as a separate `mmproj` file, 0.36 GB (Q8_0) for the 2B and 4B and
0.62 GB for the 9B (measured: 364,664,352, 366,894,624 and 624,229,664 bytes). It is loaded only
when an image arrives. Text-only, the headline is the whole download; with images, the 2B is
0.79 GB and the 9B is 2.62 GB (reasoned).

## What is in the file

The tensor tables are where the small models stop looking like the 27B. Here is the 2B, measured
from its GGUF header, every tensor shape times the ggml type size (PTQ1_0 is 28 bytes per 128
weights, 1.75 bits; PQ2_0 34 bytes per 128, 2.125 bits; Q2_0 18 bytes per 64, 2.25 bits; F32 4
bytes). The slices sum to the exact file size for all nine packs.

| 2B, PTQ1_0 pack | Parameters | Bytes | Share of file |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Transformer block matrices (ternary) | 1,371,537,408 | 300,023,808 | 69.9% |
| Token embedding, tied to the LM head (ternary) | 508,559,360 | 111,247,360 | 25.9% |
| F32: norms and the linear-attention state path | 1,728,320 | 6,913,280 | 1.6% |
| GGUF header: tokenizer, chat template, Hadamard signs | | 10,999,008 | 2.6% |
| **Total** | **1,881,825,088** | **429,183,456** | |

Three things fall out of it.

**The vocabulary is a quarter of the model.** Qwen3.5 has a 248,320-token vocabulary at every
size. At hidden size 2048 that is a 508.6M-parameter table, **27.0%** of the 2B's language model;
at 4B (hidden 2560, also tied) it is 15.1%; at 9B, which unties the embedding and the head, the two
tables together are 22.7% (measured). In the 27B the same pair is under a tenth. PrismML ternarizes
the embedding and the head too, so this does not cost bits per weight directly. It costs in the
next two ways.

**The fixed costs do not shrink.** The header is 11.0 MB at every size, nearly all of it the
tokenizer, and the F32 tensors (RMSNorm weights, `ssm_alpha`, `ssm_beta`, `ssm_conv1d`, `ssm_a`,
`ssm_dt`, the Gated DeltaNet layers' state path that PrismML keeps out of ternary) are 6.9 MB at
2B. Together they are 4.2% of the 2B file and 2.0% of the 9B's (measured). That is why the
effective rate is **1.82, 1.81 and 1.78 bits per weight** for the 2B, 4B and 9B PTQ1_0 files, not
the format's 1.75 (measured: file bits over the GGUF's own parameter count; the model cards say
~1.83 / ~1.81 / ~1.78, the 2B's from dividing by a rounded 1.88B).

**The decode cost is not the file size.** A token reads every matrix it multiplies by. When the
embedding is tied to the LM head, as in the 2B and 4B, the whole table is read every token as the
head. When it is untied, as in the 9B, the embedding is only gathered one row at a time, so a 9B
decode step streams about **1.76 GB** in PTQ1_0 and **2.14 GB** in PQ2_0, not 2.0 and 2.42
(measured from the tensor tables). That matters for the phone numbers below.

## Reconciling 2.0 GB, 2.42 GB, "7x" and "more than 9x"

Atomic Chat's numbers are not wrong. They are a different file. The 9B ships in three packings of
the same ternary weights:

| 9B pack | Bytes on the Hub | Bits per weight | BF16 (17.91 GB) / this |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| `PTQ1_0`, five trits per byte | 1,997,486,976 | 1.78 | 8.97x |
| `PQ2_0`, one trit per 2-bit slot | 2,416,851,840 | 2.16 | 7.41x |
| `Q2_0`, upstream type, group 64 | 2,556,640,128 | 2.28 | 7.00x |

The model card's banner reads "~2.42 GB language model (PQ2_0), down from ~17.9 GB at FP16", and
`PQ2_0` is what the demo repo's `setup.sh` downloads by default, because it is cheaper to unpack.
Atomic Chat quoted that banner: 17.9 / 2.42 is **7.4x** (reasoned), so "7x" is a fair rounding of
the bigger pack. PrismML's "2.0 GB" is the smaller pack. Same weights, two layouts.

The 17.9 GB baseline checks out: 8,953,803,264 language-model parameters in the GGUF times 2 bytes
is 17.91 GB (measured), which is the whitepaper's figure. It is not the size of the Qwen3.5-9B
repo, which is 19.31 GB because it carries the vision tower and more (measured, Hub API).

"More than 9x smaller", in the launch post, is the one number that does not hold. Against the
whitepaper's own BF16 baselines (3.76, 8.41, 17.91 GB) the PTQ1_0 ratios are **8.77x, 8.86x and
8.97x** (reasoned). The whitepaper itself says "8.8–9.0× smaller" in Table 1, and its cover says
"~9×". Both of those are right. The launch post rounded up past them.

*Source: whitepaper Table 6; the launch post's Figure I has the same numbers.*

The averages give **96.7%, 94.6% and 95.1%** retention (reasoned). Counting benchmark by
benchmark, Bonsai is ahead of its baseline on **5 of 17** at 2B, **4 of 18 plus one tie** at 4B,
and **2 of 19** at 9B: HumanEval+ and IFEval (reasoned). At 9B six more are within two points
(MuSR, MATH-500, GSM8K, IFBench, BFCL v3, MBPP+), and eleven are behind by more. The biggest gaps
are the same three at every size: **GPQA Diamond** (−18.9, −14.2, −12.6 points), **OmniDocBench**
(−16.1, −15.3, −14.9), and **τ²-Bench** at 4B and 9B (−19.8, −12.2). So "keeps up or outperforms
the original model on benchmarks" is true of instruction following and coding, roughly true of
math, and not true of knowledge, document parsing or multi-turn tool use. PrismML's own text is
more careful than the repost: it calls the τ²-Bench drop "the largest single loss at 4B" and says
OmniDocBench "does not close with a larger model."

Two things about the above-100% rows are worth a closer look.

**The 2B baseline is unusually weak where Bonsai wins.** Qwen3.5-2B scores 65.96 on GSM8K but 85.40
on the harder MATH-500, and 34.33 on IFBench. Bonsai 2B scores 80.74 and 48.00. A full-precision
model doing worse on grade-school math than on competition math is the signature of a run that
failed to finish, not of a model that cannot add: with thinking on, a small model that loops or
runs out of output budget scores zero on that item. That is a reading, not a measurement (the
whitepaper does not report truncation rates), but it fits PrismML's stated focus. The release post
says the work went into "avoiding repetitive loops, following instructions … and controlling
verbosity." Those are post-training targets. A ternary model that beats its own BF16 parent got
something besides compression, and the comparison that would separate the two, the same
post-training at full precision, is not published.

**The aggregate has no single definition across sizes.** The 2B average is over 17 benchmarks, the
9B's over 19, so the three retention numbers are not on one scale. The whitepaper says so itself:
"averages compare within a size only."

The more useful table for choosing a model is the whitepaper's Table 8, which compares each ternary
model against a smaller model that needs more memory. The 9B (2 GB) against Qwen3.5-4B in Q3_K_M
(2.3 GB) leads on five of six category averages, 77.83 to 73.99, and on AIME26 82.50 to 54.17
(reported). The 4-bit 4B wins on vision. At equal memory, a bigger ternary model beats a smaller
conventional quant, which is the 27B's result again at a tenth of the scale.

## "Roughly 40% fewer tokens": not published anywhere I could find

The launch post and the thread say the 9B "uses roughly 40% fewer tokens on standard
instruction-following and everyday math tasks." I searched the whitepaper, the three model cards,
the docs pages for each model, and every Markdown file in the demo repo. None of them contains a
token count, a token-length comparison, or the figure 40%. There is no named benchmark, no baseline
(Qwen3.5-9B with thinking on? off?), and no budget. The whitepaper's own methodology runs both
models with thinking on and "the same output budgets", and reports accuracy only.

The direction is plausible: the model cards warn that "even simple prompts can use 3,000+ thinking
tokens", and verbosity was a training target. But "40% fewer" has nothing published behind it, and
it is a claim about post-training, not about ternary weights.

The launch post's figure has three device groups. The RTX 4090 bars, 604 / 369 / 249 tok/s for
2B / 4B / 9B, are the whitepaper's Table 3 numbers rounded (603.6, 368.6, 248.6, PTQ1_0, `tg128`,
batch 1). The other two groups are not in the whitepaper at all.

The whitepaper measured an **iPhone 17 Pro Max** (llama.cpp Metal, PQ2_0, `tg128`, batch 1):
**89.9, 39.8 and 22.3 tok/s** for the 2B, 4B and 9B, and its cover leads with "90 tok/s". The
launch post and the X thread instead quote an **iPhone 18 Pro Max** at **120, 51 and 34**, and an
"M5 Pro-Max" at 310 / 245 / 179, where the whitepaper's MacBook Pro M5 Pro tops out at 276.3 /
145.2 / 91.7 with MLX Swift. No document gives the iPhone 18 runtime, pack, context, prompt or
whether a drafter was on. "Up to" in the post is doing work: the whitepaper's Table 4 shows the
same 9B at 89 tok/s on the iPhone 17 with speculative decoding on copy-heavy agent prompts, and
"plain" (no speedup) on everyday prompts.

You can still sanity-check the numbers, because batch-1 decode is a bandwidth problem. Each token
streams the weights it multiplies once, so tok/s times bytes per token is the bandwidth the run
actually achieved. For the whitepaper's iPhone 17 rows, with the per-token bytes measured above
(PQ2_0), that is **45.5 GB/s at 2B, 45.2 at 4B and 47.6 at 9B** (reasoned): flat across sizes, which
is what a phone that is streaming weights as fast as it can looks like. For the iPhone 18 figures
it is **60.8, 57.9 and 72.6 GB/s**. The 9B's is a fifth higher than the 2B's on the same phone,
which a plain bandwidth-bound decode cannot do; something else (a drafter, a different pack, a
shorter context) is likely in the 34. That is an inference, and I could not confirm it.

The same arithmetic on the RTX 4090's PTQ1_0 rows gives 252 / 346 / 438 GB/s (reasoned), well under
the card's ~1 TB/s: at these sizes a desktop GPU is limited by kernel overheads, not by memory,
which is also why the whitepaper's speedups over BF16 are largest on the slowest devices
(5.2x on the phone for the 2B, 1.5x on a B200).

## Running it on a CPU

  repo="prism-ml/Ternary-Bonsai-2-2B-gguf"
  claimed="2B, 0.43 GB language model"
  note="Three language-model packs (PTQ1_0 0.43 GB, PQ2_0 0.52 GB, Q2_0 0.55 GB) and two vision projectors. The 2B ships no MTP head or DFlash drafter."

PrismML's demo repo pins `prism-b10777-e3de715` of its fork; I built the head of the `prism` branch (`5ae2e16`, 15 October)
with `GGML_NATIVE=ON`, on a shared 4-core Intel Xeon cloud VM with AVX-512, CPU only. Building
`llama-bench`, `llama-completion` and `llama-simple` with `-j2` took about 8 minutes.

```bash
git clone --depth 1 -b prism https://github.com/PrismML-Eng/llama.cpp && cd llama.cpp
cmake -B build -DGGML_NATIVE=ON -DLLAMA_CURL=OFF -DCMAKE_BUILD_TYPE=Release
cmake --build build -j2 --target llama-bench llama-completion llama-simple
./build/bin/llama-bench -m Ternary-Bonsai-2-2B-PTQ1_0.gguf -t 2 -p 512 -n 128 -r 3
```

All measured, `-t 2`, every command under `nice -n 19` on a machine five other jobs were sharing
(load average 7-9 on 4 cores during the runs), so read the error bars:

| Model, pack | File | Prompt processing | Generation | Peak RSS |
| :--- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 2B `PTQ1_0` | 0.43 GB | 5.54 ± 0.38 tok/s (pp512) | 2.20 ± 0.79 tok/s (tg128) | |
| 2B `PQ2_0` | 0.52 GB | 18.73 ± 0.84 tok/s (pp512) | 3.81 ± 1.67 tok/s (tg128) | |
| 2B `Q2_0` | 0.55 GB | 2.41 ± 0.28 tok/s (pp512) | 1.61 ± 0.17 tok/s (tg128) | |
| 2B, all three in one `llama-bench` | | | | 1,148.9 MB |
| 4B `PTQ1_0` | 0.95 GB | 1.95 ± 0.29 tok/s (pp128) | 0.80 ± 0.14 tok/s (tg64) | 1,072.8 MB |

None of this is comparable to PrismML's charts. The point is the ordering, which the source explains. In the fork's `ggml-cpu/arch/x86/quants.c`, `PQ2_0`
has an AVX2 dot product against `Q8_K` activations, `PTQ1_0` has only an SSSE3 path (unpacking five
trits from a byte is arithmetic, not a shift and a mask), and `Q2_0` has no x86 kernel at all and
falls back to the generic C loop. So on this CPU the 2-bit-slot pack is 3.4x faster at prompt
processing than the denser one (measured), the opposite of the RTX 4090, where `PTQ1_0` is the
fastest pack at every size (reported). Decode here runs at under 1 GB/s of effective weight traffic
(reasoned: 2.20 tok/s times 0.418 GB), so it is compute-bound, not bandwidth-bound; the widget's
bandwidth arithmetic does not apply to it. PrismML targets Metal and CUDA; the CPU backend works, but it is
not where the tok/s figures come from.

Generation swung by more than 2x between identical runs (2.23 and 5.70 tok/s for the same 64-token
decode, measured): the machine, not the model. Whether CPU weight repacking helps decode was lost in
that noise.

### What loads in RAM

A 0.52 GB file took **about 1.1 GB of resident memory** to run (measured, peak RSS 1,086-1,161 MB
across the `PQ2_0` runs). The fork's verbose log accounts for it:

- **483 MiB** of the file, memory-mapped.
- **476 MiB** more: on x86 the CPU backend repacks 151 tensors into an interleaved
  `pq2_0_4x8` layout for its GEMM, a second copy of nearly the whole model.
- **501 MiB** of compute buffer at the default 512-token micro-batch, about the size of a logits
  tensor for 512 positions over 248,320 vocabulary entries in F32 (reasoned: 512 × 248,320 × 4
  bytes is 485 MiB). The vocabulary shows up again.
- **43 MiB** of context state: the KV cache for the six full-attention layers and 19.27 MiB of
  recurrent state for the 18 linear-attention layers.

Run with `-nr` (no repack) and `-ub 64 -b 64` and the same model peaks at **633.4 MB** (measured).
On a phone the Metal path has no CPU repack, but the logits buffer is the same arithmetic, so the
micro-batch is worth setting deliberately on a memory-tight device.

### What it said

Four prompts to the 2B `PQ2_0` pack, with the model card's sampling settings, one sample each. This
is a smoke test, not an evaluation.

**Instruction following**, thinking off, "List exactly three uses for a paperclip. Use a numbered
list, at most six words per item, and no other text":

```text
1. Holds things together
2. Measures distance
3. Cuts metal
```

The format is exactly right: three items, numbered, nothing else, 16 tokens. The content is half
right. A paperclip does not cut metal.

**A tool call**, thinking off, with one `set_alarm(time, label)` function in the system prompt in
the chat template's own format, and "Wake me up at 6:45 tomorrow, call it gym":

```text
<tool_call>
<function=set_alarm>
<parameter=time>
06:45
</parameter>
<parameter=label>
Gym
</parameter>
</function>
</tool_call>
```

Well-formed, correct arguments, zero-padded to the `HH:MM` the schema asked for, 40 tokens.

**A word problem**, "muffins at 3 dollars, coffee at 2, 4 muffins and some coffees for 20 dollars,
how many coffees": correct (4) with thinking off in 193 tokens, and correct with thinking on in 178,
because with thinking on the model closed its `<think>` block immediately and wrote no reasoning
at all. One prompt proves nothing about "40% fewer tokens", but it is the behaviour that claim
describes.

## What I could not check

- **"Roughly 40% fewer tokens."** No published measurement, benchmark or baseline.
- **The iPhone 18 Pro Max and "M5 Pro-Max" figures.** No runtime, pack, prompt or drafter setting
  is published for either; the whitepaper's measured phone is an iPhone 17 Pro Max.
- **Whether the above-baseline 2B scores come from ternary training or from post-training**, and
  whether the Qwen3.5-2B baseline runs were truncated. The whitepaper reports neither.
- **The 9B on a CPU.** I parsed its header but did not download its weights.
- **The 2B MLX pack's size:** 1.34 GB in the whitepaper and docs, 1,200,284,784 bytes on the Hub
  (measured).

**"Keeps up or outperforms"** is true for instruction following and coding and false for GPQA
Diamond, OmniDocBench and τ²-Bench, 12-20 points behind; the averages are 95-97%. **"40% fewer
tokens"** and the **iPhone 18 Pro Max** figures have no published method. On a shared CPU the 2B
runs, emits a correct tool call, and uses about 1.1 GB of RAM by default for a 0.52 GB file.
</Callout>

## The takeaway

The 27B made the case that a ternary format with a rotation and a purpose-built kernel keeps most
of a model's ability at a ninth of the bytes. The small models make a different case: at 2B, the
format is no longer the whole story. A 248,320-token vocabulary is a quarter of the weights and,
on a CPU, most of the compute buffer; an 11 MB tokenizer header is 2.6% of the file; and the
choice between `PTQ1_0` and `PQ2_0` moves the size by 20% and the speed by a factor that depends on
which kernels your hardware has. The headline sizes are honest. The best number in the release is
the one in the whitepaper's Table 8: a 2 GB ternary 9B beats a 2.3 GB conventional 4B on five of
six categories. If memory is the constraint, a bigger ternary model is the better buy, and the
[27B](/articles/bonsai-2-27b) is the same argument at laptop scale.
