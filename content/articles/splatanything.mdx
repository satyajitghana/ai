Ritian Yu's post on 9 November introduced SplatAnything as a model that turns
"everyday objects and human subjects to expansive aerial scenes" into 3D
Gaussian scenes "in few seconds". Hugging Face's paper account summarised it
as "Gaussian Spacing Normalization for feed-forward 3DGS across arbitrary
scenes". Both lines are true, and the second is the interesting one. The
architecture is almost the same as the last two versions of this family. What
changed is a single number per scene: the factor the scene is divided by
before the Gaussian head sees it.

That sounds too small to matter. It turns out to decide whether one network
can be right about Gaussian size on a tabletop object and on a city block at
the same time, and the reasoning behind it is worth having even if you never
run this model.

I read the paper ([arXiv:2611.08264](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.08264), 5
November 2026), the release at
[`zju3dv/SplatAnything`](https://github.com/zju3dv/SplatAnything) (commit
`c1a8992`, 9 November), the source of the
[Hugging Face Space](https://huggingface.co/spaces/ritianyu/SplatAnything), and
the headers of both checkpoint files in
[`ritianyu/SplatAnything`](https://huggingface.co/ritianyu/SplatAnything)
through HTTP range requests. I ported the release's normalization to numpy and
ran it on six synthetic captures. I did not run the model: it needs 16.4 GB of
weights and, in practice, an NVIDIA GPU. Numbers are **measured** (I computed
or read them myself), **reported** (the authors' figure) or **reasoned** (my
arithmetic or argument on the other two).

A day later I went back for a second pass, because the first left three
questions open. What does the release do on a frame that is part sky, given
that sky masking is off by default? What does the voxel merge do to the size
of the output as resolution and view count change? And is the strange ETH3D
column in the main table a typo? For that pass I wrote a second ray caster
with four scene types, ported the rest of the inference-side code
(`merge_voxels` in full, the pose-alignment factor, the filters) to numpy,
and parsed every table in the arXiv HTML so that each comparison below could
be recomputed rather than read off by eye. All of it ran on four shared CPU
cores in under three minutes.

## Why Gaussian size depends on pixels, not metres

Feed-forward 3DGS models in this family work per pixel. A geometry model
predicts depth for every pixel of every input view, each pixel is
back-projected to a 3D point, and that point becomes the centre of one
Gaussian. The network's job is the rest: opacity, rotation, colour and,
critically, scale.

Scale is the attribute that can go wrong in both directions. If a Gaussian is
much smaller than the distance to its neighbours, the rendered surface has
gaps. If it is much larger, neighbours pile on top of each other and fine
detail smears. The right size is set by the distance between neighbouring
centres, which the paper calls **Gaussian spacing**.

For two horizontally adjacent pixels at roughly the same depth $D$, a pinhole
camera with focal length $f_x$ (in pixels) puts their back-projected points
$D / f_x$ apart. The paper takes the geometric mean of the two directions
(Eq. 4):

$$
d_i(\mathbf{u}) \approx \frac{D_i(\mathbf{u})}{\sqrt{f_{x,i}\, f_{y,i}}}
$$

So spacing is depth divided by focal length in pixels. Metres matter, but so
does the camera: a wider field of view or a lower image resolution means a
smaller focal length in pixels, and the same wall at the same distance gets
sparser Gaussians.

## What everyone normalizes by instead

A geometry foundation model cannot know absolute scale from images, so it
predicts the scene in some canonical frame. The common choice, which the
paper calls **RDNorm**, divides everything by the mean distance of the
reconstructed points from the reference camera (Eq. 5):

$$
s_r = \frac{1}{N_p} \sum_{i,\,\mathbf{u}} \left\| \mathbf{P}_i(\mathbf{u}) \right\|_2
$$

Feed-forward 3DGS methods built on these models (the paper lists DA3-GS,
Mirror2-GS and others) inherit that frame and predict Gaussian scales inside
it. After RDNorm the spacing is $D / (\sqrt{f_x f_y}\, s_r)$ (Eq. 7). The
depth term is now relative, but the focal length is untouched, and the ratio
between median depth and mean radial distance depends on the shape of the
scene and where the cameras stand. Nothing pins the spacing.

The consequence the paper draws is about supervision, not geometry. Two image
patches that look the same (a stretch of plaster, a patch of grass) can need
Gaussians of different size in normalized units, depending on which scene
they came from. The rendering loss pushes the scale head toward one answer on
one scene and a different answer on another. A head that hedges ends up too
small on some scenes, which leaves holes, and too big on others, which blurs.

## GSNorm is three lines

The fix is to choose the scene's scale factor so that the quantity the head
has to predict against is the same everywhere. **GSNorm** sets the factor to
the median spacing over every valid pixel of every view (Eq. 10):

$$
s_g = \operatorname*{median}_{i,\,\mathbf{u}} \left( \frac{D_i(\mathbf{u})}{\sqrt{f_{x,i}\, f_{y,i}}} \right)
$$

then divides depths and camera translations by it. Rotations and intrinsics
stay. Because the median commutes with positive scaling, the median spacing
after the division is exactly 1. The relative variation inside the scene
(near ground versus far walls) is kept.

The release implements this in `normalize_gaussian_spacing`, in
`src/models/models/rasterization.py` (**measured**, identical in the GitHub
repository and the Space):

```python
# src/models/models/rasterization.py (zju3dv/SplatAnything, c1a8992), abridged
focal = (intrinsics[..., 0, 0] * intrinsics[..., 1, 1]).clamp_min(0).sqrt()
spacing = depth[b] / focal[b, :, None, None]
factor[b] = self.spacing_target / spacing[valid].median()
depth = depth * factor[:, None, None, None]
poses[..., :3, 3] *= factor[:, None, None]
```

One detail differs from the paper. Eq. 9 pins the median spacing to 1; the
code pins it to `spacing_target=0.0013227513`, which is 1/756 to the ten
digits shown (**measured**), and 756 is the release's default input width. A
constant factor changes nothing about the idea, but it changes how to read
every other constant in the code. The Gaussian scale clamp in the paper's
Eq. 17 is `exp(s).clamp_max(0.3)`; with the median spacing at 1/756, 0.3 is
about 227 median spacings (**reasoned**), a guard against blow-ups rather than
a working bound. Read against Eq. 9 literally, it would have been under a
third of a spacing.

Before the median, the function also reconciles the predicted depth with the
camera translations, using the median ratio of pairwise camera-centre
distances. In RGB-only mode the cameras *are* the geometry model's, so that
ratio is 1 (**reasoned** from the code path), and only the spacing factor acts.

## The inference path, shape by shape

The paper describes the model in two paragraphs and an equation. The release
is short enough to read in full, and reading it answers several questions the
paper leaves open. Here is one forward pass at the default 756 by 756 input,
with shapes from the code (**measured** unless marked).

**Preprocessing.** Each image is cropped symmetrically around its principal
point, rescaled, and cropped again to the target size, with the intrinsics
updated at each step. Sizes must be multiples of 14, the patch size, so 756
gives a 54 by 54 grid of patches. Images go in as a tensor of shape
$[B, S, 3, H, W]$ with values in 0 to 1.

**Geometry.** The frozen WorldMirror 2.0 returns, per view, a depth map
$[B, S, H, W]$, a 3 by 3 intrinsic matrix and a 4 by 4 camera-to-world pose,
in its own radial-normalized frame. Its encoder also returns a list of token
tensors that the Gaussian side reuses.

**Alignment, then GSNorm.** When you supply camera poses, the predicted depth
has to be brought into the scale of your cameras first. The release does this
with the median, over every pair of cameras $i, j$, of

$$
a = \operatorname*{median}_{i<j} \frac{\lVert \mathbf{C}^{\text{given}}_i - \mathbf{C}^{\text{given}}_j \rVert}{\lVert \mathbf{C}^{\text{pred}}_i - \mathbf{C}^{\text{pred}}_j \rVert}
$$

where $\mathbf{C}$ are camera centres; depth is multiplied by $a$. Pairs with
a zero baseline in either frame are skipped, and if none are left (one view,
or a cubemap whose faces share a centre) $a$ is 1. That guard is the code's
answer to the CDNorm failure in Table 12: it never divides by a baseline that
does not exist. The paper says the same alignment is used in training to
render against ground-truth target cameras. Then comes GSNorm, as above: one
factor $s$ per scene, depths and camera translations multiplied by it.

**Features.** The Gaussian backbone (a fine-tuned copy of the geometry
transformer) yields four token layers of 2,048 channels on the 54 by 54 grid.
`GSFeatHead` is a DPT-style decoder: four 1 by 1 projections to 256, 512,
1,024 and 1,024 channels, resized to 216, 108, 54 and 27 cells a side (a 4x
and a 2x transposed convolution, an identity, a stride-2 convolution), fused
coarse to fine by four refinement blocks at 256 channels, reduced to 128
channels and upsampled bilinearly to the full 756 by 756. A 7 by 7
convolution from the RGB image to 128 channels is added on top, so every
pixel's feature carries its own colour at full resolution, not only what the
14-pixel patches saw.

**Decoding.** `DPTDecoder` turns that $[B \cdot S, 128, H, W]$ map into 12
channels with a 3 by 3 convolution to 256, a ReLU and a 1 by 1 convolution,
eight views at a time to bound memory. The channels split 4, 3, 1, 3, 1:

- rotation, a quaternion divided by its norm;
- scale, $\min(e^{\hat{s}}, 0.3)$ per axis, in GSNorm units;
- opacity, $\sigma(\hat{o})$;
- colour, as a residual on the input pixel's own colour, stored as the
  degree-0 spherical-harmonic coefficient $(c - 0.5)/0.2821 + \Delta$;
- merge weight, $\sigma(\hat{w})$.

Centres are not predicted: each is the pixel back-projected with its
normalized depth. At 756 by 756 that is 571,536 Gaussians per view
(**reasoned**, $756^2$) before anything is filtered.

**Filtering.** With the shipped config, every scene loses the 0.5% of
Gaussians whose largest scale axis is biggest (`max_scale_pct: 99.5`, applied
as a rank cut, so it removes 0.5% of every scene whether or not anything is
wrong with them). Sky masking is off unless you pass `--sky_seg`, which runs a
separate 320 by 320 ONNX segmenter and drops sky pixels both from the GSNorm
statistic and from the output; a view it calls more than 95% sky is left
unmasked on the theory that the segmenter is wrong.

**Merging and export.** The survivors are averaged per voxel, as described
below, and written as a PLY with Gaussians under opacity 0.05 removed.

One thing in that path disagrees with the paper. The paper says "the network
predicts opacity" and that opacity "is activated using the sigmoid function"
(**reported**). The release computes that sigmoid and then never uses it. In
`merge_voxels`, the value written to the opacity field is the merge weight:
for a voxel holding Gaussians with weights $w_k$, opacity is
$\sum_k w_k^2 / \sum_k w_k$, and for a voxel with a single Gaussian that is
just its own $w$ (**measured**, the line is
`values = weights if name == 'opacities' else scene[name]`). The 0.05 export
threshold is therefore a threshold on merge weights. A code comment calls this
"the trained configuration's rule", so the checkpoint was presumably trained
with it and the head has learned to put opacity into $w$; the fifth output
channel group is dead weight at inference (**reasoned**). The rule has a
consequence worth knowing if you edit the merge: it averages opacity instead
of compositing it. Four Gaussians of opacity 0.5 in one voxel export as one
Gaussian of opacity 0.5, where rendering the four unmerged would have
accumulated about 0.94 along a ray through all of them (**reasoned**,
$1 - 0.5^4$). Swap in a compositing rule and the model's surfaces will come
out denser than it was trained for.

## How it is trained

<Figure
  src="/articles/splatanything/fig9.jpg"

/>

None of the training code is released, so this section is the paper's
account (**reported** throughout, my arithmetic marked).

- **Schedule.** 20,000 iterations on 64 NVIDIA H20 GPUs, a 500-step warm-up,
  learning rate $5 \times 10^{-6}$ for the Gaussian backbone and
  $2 \times 10^{-5}$ for the head, which is trained from scratch. The
  geometry model is frozen throughout.
- **Inputs.** 4 to 32 source views per sample, with the input resolution
  varied from 504 to 1024. That resolution range is exactly why GSNorm's
  focal-length term matters during training, not only at test time: under
  RDNorm the same scene at the two ends of that range asks for scale targets
  about two times apart (**reasoned**, $1024/504 = 2.03$).
- **Loss.** $\mathcal{L} = \lambda_{\text{MSE}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{MSE}} + \lambda_{\text{LPIPS}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{LPIPS}}$
  with both weights 1, on target views rendered from ground-truth cameras
  after the baseline alignment above. There is no depth loss, which is
  consistent with the centres being fixed to the frozen geometry.
- **Data.** 14 datasets, 22,659 scenes in all (**reasoned**, summed from
  Table 4), dominated by OmniWorld (10,464) and OmniObject3D (5,914), with
  MatrixCity contributing 8 city scenes and UnrealStereo4K 18.
- **View sampling.** Source views come from a mix of trajectory patterns
  (Figure 8 in the paper), and targets from the three modes in Figure 9. The
  ablation without this mix, sampling views at random and splitting them,
  costs 1.3% to 9.0% LPIPS depending on view count (**reasoned**, from
  Table 13).

Table 13 makes a comparison possible that the paper does not draw. At four
input views, training on Hypersim alone, 364 of the 22,659 scenes or 1.6%,
costs 2.0% LPIPS (0.2499 against 0.2450); predicting centre offsets instead
of fixing them costs 5.4%; freezing the Gaussian backbone costs 2.1%
(**reasoned**, from the reported values). Swapping GSNorm for RDNorm, in
Table 12, costs 11.9% (0.278 against 0.245). The paper calls fixed centres
"the largest and most consistent gain" among its Table 13 choices, which is
true of that table. Across both tables, the normalization is the largest
effect the paper measures, about six times the effect of 62 times more
training scenes. Those numbers come from two tables run separately, so read
the ratio as an order of magnitude.

## What the normalization does on six scenes

To see how much RDNorm actually leaves on the table, I wrote a ray caster in
numpy (numpy 2.5.3, Python 3.13, CPU, under a second) for six captures with
deliberately different geometry and cameras: an object on a ground plane
filmed from an arc at 0.5 m, the same object at double the image width, an
indoor room, a street canyon with an 80 m sky cut-off, an aerial pass at 120 m
altitude, and a four-face cubemap from one point with a 95-degree field of
view, the setting the paper's panorama appendix uses. Eight views each (four
for the cubemap), 96 by 96 rays per view with the full-resolution focal
length. For each, I computed the median spacing under RDNorm, under the two
alternatives the paper's appendix tries (CDNorm: median camera-centre
distance to the reference camera; DNorm: median depth), and under GSNorm.

<SpacingSpread />

The largest-to-smallest ratio of median spacing across the six scenes is
**3.41x** under RDNorm, **3.56x** under DNorm and **9.41x** under CDNorm
(**measured**). GSNorm is 1x by construction. The paper's Figure 4, on real
data grouped into object, indoor and outdoor scenes, reports **4.5x** for
RDNorm, from 0.30 to 1.36 relative to GSNorm (**reported**):

<Figure
  src="/articles/splatanything/fig4.png"
  alt="A line chart of median Gaussian spacing relative to GSNorm on a log axis, for three scene categories. The GSNorm line is flat at 1.0 for Object, Indoor and Outdoor. The Radial Distance Normalization line goes from 0.57 for Object up to 1.36 for Indoor and down to 0.30 for Outdoor, with error bars, and a bracket marks the gap as 4.5x variation."
  caption="Median Gaussian spacing per scene category on the paper's data. RDNorm's spread is 4.5x; my synthetic scenes give 3.41x, the same order. (paper, Figure 4)."
/>

Three things in the toy are worth naming because they are not visible in the
paper's three-category plot.

**Resolution alone moves it.** The object at 1022 px has exactly half the
RDNorm spacing of the same object at 504 px: 0.000678 against 0.001374
(**measured**). Nothing about the scene changed. The paper trains with input
resolution varying from 504 to 1024, so under RDNorm the same training scene
asks for two different scale targets depending on which resolution was drawn.
GSNorm divides the focal length out.

**Wide fields of view push it the other way.** The cubemap faces have a focal
length of 346 px at 756 px wide, against 540 px for the 70-degree room, and
the cubemap has the largest RDNorm spacing of all six (**measured**). DNorm,
which ignores focal length, has the same problem, which is the failure mode
the paper's appendix shows for a roughly 100-degree scene.

**CDNorm is undefined with one camera centre.** The cubemap's four faces
share a centre, so the median camera distance is zero. The same is true of a
single input view. That is the likeliest reading of the paper's Table 12 row
where CDNorm at one view scores DISTS 0.793 and LPIPS 1.027, three times
worse than every other variant (**reasoned**).

## What a confused scale head does

The second question is what those spreads cost. A real network sees image
content and, through its backbone, some geometric context, so it is not blind
to scale. The worst case is a head with no scale cue at all, which outputs one
Gaussian size in normalized units for every pixel of every scene. I fitted
that constant as the geometric mean of the ideal size over all six scenes
(ideal sigma 0.7 times local spacing) and measured where each scene's pixels
land against their own ideal.

<SplatCoverage />

Under RDNorm, the cubemap's Gaussians come out at **0.52** of their ideal
size, and **38%** of its pixels fall under half the ideal, where the alpha
between four neighbouring centres drops below 0.5 (**measured**). The object
at 1022 px goes the other way, at **1.78** times its ideal (**measured**).
Under GSNorm the same constant predictor lands at 0.98 of ideal at every
scene's median (**measured**), and the cubemap's gap share goes to 0.

The toy also shows the limit. GSNorm aligns the median, not the distribution.
The street canyon's spacing runs from 0.22 to 2.53 times its own median
between the 10th and 90th percentiles (**measured**): near asphalt and far
facades in one image. Under every normalization, the constant predictor
leaves 15-24% of its pixels too small and 33-38% too large (**measured**).
Removing the between-scene spread took the standard deviation of
log(predicted / ideal) over all pixels from 0.585 to 0.488 (**measured**); the
remainder is within-scene, and a scale head still has to learn it from
content. GSNorm makes that problem the same problem everywhere. It does not
solve it.

## Sky inside the median

GSNorm's statistic is a median over "valid" pixels, and at inference the
release decides validity with one test: depth above zero and a finite
spacing (**measured**). A geometry model gives sky a large, finite depth, so
unless you pass `--sky_seg`, every sky pixel is a valid sample. The training
code is not released, so I cannot say what the statistic saw in training; the
datasets in Table 4 come with depth maps in which sky is usually invalid, and
the paper's Eq. 10 is written over valid pixels only (**reasoned**). The
question is how far the default drifts from the masked version, and the toy
answers it directly.

I built a street canyon (15 to 22 m walls, a 300 m street, sky above and
beyond) filmed from 1.6 m, and an aerial pass at 120 m over a 5 km plain
with 160 box buildings, then tilted the cameras up step by step. Eight views
of 756 px at 60 degrees each, every fourth ray. For each tilt I computed the
GSNorm factor twice, once with sky masked out of the median as `--sky_seg`
does, once with it left in as the default does. The depth I give the sky does
not matter as long as it is beyond the median, which is the point of a
median.

<SkyShift />

The street, looking straight down the road, is 9.5% sky and the default
factor is off by 1.08x; tilted up 25 degrees it is 27.7% sky and off by 1.29x
(**measured**). The aerial pass is worse because its horizon carries more of
the frame: at 30 degrees down it is 4.3% sky and off by 1.04x, at 15 degrees
30.3% and 1.48x, at 10 degrees 38.2% and 2.04x, at 5 degrees 45.7% and 3.51x
(**measured**). Past half the frame the median is itself a sky value and the
whole scene's scale is whatever the geometry model decided the sky's depth
was. The curve is not smooth everywhere: the street at 10 degrees down is
only 4.6% sky but off by 1.14x, because that tilt puts the median in a sparse
stretch of the depth histogram between ground and walls, where a small
shift in rank is a large shift in value (**reasoned**, from the toy's depth
distribution).

What a 2x error does downstream is the problem GSNorm exists to remove. The
real surfaces of the 10-degree aerial pass reach the Gaussian head at 0.49
of the spacing it is used to, and the voxel merge, set at 1.36 target
spacings, now spans 2.78 of their spacings and averages away more detail
(**measured** and **reasoned** from it). The fix is one flag, and the
release's README offers it; the default leaves it off. For aerial and street
captures with a visible horizon I would turn it on, for indoor scenes it
makes no difference (**reasoned**).

## The architecture around it

<Figure
  src="/articles/splatanything/fig3.jpg"
  alt="A left-to-right pipeline. Input aerial images of a city go into a Geometry Model marked frozen, which outputs depth maps and camera poses. A small point cloud in a dashed box is labelled Radial Distance Norm. Space, Mean(Radial Distance) = 1. A Scale-Renorm. block turns it into a larger point cloud labelled Gaussian Spacing Norm. Space, Median(Gaussian Spacing) = 1. A trainable Gaussian Model, which also receives the input images directly, produces the output 3DGS city scene."
  caption="SplatAnything's pipeline: a frozen WorldMirror 2.0 predicts depth and cameras, GSNorm rescales them, and a separately fine-tuned Gaussian model predicts per-pixel attributes. (paper, Figure 3)."
/>

The rest is close to [InfiniSplat v2](/articles/infinisplat-2), with the
geometry model swapped from Depth Anything 3 to Tencent's WorldMirror 2.0
(**reported**):

- **Frozen geometry.** WorldMirror 2.0 predicts depth, intrinsics and camera
  poses. Its weights in the release are 1,263,337,852 parameters in FP32, 5.05
  GB, in 1,593 tensors, about 0.92 billion of them in the visual geometry
  transformer (**measured**, safetensors header).
- **A second backbone for Gaussians.** A copy of the geometry backbone is
  fine-tuned for appearance, and a small head trained from scratch decodes it.
  The release's decoder is two convolutions, 128 to 256 to 12 channels: 4 for
  rotation, 3 for log-scale, 1 opacity, 3 colour residual, 1 merge weight
  (**measured**, `DPTDecoder`).
- **Fixed centres.** Every Gaussian sits on its back-projected pixel. The
  appendix's ablation finds letting the head predict offsets is the single
  most damaging change, LPIPS at one view going from 0.3667 to 0.4010
  (**reported**, Table 13).
- **Colour is one RGB per Gaussian.** The head predicts a residual on the
  input pixel colour and the export writes only the degree-0 spherical
  harmonic (**measured**, `sh=((rgb - 0.5) / 0.2820… + residual_sh)`), so
  view-dependent effects are baked in.
- **Voxel merge.** Overlapping views put several Gaussians near the same
  point; the release averages them per voxel, weighted by the predicted merge
  weight. Opacity in the merged output is not the opacity channel: it is
  `sum(w * w) / sum(w)` over the merge weights, which a comment in the code
  calls the trained configuration's rule (**measured**).

The voxel merge is where GSNorm earns a second job. The voxel size is a
constant in normalized units, and in GSNorm space a constant means a fixed
multiple of the median spacing: the GitHub config's `0.002` is 1.51 median
spacings, the Space's `0.0018` is 1.36 (**reasoned**, from 1/756). I ran my
port of the merge at full resolution on the toy scenes. A single view keeps
53-78% of its Gaussians after merging at 0.0018; four overlapping views keep
24-38% (**measured**), so redundancy from extra views is what the merge
removes. The object at 504 and 1022 px keeps the same 77.7% (**measured**):
the merge is resolution-invariant in GSNorm space, where under RDNorm the same
voxel would span twice as many pixel spacings at 1022 px as at 504 px
(**reasoned**). The cubemap's four faces barely overlap, and four views keep
53.4% against 53.0% for one.

The two voxel sizes are a small loose end. The GitHub repository's
`configs/inference.yaml` says `0.002` and its comment says the checkpoint was
fine-tuned at that voxel; the Space's config says `0.0018` (**measured**). The
Space is what the post links to.

## What the merge keeps, by resolution and by view count

I ran the full merge port at full resolution on three of the new toy scenes
(an object on a ground plane, a 6 by 8 m room filmed from a circle, a street
canyon), sweeping input width from 504 to 1022 px at four views, and then
view count from 1 to 16 at 756 px with the cameras spread over the same span,
as the paper's benchmark does. For the resolution sweep I also counted what a
voxel fixed in metres would keep, sized to match at 756 px: that is what a
constant voxel in a radial-normalized space amounts to for one scene, and
roughly what a merge in RDNorm space would do.

<MergeBudget />

Three results (all **measured**):

- **The merge keeps a fixed share at any resolution.** The room keeps 73.8%
  of its Gaussians at 504 px and 73.8% at 1022 px; the object 49.4% and
  49.3%; the street 47.1% and 47.3%. The output therefore grows with pixel
  count: 4.11 times more Gaussians for the room at 1022 than at 504 px, the
  same 4.11 as the pixel ratio. A fixed-metre voxel caps that growth at 2.44
  times for the room.
- **Extra views are mostly redundant, as intended.** Going from 1 to 16 views
  in a fixed span multiplies the raw count by 16 and the merged count by 6.2
  (object), 9.2 (street) and 11.2 (room). The room grows most because its
  views look in different directions and overlap least.
- **The factor depends on which views go in.** The room keeps 54.5% with two
  views and 73.8% with four. The four-view set sees farther walls, which
  raises its median spacing, lowers the factor, and makes each fixed voxel
  hold fewer neighbours. Normalizing each of 16 room views on its own gives
  factors from 0.57 to 1.37 times the factor of all 16 together, a 2.4x
  spread; for the object and the street it is 1.02x. GSNorm normalizes the
  input set, not the scene, so the same room comes out at different scales
  depending on what was photographed.

The practical reading is about file size. Because GSNorm ties the voxel to
pixel spacing, raising the input resolution does not buy you a coarser merge
for free: at 1022 by 1022 and 32 views the release can emit tens of millions
of Gaussians before the merge removes its share (**reasoned**, $32 \times 1022^2 = 33.4$
million raw). If you need a smaller PLY, the knob is `gs_voxel_size` in the
config, at the price of running the checkpoint off the voxel it was
fine-tuned at.

## Checking the numbers

The cleanest evidence is the paper's own ablation: same architecture, same
training, only the normalization space changed.

<NormAblation />

GSNorm beats RDNorm at every input-view count in Table 12, by 3.1% to 13.4%
depending on view count and metric (**reasoned**, from the reported values).
The gap is smallest at the extremes: 4.5% DISTS at one view and 3.1% at 64
views, and largest in the 4-32 range the model was trained on. Two rows tell
more than the winner:

- **Scale calibration makes RDNorm worse.** Keeping RDNorm and rescaling the
  predicted Gaussian scales afterwards to match each scene's median spacing
  is the obvious cheap fix. It loses to plain RDNorm at every view count (LPIPS
  0.309 against 0.278 at four views, **reported**). My constant-predictor toy
  cannot tell those two apart, because for a constant output a uniform rescale
  is exactly GSNorm. The paper's result says the benefit is in what the head
  learns when its targets are consistent, not in the output's units
  (**reasoned**).
- **DNorm is no better than RDNorm.** Normalizing by median depth without the
  focal length gives 0.284 against 0.278 LPIPS at four views (**reported**),
  consistent with the toy's 3.56x spread being no smaller than RDNorm's.

Against other methods, Table 1 averages each method over 4 to 64 input
views on seven datasets with extrapolated target views. I parsed all fourteen
tables in the arXiv HTML and recomputed Table 1 from the per-view-count
Tables 5 to 9: all 182 cells (13 methods, 7 datasets, 2 metrics) match the
mean of the per-view values to the third decimal (**measured**). One detail
the caption leaves out: Table 9 has no ETH3D or DTU numbers at 64 views, so
those two columns of Table 1 are averages over 4 to 32 views, not 4 to 64. In the RGB-only
group, SplatAnything has the best number in 13 of 14 dataset-metric cells;
with poses supplied, 12 of 14 (**reasoned**, counted from the table). The
fairest comparison is Mirror2-GS, the same WorldMirror 2.0 with its own
Gaussian head in its native radial-normalized space: SplatAnything's LPIPS is lower on all seven
datasets, by 6.8% on ScanNet to 30.0% on ETH3D, 17.9% on average
(**reasoned**). That gap mixes GSNorm with a fine-tuned backbone, 14 training
datasets and a new view-sampling scheme, so only the ablation isolates the
normalization.

Three caveats from the tables themselves:

- **PSNR tells a different story in places.** ReSplat has the highest PSNR
  among posed methods at 4 to 32 views (19.03 to 19.67 against SplatAnything's
  18.39 to 19.12, **reported**, Table 10). The paper's headline metrics are
  LPIPS and DISTS, following SHARP, on the argument that PSNR rewards blur.
  That is a fair argument and also the one every method that loses on PSNR
  makes.
- **The ETH3D LPIPS column is not a typo, but it does not measure what its
  neighbours do.** Every method's ETH3D LPIPS in Table 1 is between 0.027 and
  0.154 while its ETH3D DISTS is between 0.133 and 0.445 (**reported**). My
  first guess was a column slip in Table 1. It is not: the same low values
  appear in each per-view table (SplatAnything's ETH3D LPIPS is 0.047, 0.040,
  0.037 and 0.071 at 4, 8, 16 and 32 views, **reported**), and Table 1
  reproduces from them. What does stand out is agreement between the two
  metrics. Across the 13 methods, the Spearman rank correlation between DISTS
  and LPIPS is 0.62 to 0.98 on the other six datasets and 0.04 on ETH3D
  (**measured**, from Table 1). On ETH3D the two perceptual metrics rank the
  methods essentially independently. The ratio LPIPS over DISTS has a median
  of 0.69 to 1.59 elsewhere and 0.28 on ETH3D. Whatever the cause (image
  size, the high-resolution DSLR frames, a preprocessing step in the
  evaluation), the ETH3D LPIPS column carries little ranking signal, and the
  30.0% gain above is the number I would trust least. On that column
  SplatAnything is third among pose-free methods, behind QuerySplat (0.031)
  and YoNoSplat (0.033); it is the one cell of fourteen it does not win.
- **Training on LPIPS does not explain the margin.** The model is trained
  with an LPIPS loss and then scored on LPIPS, which could flatter it against
  methods trained otherwise. DISTS is not in its loss. Against Mirror2-GS, the
  average gain is 18.5% on DISTS and 17.9% on LPIPS (**reasoned**, from Table
  1), so the held-out metric shows the same gap.
- **It is slower than its own base.** On one H20 at 448 by 896, the median
  forward pass is 411 ms at 4 views and 11,037 ms at 64, against Mirror2-GS's
  246 ms and 6,060 ms (**reported**, Table 11), about 1.7-1.8 times slower
  (**reasoned**). The second backbone is the likely cost. "Few seconds" holds
  up to about 32 views (3,991 ms).

## What is released, and under what terms

<RepoCard repo="zju3dv/SplatAnything" />

- **Code: inference only.** Every non-empty model file under `src/models/` starts with
  `# Modified for SplatAnything: inference-only release.`, and
  `rasterization.py` describes itself as decoding and voxel merging "(no
  rasterization or training)" (**measured**). There is no training loop,
  loss, rasterizer or data pipeline, so the paper's training and its
  benchmark cannot be reproduced from it. The view-sampling scheme and the
  benchmark split are described in the appendix, not shipped.
- **Weights.** The model repository holds `SplatAnything.ckpt` (11.33 GB),
  `worldmirror2.safetensors` (5.05 GB) and an optional sky-segmentation ONNX
  model (176 MB) (**measured**). The checkpoint is a PyTorch Lightning zip with
  5,107 entries, and its pickle names `optimizer_states` and `lr_schedulers`
  alongside `state_dict` (**measured**, read with `pickletools`, nothing
  executed). Part of those 11.33 GB is training state that inference never
  touches.
- **Licence, two answers.** The Hugging Face model card says `apache-2.0`
  (**measured**). The GitHub repository and the Space ship `License.txt`, the
  Tencent HunyuanWorld-Mirror Community License, which does not apply in the
  European Union, the United Kingdom or South Korea, requires a separate

- **Hardware.** The Space runs on a ZeroGPU A10G slot. The docs offer

## What I could not check

The list of things the second pass did not settle is as long as the list of
things it did.

- **Nothing here ran the model.** Every measurement above is either the
  paper's own number or my port of the release's inference code run on
  ray-cast depth. The ports are faithful line by line for the RGB-only path
  (the spacing statistic reproduces the target to ten digits, the merge uses
  the same flattened voxel key and the same weighted means), but a ray caster
  gives perfect depth, and WorldMirror 2.0 does not.
- **The training-time statistic.** Whether training computed the GSNorm
  median over sky pixels or not decides whether the default inference setting
  matches training. The code that would answer it is not released.
- **What the sky depth is.** My sky sweep only needs sky to be beyond the
  median. Past 50% sky the result depends on the depth the geometry model
  actually assigns, which needs the model.
- **The voxel key.** The merge flattens 3D voxel indices into one 64-bit
  integer. I checked whether far points could overflow it: the product of the
  grid's three side lengths passes $2^{63}$ once points sit about 2,000 to
  2,500 times the median depth from the scene, and the key then wraps. In
  400,000 points with a shell out to 10,000 times the median depth, the
  wrapped keys produced zero collisions (**measured**), so the wrap behaves
  like a hash and is harmless in practice.
- **The ablation's variance.** Tables 12 and 13 are single runs with no
  error bars. Gains of 2% (data scaling) are within what a second seed could
  move; the 12% of GSNorm over RDNorm at four views is much less likely to be.
- **The benchmark.** The view selection and evaluation split are described,
  not shipped, so no number in Tables 1 to 13 can be rerun from the release.

Where it sits against prior work: InfiniSplat v2 on this site
([InfiniSplat 2](/articles/infinisplat-2)) is the direct predecessor and
shares the per-pixel design; AnySplat, whose voxel merge this release
inherits, normalizes in the geometry model's radial space, which is the
RDNorm row of the ablation. The paper's contribution relative to both is the
normalization and the training mix around it, not a new architecture.

## Where it fits

If you build anything that predicts per-pixel Gaussians, or per-pixel
anything with a physical size (surfels, splat radii, point sizes for a
renderer), the transferable part is this paper's diagnosis, and it is
cheap to check on your own data. Compute `median(depth / sqrt(fx * fy))` per
training scene in whatever frame your model works in. If that number varies
by more than a factor of two or three across your data, and especially if it
moves with image resolution, your scale head is being asked for different
answers to the same question. Dividing by it costs one median.

What it does not fix is the spread inside a scene. A street, a corridor or
anything with a ground plane running to the horizon has an order of magnitude
of spacing within one image, and that is still the head's job. It is also no
substitute for geometry: centres are fixed to the frozen model's depth, so
whatever WorldMirror 2.0 gets wrong stays wrong. For the problem of drifting
cameras, which is a different failure from mis-sized Gaussians, see
[VoxelTTO](/articles/voxel-tto); for the rest of this autumn's feed-forward
reconstruction papers, the
[3D reconstruction roundup](/articles/3d-reconstruction-roundup).

The novelty is small. It is one statistic replaced by a better-chosen one,
the kind of fix that reads as obvious once someone has drawn Figure 2. That
is also why it will last: the next feed-forward splatting model will have to
pick a normalization, and now there is a measured reason to pick this one.
