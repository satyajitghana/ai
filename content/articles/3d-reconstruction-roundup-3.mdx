The [first roundup](/articles/3d-reconstruction-roundup) sorted papers by the
problem they attack, and the [second](/articles/3d-reconstruction-roundup-2)
by the old idea each one bolts onto a feed-forward model. This week's five were
picked for a different reason: each one arrived as a post with a project page,
and the question was how much of it a reader can check from outside. The
answer runs from "the per-sample results are in the repository, recompute the
table yourself" (Trainspotting) to "the only evidence is a public leaderboard
row" (FPV-SLAM). For each: what is new, what is released, and what is
checkable.

## XM4D: a certificate for the camera, then one for the motion

**What is new.** Dynamic scene reconstruction from one video usually ends in a
large nonconvex photometric optimisation, started from whatever the camera
estimate gives it. XM4D, from Harvard's Computational Robotics group, splits
the problem in two and makes each half a problem with a provable global
optimum. Static point tracks are lifted to 3D with a monocular depth model,
and a scaled bundle adjustment over camera poses, per-frame depth scales and
landmarks is reduced, by eliminating translations and landmarks analytically,
to a quadratically constrained quadratic program over scaled rotations. Its
semidefinite relaxation is solved with a GPU Burer–Monteiro solver and a
Riemannian staircase, which returns a certificate when the answer is globally
optimal (reported). The dynamic half represents motion with an embedded
deformation graph, a set of control nodes each with a rotation and a position
per frame, and shows that tracking, rigidity and smoothness terms reduce to a
rotation-only problem of the same shape, so the same solver certifies it. Only
then does 4D Gaussian splatting refine appearance.

The conference version, XM, did the static half and won RSS 2025's best
systems paper; the new pieces are the dynamic stage and a "uniform sphere"
constraint on the depth scales that fixes global scale without anchoring the
first frame (reported).

**What the tables show.** Table 2 of the paper averages camera error per
dataset over seven benchmarks. XM4D is best on Replica and TUM and in
translation on Sintel. It is not best everywhere: on Syn4D, NVIDIA and DyCheck
MegaSAM has the lower error (DyCheck ATE 0.005 against 0.008, NVIDIA 0.024
against 0.038), and on IMC's unordered photo collections COLMAP wins by a wide
margin (ATE 0.003 against 0.047), all reported. The paper says this plainly.
The runtime table is where it pulls ahead: the full dynamic reconstruction
takes 34.6 minutes a DyCheck scene against 40.5 for MoSca and 512.4 for Shape
of Motion, and on Replica room0 it reaches 2,000 frames in 55 minutes, where
VGGT runs out of GPU memory at 200 frames (reported). Novel-view rendering is
best on all three dynamic benchmarks, for example 19.51 mPSNR on DyCheck
against MoSca's 18.91 (reported).

The paper's own limitations paragraph is the one to read: the certificate
covers the optimisation, not its inputs. If the monocular depth or the point
tracks are wrong, the solver returns the certified optimum of the wrong
problem. Its memory also grows quadratically with the number of frames,
because the data matrices are stored densely (reported).

**What is released.** The paper says "visualizations and code are available
on the project page". The page's Code button points at
`ComputationalRobotics/XM4D-release`, which `git` could not reach without
credentials on 10 November, so it is private or not yet created (measured).
The public `ComputationalRobotics/XM4D` repository is the project page's own
HTML, viewer and assets, 294 files, last commit 9 November (measured). The
static solver from the conference paper is public under MIT:

**What is checkable, by running something small.** A certificate is the
unusual claim here, and it is easy to see on a toy. The smallest problem of
XM4D's kind is rotation synchronisation: recover twenty 3D rotations from
noisy measurements of the relative rotation between thirty pairs of them. I
wrote it in numpy (2.5.3, Python 3.13): plain Riemannian descent on the
rotations from ten random starts per problem, and separately the semidefinite
relaxation solved the way XM does, by Burer–Monteiro factorisation with a
staircase that starts at rank 3, checks the dual certificate (the smallest
eigenvalue of the certificate matrix must be non-negative), and adds a row
when it fails. Twenty random problems per noise level; noise is a rotation
with the given standard deviation in radians per axis.

<CertifyToy />

Three things come out of it (all measured, about four minutes of CPU per
run of the sweep). First, local search fails even when the problem is easy. At
0.05 radians of noise, 27 of 200 random starts stop at a cost above the
certified optimum, often far above it: the median stuck run ends at about 86
times the optimal cost. That is the kind of failure the paper's Figure 3 boxes
in orange (reasoned). As noise grows the local
solver fails more often, 59 of 200 at 0.3 and 185 of 200 at 0.6. Nothing in
the local solver's output tells you which runs those are; the certificate
does.

Second, the staircase behaves as the XM4D project page describes for its own
solver ("the rank-3 solution fails the certificate, so the solver lifts to
rank 4"). At low noise most problems certify at rank 3 straight away, 16 of 20
at 0.05; the rest climb to rank 4 or 5, and every problem at every noise level
ended certified, 140 of 140.

Third, a certificate is only a proof of the solution you want when the
relaxation is tight. Up to 0.3 radians all 20 certified solutions round back
to true rotations at exactly the certified cost, so they are provably the
global optimum. At 0.5 only 10 of 20 do, and at 0.6 only 5. In the others the
semidefinite optimum genuinely has rank 4 or 5 (I checked the eigenvalues of
the solution matrix on sixteen more problems), so the certificate certifies
the relaxation, which is a lower bound, and the rounded rotations sit a few
percent above it: 18.00 against 17.43 in one case. XM4D says its relaxation
is "empirically tight" and rounds when the rank exceeds three, which is the
same behaviour. The toy also makes the paper's limitation concrete: the
certified optimum at 0.3 radians of noise is still 21 degrees from the true
rotations on average, because the optimum of a noisy problem is not the truth.

## Trainspotting: found photos as training data

**What is new.** Feed-forward reconstruction models, VGGT and its successors,
are trained mostly on single-session captures: someone walks around an object
and the object and its surroundings stay put. Hanyu Chen and Noah Snavely at
Cornell built the other kind of multi-view data from Wikimedia Commons: photos
of the same object taken by different people over years, in different places,
sometimes of different physical copies of one design. Trainspotting is 12,068
objects and 311,886 photos, each reconstructed with a pipeline of SAM 3 gating,
RoMa v2 matching, COLMAP and CasDiffMVS depth, and sorted into three regimes:
fixed objects (a monument), roaming objects (one locomotive that moves between
sites) and instance-pooled designs (many copies of a car model). Fine-tuning
VGGT-Ω on it for 7,500 steps, about 12 hours on four B200s, gives Spotter
(reported).

**What is released.** Nearly everything. The repository holds the inference
code, the reconstruction pipeline and the evaluation protocol; the checkpoint
is on Hugging Face; the dataset is on Hugging Face under CC BY-SA 4.0. The
model code and weights inherit VGGT-Ω's FAIR Noncommercial Research License,
while the evaluation and reconstruction code is MIT (read in the repository's
README). I read the checkpoint's safetensors header with two HTTP range
requests: 1,437 tensors, 1,147,607,641 parameters, all float32, of which
908,139,552 are the aggregator, 203,589,641 the camera head and 3,547,664 the
object-mask head (measured). That matches the paper's 1.14B.

**What is checkable.** The authors ship per-sample results for every model in
their main table: 1,635 rows each, one 4-view and, where an object has enough
photos, one 16-view sample from each of 1,000 held-out objects. That makes the
headline table reproducible without a GPU. Their `tables.py` was refused by
this sandbox's permission layer as downloaded code, so I wrote my own
averaging over the same CSVs. It reproduces the README table to every printed
digit: Spotter's pose AUC@30 is 81.2 against 25.0 for VGGT-Ω and 46.4 for π3,
and AUC@5 is 44.2 against 7.2 (measured). The 1,000 evaluation objects are all
marked `eval` in the split file, and none of the 11,068 training objects is
among them (measured). Per sample, Spotter beats VGGT-Ω on AUC@5 in 93.1% of
samples and ties in 3.9%; a bootstrap over objects puts the mean AUC@5 gain at
37.0 points, 95% interval 35.6 to 38.4 (measured).

<RegimeBars />

Split by regime, the gain is not confined to the hard cases. Spotter's AUC@5
is 44.0 on fixed objects, 41.7 on roaming and 45.9 on instance-pooled, close
to flat, while VGGT-Ω collapses from 20.4 to 2.9 and 2.1 (measured). The
starting checkpoint is also the weakest of the four on pose in every regime;
on fixed objects plain π3 has 26.9 to its 20.4 (measured), which says
something about VGGT-Ω on photo collections before any fine-tuning.

Two caveats the paper states itself. The held-out ground truth is
pseudo-ground truth from the same pipeline whose output Spotter was trained
on, so part of the gain may be learning the pipeline's conventions. The
independent check is NAVI in-the-wild, where AUC@30 rises from 18.8 to 76.0,
but that is 35 samples (reported). And on conventional benchmarks the post's
"it can also improve performance for normal workloads" is stronger than the
paper's "largely unchanged". The paper bounds the change at 1.1 points of
AUC@30 and 3.2 points of δ under 1.03. It does not mention AUC@5, which in its
own appendix table drops from 91.1 to 86.1 on DTU, from 40.8 to 38.8 on
7-Scenes and from 66.9 to 65.4 on ETH3D (reasoned, from the reported table).
Geometry accuracy improves slightly on 7-Scenes and Neural RGB-D. A wash on
ordinary scenes, then, with a small cost in fine pose.

## VoxLution: generation, not reconstruction from a scan

**What is new.** The post that circulated this one described it as generating
detailed 3D spaces from noisy scans. It does not take a scan as input. VoxLution,
from Angela Dai's group at TUM, is an unconditional (or layout-conditioned)
generator of indoor scenes, trained on 3D-FRONT and on the real scans of
ScanNet++, and the noise in its real-data results is noise it learned from
those scans: its limitations section says the model "inherits the holes and
noise of the training scans" (reported). What it adds is resolution. A coarse
transformer lays out the room at 25 cm voxels; cascade transformers refine it
chunk by chunk, each stage halving the voxel, down to 1.5625 cm, and a sparse
VAE turns the occupancy into a mesh.

The tokenizer is the clever part, and it needs no training: each occupied
voxel is described by which of its eight children are occupied, eight bits,
one of 256 codes. The same 256-entry codebook means the same thing at every
level, so all four cascade stages share it, and only occupied voxels produce
tokens, so the sequence grows with the surface, not the volume (reported). Each
chunk's sequence carries the tokens of coarser levels and the boundary of
neighbours already generated, which is how adjacent chunks line up.

**What the numbers say.** On 3D-FRONT FPD falls from LT3SD's 27.31 to 11.03
and FID from 4.91 to 2.40; on ScanNet++ from 34.33 to 10.14 and 7.50 to 1.96
(reported). The abstract's percentages follow from those: 59.6% and 51.1% on
3D-FRONT, 70.5% on ScanNet++ FPD, and 73.9% on FID where the paper prints 73.8
(reasoned). On ScanNet++ only two baselines are compared, both fine-tuned by
the authors. Speed is the cost: on one H100 a room takes 22 minutes, a 4 m
square takes 16 against 8 for LT3SD and 6 for WorldGrow, and 3.5 at the
coarser 3.125 cm (reported). Training takes days per transformer on four H100s.

**What is released.** The paper and a project page; the Code button reads
"coming soon". Nothing to run.

## LucidLyra: understanding at the cost of reconstruction alone

**What is new.** NVIDIA's Lyra already turns one image into a 3D Gaussian
scene by decoding the latents of a camera-controlled video model. LucidLyra
gives each Gaussian two more things: a semantic feature aligned with language
and an instance embedding, so the scene can be queried ("select the chair") and
split into objects. The supervision comes from frozen 2D teachers run on the
video model's own generated frames, SAM and SAM 2 for object tracks and a
projected SigLIP 2 for semantics, and the decoder learns all of it in one
feed-forward pass, without captured multi-view data or 3D labels (reported).

**What the headline is measured against.** The abstract-level claim is a
31.7-point mIoU gain over "the strongest comparable baseline" on the authors'
LucidLyra-SDG benchmark: 64.1 against 32.4 for Lyra with LangSplatV2 fitted per
scene (reported). The row that matters is the one the paper calls a reference:
"Lyra + Lifted Teachers", which assigns the teachers' features directly to
Lyra's Gaussians. It scores 60.5 mIoU and 22.8 instance mTrackPR on SDG, against
LucidLyra's 64.1 and 21.5; on the ADE20K-conditioned set 50.1 against 49.8
(reported). So LucidLyra roughly matches what you get by lifting the teachers
into the scene, which is what distillation should give. The gain is time:
teacher lifting takes 64 minutes a scene on an A100 and LucidLyra 2.1 (reported),
about thirty times faster (reasoned). Rendering does not suffer, 25.02 dB
against Lyra's 24.49. Each benchmark is 25 curated scenes, with reference labels
propagated and then reviewed by people.

**What is released.** Paper and project page; "code coming soon", and "all
data and benchmarks will be released" (reported). Nothing to check beyond the
tables.

## FPV-SLAM: a leaderboard row and a blog post

**What is new.** FPV Labs, a startup building egocentric data for robot
learning, describes a sliding-window visual-inertial odometry adapted for
head-mounted video, calibration estimated per recording from the footage
itself, and a keyframe visual-inertial bundle adjustment with loop closure on
top. It runs offline, about 10 frames per second on a 16-core cloud machine
with refinement (reported). There is no paper and no code; the system is "open
for early access".

**What is checkable.** LaMAria, ETH Zurich's egocentric SLAM benchmark, keeps
its control points hidden and publishes a leaderboard, so the ranking claims
can be read off it. Everything in the post's results section matches the board
as of 10 November: 61.4 on Short against Mighty Camera's 57.1, a 12.2-point
lead in low light and 10.1 on long walks over the next mono-inertial entry,
long-walk recall within 5 m of 86.9% against 76.9% (measured). The "4.5x
ORB-SLAM3" is the mean of the five category scores, 45.2 against 10.0
(measured from the board). Two things the post does not say. Its lead is
narrow on moving platforms, 25.8 against 23.5, and on medium walks, 44.7
against 40.5. And its binocular entry, described as third, is now fourth by
Short score, behind Aria's own SLAM, SLAM Dunk and GroundedSLAM, and behind
RedComet and FlexVIO on medium, long and low-light walks (measured).

<LamariaBoard />

The post is also unusually frank about the benchmark. LaMAria aligns each walk
with one similarity transform before scoring horizontal error at sparse
control points, which forgives a uniform scale error; a robot needs metric
pose in SE(3) straight out of the tracker, on short clips with hands in the
way, which city walks do not test (reported). That is the right objection, and
it applies to their own number too.

## What to take from the week

Only one of the five can be rerun end to end by a reader, and it is the one
whose contribution is a dataset. Trainspotting's checkable claim held: the
table recomputes from the released rows, and the gain is spread across regimes
rather than concentrated where the baseline fails. The caveats sit where the
authors put them, on pseudo-ground truth and on 35 independent samples, plus
one they did not, the AUC@5 cost on conventional data. XM4D's claim of
certified optimality is the most interesting idea of the week and the easiest
to misread: it certifies the solve, not the depth and tracks fed into it, and
on three of its seven camera benchmarks MegaSAM still has lower error.
VoxLution and LucidLyra are paper-only for now, and their numbers are
consistent with themselves once you read which row they are compared against.
FPV-SLAM is a product announcement whose one checkable claim checks out.
