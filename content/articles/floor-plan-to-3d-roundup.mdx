Between 12 and 14 November four posts went past that all showed the same trick: a 2D plan goes in and a 3D building comes out. [Snaptrude](https://x.com/snaptrude/status/2120880040527134975) gave its agent "the DWGs for an 8-floor hotel and one prompt" and got "the full BIM model". [levelsio](https://x.com/levelsio/status/2121291126803980696) showed Interior AI 3D working from "just" a floor plan. [Romain Briaux](https://x.com/rbriaux/status/2121257488645554548) showed a tool where you edit the plan in 2D, see it in 3D, and layer insulation, plumbing, ventilation and electricity on top. And [dextune](https://x.com/dextune/status/2121543671011242180) posted 3d-new-era-ai, an open-source home design editor that MCP clients drive.

I build drawings-to-BIM and scan-to-BIM systems for a living (Revit driven over MCP from drawing sets, and point clouds turned into elements that compile to IFC), so "plan to 3D" makes me ask what comes out. BIM elements: a wall that knows its axis, thickness, height, storey and type, a door that knows its host wall, in a file someone else's software opens intact. A mesh: triangles you can walk through and paint, but cannot ask how thick the north wall is. Or a picture of a model, which is fine for a client meeting and useless after it.

For each tool I asked what it produces, how it gets walls, openings and storeys out of the drawing, and where it would fall over on real drawings. Only one has source code. For the other three I keep what a video frame or the author's reply shows apart from what the vendor claims.

## What a plan sheet holds, and what a wall needs

A floor plan is a horizontal cut through a building about a metre above the floor, looking down. The cut shows wall footprints (axes, thicknesses), door swings and window symbols (positions, widths), and room outlines. It shows nothing vertical. Wall heights, sills and heads, slabs and storey elevations live in sections, elevations and schedules. A wall's construction lives in a legend. Which storey a sheet is comes from its title block.

An IFC wall needs all of it. `IfcWall` has a placement and an extruded body, so it needs a height. It sits in an `IfcBuildingStorey` with an elevation. Its thickness is properly the sum of an `IfcMaterialLayerSet` on its `IfcWallType`. A door is an `IfcDoor` that fills an `IfcOpeningElement` that voids a specific wall, so the door has to know its host. Half of those fields are not on a plan at all.

So every plan-to-3D tool fills the vertical half from somewhere: a default, a setting, the person's prompt, or a model's guess. That source separates these four tools more than the 3D view does.

Every one of them reads the plan-shaped fields and gets the vertical ones elsewhere: Snaptrude from the prompt, the barn tool from per-storey settings, 3d-new-era-ai from defaults, Interior AI from its model. Nobody here reads a section.

## Three ways to get walls out of a drawing

**Read the vector entities.** A DWG, or a PDF exported from Archicad or Revit, already holds lines. A wall is two parallel lines with a hatch between them on a layer like `A-WALL`; pair them, take the midline as the axis and the spacing as the thickness. Doors are often named blocks. It still breaks: layer names are whatever the drafter chose, exploded blocks turn a door into loose arcs, and furniture on the wall layer pairs up into phantom walls.

**Trace the pixels.** A scan or flattened PDF has no entities, so you threshold it and look for thick dark bands. That works for solid-filled walls only. Hatched walls read as stripes, thin-line walls as nothing, and a door is a break in the band that the tracer must tell apart from a wall's end.

**Let a model look at it.** A vision model copes with hatching and odd symbols and guarantees no single coordinate. A wall it puts at 3.6 m may come from a dimension string, a guessed scale, or nowhere. The [image-to-DWG skill](/articles/dwg-redraw-skill) tags every value as known, scaled, inferred or unreadable, the only design here I have seen that tries.

Once you have walls, extrusion is easy. The widget extrudes one small plan two ways. In element mode doors and windows are hosted objects and the heights are defaults. In traced mode the plan goes through 3d-new-era-ai's tracing rule, covered below: a gap no wider than `max_gap` (130 cm by default) becomes solid wall, and a wider one leaves a hole from floor to top.

Drag the storey height: nothing on the plan changes, because that number is not on the drawing.

## Snaptrude Agent: DWGs in, a hotel out, and a long brief in between

[Snaptrude](https://www.snaptrude.com) is a browser-based design and BIM tool. [AEC Magazine covered the Agent's launch](https://aecmag.com/bim/snaptrude-agent-launches-for-bim-workflows/) on 29 October as an agent that takes teams "from early concept studies to construction-ready LoD 400 models". The 12 November post is the CAD case: "Turning CAD plans into BIM usually means tracing every floor again. We gave Snaptrude Agent the DWGs for an 8-floor hotel and one prompt. It asked a couple of questions, then built the full BIM model. Every element stays editable."

The post carries a 67-second video. I stepped through it.

**What the demo shows.** DWGs in, storey count and heights from the prompt, a staged plan, clarifying questions, and a coherent-looking building whose window title says BIM.

**What Snaptrude claims elsewhere.** Its [comparison page](https://snaptrude.com/vs/revit) says "Parametric BIM. Walls, slabs, columns, doors, windows, rooms with defined types and properties." and "Native IFC 2x3 and IFC 4. No plugin." Its homepage offers an example prompt, "Recreate this uploaded CAD floor plan as an editable model, including walls, doors, windows, and room boundaries."

**What I could not verify.** The video never selects an element, opens a property panel or exports a file, and the cut hides how long the build took and how the curved walls became elements. The same comparison page describes DWG import "as a trace-over underlay", and the [help article on importing CAD](https://help.snaptrude.com/en/articles/11501765-import-cad) describes a manual workflow: assign the plan to a storey, then "The Draw tools automatically snap to the CAD plan." So the documented path is a person tracing over the DWG. The agent presumably drives those same tools ("1 step · Running in a cloud browser" says the chat), but that is my reading of the UI, not something Snaptrude documents.

**Where it would break.** The help page's checklist says it: flatten the drawing, "Fully explode all blocks and groups", save as AutoCAD 2018, move it near the origin. Real hotel DWGs are xref-heavy and put the same core in slightly different places on each floor. "Keep cores aligned" is in the prompt because the agent would otherwise model what the sheets say, and whether it snaps a misaligned core or models the drafting error is what I would inspect first. The demo shows a beautiful result, not a checked one.

## Interior AI: a plan with areas and no dimensions

levelsio's Interior AI spent four years as an image-to-image restyler. On 11 November he [wrote](https://x.com/levelsio/status/2120641573671358926) that "AI can finally generate headless Blender 3d scenes from just a rough video ..., or even a few photos, and even a floorplan (which no image model was able to understand before, believe me I tried 100 times!)". By the next day it was a live feature, "Real video-to-3d with my own built headless Blender on a VPS", and when someone asked about the engine he [answered](https://x.com/levelsio/status/2120691736657735765) "This is ThreeJS, it's just generated in Blender". He does not name the model that writes the scene.

So the pipeline, in his words: a model produces a Blender scene, Blender runs headless on a server, and the result is shown and edited in a three.js viewer. The 13 November post is the floor-plan input.

levelsio lists the misses himself: "the big I guess sliding windows to outdoor garden are missing, and bath tub is just a box, and I don't see a toilet seat etc." The sliding doors are drawn as thin lines in a gap of the wall band, the hardest thing on that sheet to tell from a window or from nothing.

**What it produces.** A mesh scene of one storey whose furniture you can move, delete and recolour. No storeys, wall types or element export, and no claim of any. Fine for redecorating a room; not BIM.

**Where it would break.** More than one floor, hatched or outlined walls, and area labels that disagree with the drawing (marketing plans count wall thickness in or out by market), which a model reconciling scale against them inherits.

Sheets that disagree (an electrical plan on an older revision) arrive as clashes. For one barn and its own architect's PDFs, ~1 cm is as good as the drawings.

## 3d-new-era-ai: the one with source

[3d-new-era-ai](https://github.com/leandrodaf/3d-new-era-ai) is a home design editor written in Rust, licensed MIT or Apache-2.0, that bills itself as "A Sweet Home 3D alternative" and ships an MCP server: "Claude, ChatGPT, Codex, Gemini, Cursor or any other MCP client draws the walls, furnishes the rooms, builds the joinery, checks lighting and ergonomics against real standards and renders the photos." There is a desktop app, a browser editor and a hosted connector. I cloned commit `81711b4` (2 November 2026) and read the core model, the MCP tools that touch walls, the tracer, the mesh builder and the exporter. By my count the crates hold 131,452 lines of Rust across 199 files. I did not run it.

### The data model is element-level

Every change is a `Command` executed by a `Document`, and "The desktop UI, the MCP server and the REST API are all just clients of that rule." A click and an agent's tool call share one undo stack; batches are atomic.

A `Wall` in `newera-core/src/elements.rs` has `start`, `end`, `thickness` and `height` in centimetres, an optional arc, an optional `level`, an optional `wall_type`, finishes per side, baseboards, and `height_at_end` for gable walls. A `Level` has `elevation`, `height` (default 250 cm) and `floor_thickness` (default 12 cm). Doors and windows are furniture pieces with an `Opening` (door, window or passage, with hinge side, leaves and sliding). Rooms are polygons with a name and a use.

That is a real building model, much closer to BIM than a mesh. It falls short of IFC in a few places.

**Wall types are a name and a thickness.** `WALL_TYPES` in `materials.rs` is a list like `drywall-95`, "Drywall 95 mm", with a thickness of 9.5 cm and a Portuguese description of the stud and boards. The layers are prose, not data.

**Openings find their host at render time.** `wall_cuts` in `furniture.rs` takes every door or window and cuts it into each straight wall it is aligned with (within 5°) and sits inside (within half the wall's thickness or the piece's depth, plus a centimetre). No door-to-wall relation is stored, so an exporter would have to recompute the host, and the first filter in that function, `.filter(|(_, w)| !w.is_arc())`, means curved walls never get holes. `mesh.rs` skips arcs the same way when it splits wall faces at openings.

**Rooms are derived.** `detect_room` in `detect.rs` unions all wall footprints and returns the hole containing a point: "Holes in the union of all wall footprints are exactly the enclosed rooms." That is how an agent creates a room by naming a point.

### The tracer: dark bands, and nothing else

The MCP tool `edit_background` puts a scanned plan under the drawing at real scale (one or several known distances fit X and Y scales by least squares, in `BackgroundImage::fit_scale`) and, with `action: "trace"`, turns it into walls. The tracer in `newera-mcp/src/trace.rs` says what it is in its first line: "thick dark bands running across or down the image become wall axes with their thickness. Good for orthogonal plans with filled walls."

Step by step, with the defaults from `background.rs`:

1. **Ink mask.** A pixel is ink if its luminance is under 128 and it is not saturated, so "Humanized plans paint lawns, plants, furniture and cars in color; walls are black or gray."
2. **Bands.** Scan every row for ink runs at least `min_len` long (60 cm), and track runs that continue on the next row. Do the same down every column. A band whose stack of rows is between `t_min` and `t_max` thick (5 to 45 cm) is a wall; its middle row is the axis.
3. **Snap.** A horizontal wall's ends move onto the axis of a vertical wall they run into, and vice versa.
4. **Merge.** Collinear pieces of similar thickness join if the gap between them is at most `max_gap`, 130 cm. The comment in the tool schema: "Doors and windows up to this wide don't split a wall."
5. **Clean.** Drop short pieces that touch nothing, then keep only connected clusters whose total length is at least a fifth of the biggest one: "A house is one connected set of walls."
6. **Create.** Each traced wall becomes a `Wall` with height `h`, 250 cm by default, in one undo step.

Step 4 is right for topology (one wall, not two stubs) and it deletes the opening. The tracer never creates a door or window: openings up to 130 cm become solid wall the agent must cut again by placing a door, and wider ones leave a full-height void. The project's own test is named "colored_blobs_are_not_walls_and_openings_do_not_split_them".

I wanted to see that on a real plan, so I ported the tracer to Python (about 170 lines, following the Rust step for step, my code, not theirs) and ran it on levelsio's plan. That plan has no dimensions, so I set the scale at 1.3 cm per pixel from a door opening of about 60 pixels, assuming a 76 cm door. At the defaults the port finds 20 walls and bridges five gaps of 60, 61, 81, 92 and 92 cm. Those are door openings, now solid wall. With `max_gap` at 0 it finds 24.

What is missing teaches more. The living room's top and right walls are not traced, nor the kitchen's lower wall with the sliding doors. They are mostly glass drawn as thin lines, so the band breaks into pieces too short to keep, or into a cluster cut off from the house that step 5 drops. Those are the walls Interior AI's model missed too.

The repository's showcase suggests the authors know this. The README's 105 m² apartment was "traced from an openly licensed floor plan" of the FOCSA Building in Havana, and `docs/SHOWCASE.md` says how: the drawing has no dimensions, so the scale "came from things with known sizes (doors, toilet, cooktop): 2.4 cm per pixel", and the agent then called `create` with `px: true` and "coordinates read straight off the image (25 cm party walls, 12 cm partitions)". The agent typed the walls. It did not use the tracer. That took "About 60 MCP calls" for the whole apartment, furniture and lighting included.

### What comes out

`export` writes a PDF, SVG or PNG plan, a GLB or OBJ model, and the joinery cut list as CSV or DXF. There is no IFC anywhere in the repository; I grepped. DXF appears only for cut-list sheets. And the GLB carries none of the element model: `export.rs` writes one node named `"home"` with one mesh, its triangles grouped only by material look. The wall ids, types, storeys and hosted openings exist inside the `.newera` project and stop at the export.

**Where it would break.** Non-orthogonal plans (the tracer sees only horizontal and vertical bands), hatched or outlined walls, multi-storey sets registered by hand one image at a time, and any handoff to a BIM tool. Inside its own world (a home furnished, lit, checked against cited standards and rendered) it is the most complete open tool for an agent I have seen. It is not a drawings-to-BIM tool and does not claim to be.

## What I would use, and for what

With a real DWG set due Friday, Snaptrude is the only one that claims the output I need. I would export its IFC first and check it the way I checked [Mutiny BIM's](/articles/mutiny-bim) with IfcOpenShell: count `IfcWall` and `IfcDoor`, check each door's `IfcRelFillsElement`, compare storey elevations to the section. Its brief shows the uncomfortable part. Someone typed the storey heights, the core rule and the work order. That is a modelling brief by a person who already understood the building, not "one prompt".

The barn tool is the one I would learn from: vector reading without a model, build-ups stored as layers, opening heights taken from the drawing. Give it an IFC writer and it is a useful small-building tool.

Interior AI is for looking, not building, and its own panel says so.

3d-new-era-ai is worth reading for its command model and MCP design notes. For drawings to BIM it lacks a vector reader, stored host relations for openings, and an exporter that keeps elements as elements.

All four share the gap the [3D and BIM tools roundup](/articles/agent-3d-bim-tools) found from the scan side: the step to correct, typed, hosted elements is the thinnest, and the agent's guesses go unchecked there. The fix is not a better 3D view. It is reading the sections and schedules too, and saying for every height where it came from.
