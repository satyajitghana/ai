Moonshot launched **Kimi K3.1 Pro** at 17:15 UTC on 23 October, after a teaser earlier the same day. The
[launch post](https://x.com/Kimi_Moonshot/status/2113680701770068067) calls it "our most capable 3T-class open-weight
model": a 1M-token context, 2.8T total and 104B active parameters, multimodal, full-project game builds in Unreal,
Unity and Godot, parametric 3D in Blender and CAD, finance hallucination "down by 65%", and "matching the strongest
proprietary models across coding, agentic and knowledge benchmarks". It is live on the Kimi API now, with a
**Highspeed** variant "2–3× faster". The weights were promised **by 4 November 2026**.

When I first wrote this, on 24 October, the Hugging Face API listed one K3 repository under `moonshotai`, the July
`Kimi-K3`, and nothing for K3.1 (measured then). With no `config.json` to diff, that version checked what could be
checked: the launch blog's own benchmark table, the pricing pages, a third-party measurement, and the arithmetic of
the demo that went viral with it. Those sections stand, dated. `moonshotai/Kimi-K3.1-Pro` was created on 2 November at
04:13 UTC (measured, from the repository's initial commit), and its files now answer the architecture question
directly. Every claim is labelled **measured** (read from files or pages), **reported** (Moonshot or another party
says it) or **reasoned** (my arithmetic or inference).

If you have not read the [Kimi K3 article](/articles/kimi-k3), the short version: K3 is a 93-layer MoE that routes
each token to 16 of 896 experts, mixes Kimi Delta Attention with Gated MLA 3:1, reaches back across depth with
Attention Residuals, and was post-trained as nine RL experts merged into one model by on-policy distillation. The
experts, the KDA layers, Attention Residuals and the 1M window carry over to K3.1 Pro unchanged. The 24 Gated MLA
layers do not.

## What changed from K3

The blog's first technical sentence settles the architecture question: "Kimi K3.1 Pro is a post-trained version of
Kimi K3, with substantially larger post-training compute" (reported). On 24 October I read that as same base, same
shape, and the files say that was half right. The 2.8T / 104B, the
1,048,576-token window and the text, image and video inputs in the launch post are K3's numbers, and the API docs say
"All K3 series models support a 1,048,576-token context window." OpenRouter lists both K3.1 Pro endpoints with the
quantization tag `mxfp4`, which matches the MXFP4 expert weights K3 was trained to serve in (third-party listing).

So most of the diff is not in the weights' shape. It is in training data, RL scale, price and speed, plus one
structural change the launch blog does not mention:

the cyber row. The blog says that for the open-weight release

## What the weights show: a sparse-attention indexer

*Added 12 November.* I fetched `config.json`, `LICENSE`, the model card and `model.safetensors.index.json` from both
repositories, then read the JSON header of every one of the 96 safetensors shards in each by HTTP range request: the
first eight bytes give the header length, a second request reads the header. That is the name, dtype and shape of
every tensor without downloading a weight (measured).

`config.json` differs from K3's in six places. `model_type` goes from `kimi_k3` to `kimi_k31`, and the architecture
class from `KimiK3ForConditionalGeneration` to `KimiK31ForConditionalGeneration`. The text config gains a `dsa_config`
block. The quantization `ignore` list gains two entries, for the router gate and the latent up and down projections;
those were BF16 in K3 already, and no tensor's dtype changed. `auto_map` loses its model classes, and
`transformers_version` moves from 4.56.2 to 4.57.6. Everything else matches field for field: 93 layers, the first one
dense, 896 routed experts with 16 per token and 2 shared, `kv_lora_rank` 512, `q_lora_rank` 1,536, 96 heads, the
same 69-entry KDA layer list and the same 1,048,576 positions (measured).

The headers agree with the config:

| | Kimi K3 | Kimi K3.1 Pro |
|---|---:|---:|
| tensors | 497,220 | 497,340 |
| parameters (Hugging Face count) | 2,779,931,837,184 | 2,780,110,363,392 |
| BF16 parameters | 57,179,884,544 | 57,358,410,752 |
| MXFP4 expert parameters | 2,722,740,830,208 | 2,722,740,830,208 |
| tensor payload | 1,560,860,324,864 B | 1,561,217,377,280 B |
| shards whose sha256 matches K3's | | 0 of 96 |

All 497,220 of K3's tensors are in K3.1 Pro under the same name, at the same shape and dtype, in the same shard
(measured). None of the 96 shards has K3's hash, including the 72 whose byte size is identical, so the weights moved
everywhere, as more training would make them. The tokenizer, `tiktoken.model`, has the same sha256 as K3's.

The 120 new tensors are five per layer in 24 layers: zero-indexed layers 3, 7, 11 and so on to 91, plus 92. Those are
the layers the config lists as full attention, numbered from one: 4, 8, … 92, 93. In each (measured shapes; what
each one reads from is reasoned from its input width):

- `self_attn.indexer.wq_b`, 4,096 by 1,536: 32 heads of 128, read from the 1,536-wide query latent the MLA already
  computes.
- `self_attn.indexer.wk`, 128 by 7,168: one 128-wide key per token, straight from the residual stream.
- `self_attn.indexer.weights_proj`, 32 by 7,168: a weight per head, per token.
- `self_attn.indexer.k_norm`, a 128-wide LayerNorm weight and bias.

That is 7,438,592 parameters a layer and 178,526,208 in all, all BF16. It is exactly the gap between the two Hugging
Face counts, so nothing else was added or removed (measured). The `dsa_config` block names the same 24 layers and
sets `index_num_heads` 32, `index_head_dim` 128, `index_topk` 2,048 and `index_rope_dim` 0.

The model card says what it is: "Compared with Kimi K3, we replace the full-attention Gated MLA layers with Gated
DeepSeek Sparse Attention (Gated DSA) layers for more efficient long-context modeling" (reported). "Replace" is
stronger than the files. Every MLA tensor is still there at K3's shape, `q_a_proj` 1,536 by 7,168,
`kv_a_proj_with_mqa` 576 by 7,168, `kv_b_proj`, `o_proj` and the output gate `g_proj`. What changed is that each of
those layers gained an indexer in front of its attention (measured shapes, reasoned reading).

What the indexer does is reasoned, not read: the repository ships no modelling code. The tensor names are DeepSeek's
lightning indexer, the same set the [HY4 Preview](/articles/hy4-preview) and [Le Chonk](/articles/mistral-le-chonk)
files carry. For each new token, the indexer forms 32 small query heads, scores every earlier token's 128-wide key with a
weighted sum over those heads, and hands only the top 2,048 tokens to the real MLA attention. With `index_rope_dim` at
0 it uses no positional encoding, like K3's MLA. Below 2,048 tokens of context nothing is left out.

Why bother, in a model that is already mostly linear attention (reasoned)? K3's 69 KDA layers keep a constant-size
state, so the 24 MLA layers are the only part whose per-token attention cost grows with the context. With the
indexer, their expensive attention reads 2,048 cached entries instead of all of them: at the full 1,048,576 tokens,
512 times fewer. The indexer still scans every earlier token, but with one 128-wide key against MLA's 576-wide cache
entry. The cache grows: if the indexer key is cached at the same precision as the MLA entry, each of the 24 layers
stores 128 more values per token on top of 576, 22% more in those layers.

Two consequences for the rest of this article (reasoned). First, "post-trained version" covers more than RL:
indexer weights that K3 never had were trained after K3. DeepSeek, which introduced DSA, added it to an existing
checkpoint by continued pre-training before post-training: a short warm-up that trains only the indexer, aligned to
the dense attention's distribution, then all parameters with the sparse selection on (reported, the
[DeepSeek-V3.2 paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.02556), section 2.1.1). Moonshot says nothing about how it did it. The launch
blog, still dated 24 October when I reread it on 12 November, does not mention sparse attention and still says "The
full model weights will be released by Nov 4" (measured). The card is the only source. Second, the doubled output
speed Artificial Analysis measured is no longer purely a serving question; see the Highspeed section below.

One practical note (measured): K3's repository ships `modeling_kimi_k3.py` and `modeling_kimi_linear.py`; K3.1
Pro's ships neither, and its `auto_map` names only the config class. So `transformers` with `trust_remote_code` finds
no model class in the repository. The card recommends vLLM, SGLang and TokenSpeed instead, with recipes I did not run.

## Joint RL against K3's nine experts

The indexer is about what long context costs; no source credits it with benchmark points. So the points K3.1 Pro gains
over K3 are, on Moonshot's account, bought with post-training. That account is one paragraph, and it is about
plumbing, not a new objective. To run "large-scale joint RL across hundreds of
downstream domains" they optimized "the RL framework, long-context inference, training efficiency, sandbox
infrastructure, rollout scheduling, and a shared platform for environment quality checks", so that "each increment of
RL compute delivers stable, predictable returns" (reported).

That list reads like a list of the ways RL spending gets wasted, and it is worth taking one item at a time:

- **Long-context inference** is most of the cost. An agent's RL step is mostly generation: long trajectories against
  tools before any gradient. K3 already paused straggler trajectories and resumed them next iteration so a few long
  ones would not stall the batch.
- **Sandboxes and rollout scheduling** decide how many environments are live at once. K3's AgentENV microVMs sit
  idle up to 98% of their lifetime waiting on the model, and pausing them is what made 51 million sandboxes affordable.
- **Environment quality checks** are the quiet one. Hundreds of domains means hundreds of reward functions, and one
  that pays for the wrong behaviour poisons the shared policy. K3's GPU-kernel reward already needed a growing list of
  hacking detectors.
- **"Joint"** is the change from K3, whose RL trained nine separate experts, three domains by three effort levels,
  and merged them with Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation. Joint RL across hundreds of domains is a different
  recipe, but the blog does not say whether the expert-then-distil structure survived.

The blog gives no number for the compute: no GPU-hours, no ratio to K3's post-training, no curve of score against
RL steps behind "stable, predictable returns", and no technical report yet.

Set side by side, the two recipes show how little is on the record. K3's report names every stage: an SFT cold
start; RL that trains nine separate experts, three domains (general tasks, general agents, coding agents) crossed with
three reasoning efforts; and Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation, which folds the nine back into one model by paying
the student a dense per-token reward, the clipped log-ratio between the matching expert and itself, inside the same
RL framework (reported, from the [K3 article](/articles/kimi-k3)'s reading of the report). Within a domain, the low
and high experts come from the max one by annealing a token-budget multiplier. K3.1 Pro's sources give four facts and
no order: it starts from K3; mid-training was redone without cyber data; the RL is "joint" across "hundreds of
downstream domains" with "substantially larger post-training compute"; and, from the files, 24 layers gained an
indexer.

<RlRecipes />

What both ends share is measured. Both cards expose `reasoning_effort` with the same three values, `low`, `high` and
`max`, defaulting to `max`. So whatever replaced or extended the expert grid still yields one checkpoint with three
effort behaviours. Whether that is still the budget-multiplier curriculum, whether "joint" means one policy trained on
every domain at once instead of nine experts, and whether on-policy distillation survived, no source says. Hundreds
of domains in one RL run is a different problem from three, mostly because of the reward functions, which is the
point of the quality-check platform in Moonshot's list; beyond that list, I have nothing to add that the sources
support.

The results that can be laid side by side with K3 are all Moonshot's (reported): Kimi Code Bench 3.0 Long-horizon from
55.95 to 61.2, financial modeling up 7.2 points, financial research up 11.6, a simulated business operation at about
4.2× K3's score, and xdev-bench-hard, Moonshot's exploit-writing benchmark, from 33.3 to 81.0. One independent number
agrees on direction: Artificial Analysis's current Intelligence Index has K3 (Max) at 43.6 and K3.1 Pro at 55.1
(third-party, read from AA's model page on 24 October; that is a newer index version than the v4.1 score in the K3
article, so the two are not comparable).

## What the −65% counts

"Hallucination down by 65%" comes from one sentence in the blog: on "Kimi Hallu Bench Hard", "an internal benchmark
designed to stress-test factual accuracy, its claim-level hallucination rate fell from 23.4% for K3 to 8.3%"
(reported). That is a 64.5% relative drop (reasoned), which the post rounds to 65%. The same sentence gives the
response-level number, which is less dramatic: "the share of responses containing at least one factual error was just
42.4% of its predecessor's rate", a 57.6% drop.

A claim-level rate means a model's answer is split into individual factual claims and each claim is checked; the
footnote says the checking is done by "fact-checking agents". Two things follow. The rate depends on how the splitter
counts claims, so a model that writes more small, safe claims looks better per claim than per response, which is
why the response-level 42.4% is the more conservative figure. And the checker is an LLM agent, so the benchmark
measures agreement with Moonshot's fact-checker, not with ground truth.

The blog is careful to say the rates "do not represent overall error rates in daily use". On its own chart GPT-6
Astra is lower than K3.1 Pro on both hallucination rows, 5.5 against 8.3 on the hard set.

The more interesting finding is on the API platform. The K3.1 Pro quickstart at `platform.kimi.ai`, read on 24 October,
carries what looks like an earlier draft of the blog. Its version of the same paragraph says the rate "fell from
23.35% for K3 to 6.85%", with the error-containing share at "46.4% of its predecessor's rate", and it describes the
set differently: "de-identified conversations where users had flagged factual errors in earlier models's responses"
(measured, from the page). The same page still has unfilled slots, `[xx.x%]` where the Terminal-Bench numbers go.

Two readings follow, both reasoned. The numbers moved between drafts, from a 70.7% drop to a 64.5% drop, so the
published 65% is the more cautious of the two. And if the set really is built from prompts where users flagged errors
in *earlier* models' answers, it is selected on the predecessor's failures. A model rerun on its own worst prompts
regresses toward its average, so part of K3's 23.4% is the selection itself, and K3.1 Pro's drop overstates the
improvement on a fresh sample. The published blog no longer says how the set was built, and it is internal, so I
cannot check which description is current. The Hugging Face card, published 2 November, gives a third one: "an
internal set of challenging, de-identified prompts drawn from practical work tasks" (measured, from the card). It
does not mention flagged errors.

## Highspeed: same price for a cache hit, double for everything else

Highspeed is the variant with the least explanation. The model list calls it "the high-speed variant of Kimi K3.1
Pro — same capabilities at about 2-3× the speed". Prices: \$4.00 per million input tokens and \$20.00 output, exactly
double the standard \$2.00 and \$10.00, with cached input unchanged at \$0.20 (reported). OpenRouter lists it as a
second endpoint of the same model, tagged `moonshotai/highspeed`, with the same `mxfp4` quantization and the same
943,718-token output cap (third-party listing). No tokens-per-second figure is published for either, and Artificial
Analysis does not list Highspeed yet. The 2–3× is unverified.

How would the same weights at the same precision go faster? The usual levers, none confirmed by Moonshot (reasoned):

- **Smaller batches per replica.** Decode for a big MoE is memory-bound: each step reads the active weights and the
  KV and KDA state once, whether it serves one sequence or many. Batching amortizes that read across users, which is
  what makes tokens cheap and each user slow. Serve fewer sequences per replica, or spread one replica over more GPUs,
  and each user's step gets shorter while each GPU's throughput falls. The
  [GB300 decode article](/articles/kimi-k3-gb300-megakernel) shows the trade on K3 itself: one user on 16 GPUs at
  batch 1 is a configuration chosen for speed, not for cost.
- **Harder speculative decoding.** K3 ships an [EAGLE-3-style draft](/articles/eagle-3-speculative-decoding) built from
  its multi-token-prediction layer. More draft tokens per step raise speed for one user and waste compute on rejected
  drafts, which also costs throughput.

Both cost GPU-time per token, which fits a doubled per-token price. The unchanged cache price fits too: a cache hit
skips prefill, and the speed-up is in decode. That is consistent; it is not evidence.

The standard endpoint already moved. Artificial Analysis measures K3.1 Pro's median output speed at 77.1 tokens per
second against 38.5 for K3 (Max), and its average time per Intelligence Index task at 609 seconds against 1,174
(third-party). That is about 2× on the regular endpoint before Highspeed enters (reasoned). On 24 October I put it down
to a serving change or shorter reasoning, because the network was supposed to be the same. It is not quite: the
indexer cuts what the 24 full-attention layers read per token at long context, and Artificial Analysis's tasks are
long agent runs. Serving, shorter reasoning and sparse attention could each contribute, and the files cannot say how
much each does (reasoned).

## The Spider-Man demo, checked against list price

The demo that travelled with the launch was a [post](https://x.com/bubbubbdex/status/2113701194187059221) of a
Spider-Man city game: "Kimi K3.1 Pro built Manhattan, added web swinging between skyscrapers, then threw in street
combat. 8 hours. 3.8M tokens. Total cost: \$34.72." Three things do not add up.

**The arithmetic.** At list price, 3.8M K3.1 Pro tokens cost between \$0.76, if every token is a cache hit, and \$38.00, if
every token is output (reasoned). To land on \$34.72 with nothing cached, 3.39M of the 3.8M tokens, 89%, must be output.
On Highspeed it takes 1.22M output tokens, 32%, still with no cache hits. Neither looks like an agent session. Moonshot's
own cyber table gives the mix of a long agentic trajectory: 159.8M input and 546K output tokens per successful
xdev-bench-hard run, 0.34% output (reported). Artificial Analysis's runs of K3.1 Pro across its whole index used 5.39B
input and 148M output tokens, 2.67% output, and 94% of input was served from cache; they cost \$3,117 in total, about
\$0.56 per million tokens (third-party token counts and costs, my division). At that rate 3.8M tokens is about \$2.14, and
\$34.72 buys about 62M tokens. One reply on the post makes the same point from experience, estimating an hour of agent
work at ten to twenty million tokens. The post claims under half a million an hour.

<DemoCost />

**The clock.** The demo was posted at 18:36 UTC, 81 minutes after the 17:15 launch post (measured from the two posts'
timestamps). Eight hours of building needs access from before the announcement. Early testers exist for most launches,
so this is not impossible, but the post does not say so.

**The provenance.** The developer behind an earlier project, [spiderbench](https://github.com/xikhar/spiderbench),
posted a similar browser Spider-Man city on 27 September, made with Claude Opus 5.5 using Blender, image generation and
Three.js, and published its source and assets. He replied that the K3.1 Pro post is his project with new lighting, and
says a community note was added. I compared the two clips frame by frame at 2 fps and found no identical frames: the
repost is relit at golden hour and has no minimap (measured). That neither proves nor rules out a derived build, and I
make no claim either way. What I can say is that the post's cost and token numbers are not consistent with each other
at Moonshot's prices, so the demo is not evidence for K3.1 Pro's cost or ability. The blog's own examples, built "end to
end by K3.1 Pro", are the ones to judge it by, and none of them comes with a token count either.

## Cyber: capability removed on purpose

The cyber section is the part of the blog with the most actual numbers, and it is unusual enough to note. During
development, Moonshot saw vulnerability-research scores rise steadily with post-training scale, traced the source to
cyber content in the mid-training corpus and cyber tasks in the RL mix, and removed both for the open-weight release.

The same pipeline without the removal solves 90.5% at pass@3; the final K3.1 Pro solves 81.0%; K3 solved 33.3%
(reported). So the removal cost about 9.5 points of exploit capability on Moonshot's own benchmark, while general
coding gains still lifted it far past K3. On the public cyber rows, ExploitBench and ExploitGym, K3.1 Pro is fourth
of five, behind all three proprietary models, which is what Moonshot says it is aiming for: open weights that do not
"extend the absolute frontier of cyber capability". The tokens column doubles as a cost receipt for the previous
section: a successful run is around 160M tokens.

## The 24 October checklist, run on the weights

On 24 October I listed what the weights would settle when they landed. Here is that list, scored against the files
the way the [Le Chonk follow-up](/articles/mistral-le-chonk) scored its own preview:

| 24 October said | The files say, 12 November | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Weights by 4 November | Initial commit on 2 November, 04:13 UTC (measured) | Held |
| `config.json` matches K3's field for field | Same except `model_type` `kimi_k31`, the class name, `auto_map`, a `dsa_config` block, two `ignore` entries and the `transformers` version (measured) | Did not hold |
| Tensor names and shapes identical | K3's 497,220 identical; 120 indexer tensors added (measured) | Half held |
| Every shard's hash changes | 0 of 96 match K3's (measured) | Held |
| Licence: not named | "Kimi K3.1-Pro License" (measured) | Named; K3's terms |
| TPU megakernel and `kimi-k3-in-c` load it unchanged if the config matches | The config does not match | Not checked |

**The licence.** It is K3's licence word for word, with "Kimi K3" changed to "Kimi K3.1-Pro" in the title and in the
attribution clause (measured, diffed in full). An MIT-style grant to use, modify, fine-tune and sell, with two
conditions. A business that offers model inference or fine-tuning as a service, where the customer controls inputs,
parameters or training data, needs a separate agreement with Moonshot before any commercial use once its group's
revenue passes \$20 million over any 12 months. And a commercial product with more than 100 million monthly active
users, or more than \$20 million in monthly revenue, must show "Kimi K3.1-Pro" prominently in its interface. Internal
use, and use through Moonshot's own products or certified inference partners, are exempt from both. I am not a lawyer;
the boundary of "Model as a Service" is the line to take advice on.

**Local speed.** Highspeed did not ship, as expected: the repository holds one checkpoint, and Highspeed is a way of
serving it. The [TPU megakernel](/articles/kimi-k3-tpu-megakernel) and [kimi-k3-in-c](/articles/kimi-k3-in-c) were
written for K3's dense MLA layers, so the condition I gave was false. To run K3.1 Pro they need an indexer path, or they
attend densely and ignore it, which for contexts past 2,048 tokens is not the attention the card describes (reasoned).
I have not checked whether either project has added one.

## The take

K3.1 Pro is the clearest case yet of a lab shipping a new flagship with almost no new network. The experts, the KDA
layers, the 1M window and every one of K3's tensor shapes carry over. The one structural change, a sparse-attention
indexer in the 24 full-attention layers, is in the model card and the files and not in the launch blog. Around it: an
implied mid-training rerun with cyber data removed, and a large, unquantified increase in RL. What that buys is
real but narrow: K3.1 Pro leads on agentic, tool-using work and finance-flavoured document tasks, nearly ties Opus 5.5 on
Terminal-Bench 4.0, and still trails the proprietary models on closed-book knowledge, harder vision sets, Program Bench
and, by design, cyber. It costs a third less than K3 and the standard endpoint runs twice as fast.

The −65% is a real number from an internal stress test, measured per claim by an LLM fact-checker, and the per-response
figure is 58%. An earlier draft of it, still live in the API docs, said 71% and described a set selected on earlier
models' failures. And the demo is not a measurement of anything: its tokens and its price cannot both be right at
Moonshot's own rates.
