Voxel-SLAM is what the HKU MaRS lab, the group behind FAST-LIO2, built when it wanted a map
rather than an odometry. FAST-LIO2 registers each LiDAR sweep once, against a map that already
contains every earlier mistake, and moves on. Voxel-SLAM keeps a filter of the same kind for that
first registration and then goes back over the data three more times: a bundle adjustment over
the last ten sweeps, loop closure against every place it has been, and a bundle adjustment over
the whole session when the data ends. The paper (Liu et al.,
[arXiv 2410.08935](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.08935)) reports the most accurate results of any
method it compares on 30 sequences, and the code is at
[hku-mars/Voxel-SLAM](https://github.com/hku-mars/Voxel-SLAM). The site owner's verdict before I
started was that it "apparently is really good". That is the claim this article tests.

<RepoCard repo="hku-mars/Voxel-SLAM" />

The first half is the mechanism: why a bundle adjustment on planes can correct a scan that
point-to-plane registration has already got wrong, what makes it cheap enough to run on every
sweep, and how each of the system's four layers of data association is solved. The second half
is a measurement. Upstream is a ROS 1 Noetic package; I built its C++ core with no ROS at all,
drove it from a small replay driver, and ran it on the four sequences of the site's LiDAR
shootout, two of which are the ones the
[FAST-LIO2 article](/articles/fast-lio2-lidar-inertial-odometry) used, with the same alignment
and metrics. Each run was scored three ways: the odometry alone, the odometry with the local
bundle adjustment, and the full system with loop closure and global BA.

It held up. Full SLAM tracked a Hilti basement to 3.15 cm, a 283 m Oxford walk to 3.20 cm and two
Sheldonian stairwells to 8.21 cm, untuned, with no resets; on the stairs a close cousin on the same
plane map, [PV-LIO](/articles/pv-lio), lost 1.73 m. Most of that accuracy came from the loop closure
and the end-of-session global BA, not the sliding window, and it costs about five times FAST-LIO2's
time per scan and gigabytes of memory.

On the 2.4 km Newer College park loop it landed at 24.2 cm, level with the best of the other
systems on that sequence, and there the loop closure did nothing: 916 accepted loop edges, and no optimisation.

## Four ways to reuse a measurement

The paper frames the whole system around one idea borrowed from ORB-SLAM3: a SLAM system is better
than an odometry to the extent that it associates a measurement with more than the map of the
moment. It names four kinds of association, and Voxel-SLAM has one module for each.

| association | what it links | Voxel-SLAM module | how it is solved |
|---|---|---|---|
| short-term | the newest scan to the current map | odometry | iterated EKF, point-to-plane on the voxel map's planes |
| mid-term | the last 10 scans to each other and to the map | local mapping | LiDAR-inertial bundle adjustment over a sliding window |
| long-term | the current place to every earlier visit | loop closure, global mapping | BTC place recognition, pose graph (GTSAM iSAM2), hierarchical BA |
| multi-map | one session to an earlier one | the same two | the same, across sessions |

FAST-LIO2 does the first row and nothing else. Every scan is registered once, against a map that
already contains every earlier mistake, and then the scan becomes part of the map forever. That is
fine while the map is good. When a scan is registered badly (a stairwell, a long corridor, a moment
of fast rotation) the error is written into the map, and every later scan inherits it by being
registered against it.

Voxel-SLAM keeps FAST-LIO2's filter for the first row, nearly unchanged, and spends its effort on
the other three. It runs three threads: odometry and local mapping in one, loop closure in a second,
global mapping in a third.

## One map for every module: the adaptive voxel

Everything in Voxel-SLAM is a plane, and planes live in one structure. Space is cut into root voxels
of side $L_r$, kept in a hash table. A voxel's points are tested for flatness through the
eigenvalues $\lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 \le \lambda_3$ of their covariance; a flat voxel is a plane, and
one that is not flat splits into eight children and tries again, down to `max_layer`. The code's
test (`plane_judge` in `voxel_map.hpp`) is

```cpp
return (eig_values[0] < min_eigen_value && (eig_values[0]/eig_values[2])<plane_eigen_value_thre[layer]);
```

With the shipped Hilti config (`hesai.yaml`) that is $L_r = 1$ m, `max_layer: 2` (so leaves of 1 m,
0.5 m and 0.25 m), `min_eigen_value: 0.0025` (the points' spread across the plane under 5 cm RMS)
and a ratio threshold of 1, which switches the second test off. The scans are downsampled to a
0.1 m grid before any of this (`down_size`). The paper's Section X gives the rule it used for all
its experiments: $L_r$ = 1 m and $L_d$ = 0.1 m indoors, 2 m and 0.25 m outdoors, 4 m and 0.5 m for a
drone 100 m up.

Odometry matches each new point to the plane in its voxel with the highest probability, using each
point's own range-and-bearing covariance (the VoxelMap recipe). Local mapping runs its bundle
adjustment on the same leaves. Loop closure builds its BTC descriptors from planes found the same
way, and global mapping's hierarchical BA does too. One structure, four uses.

## Why a plane BA beats point-to-plane

Point-to-plane registration, FAST-LIO2's measurement, is a cost on one pose. A point $p$ seen from
pose $T$ should land on a plane $(n, q)$ that the map already holds:

$$
r = n^\top (T p - q).
$$

The plane is a constant. If it was fitted to points that were placed wrongly, the new scan is pulled
to the wrong place, and the error is now in two scans instead of one.

Bundle adjustment makes the plane depend on every scan that sees it. Take all the points that fall
in one leaf, from every scan in the window, placed by their current poses: $p_k = T_{i(k)} p'_k$.
The best plane through them, and the sum of squared distances to it, have a closed form. The plane's
centre is the mean $\bar p$; its normal is the eigenvector of the covariance

$$
C = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{N} (p_k - \bar p)(p_k - \bar p)^\top
$$

with the smallest eigenvalue; and the residual is

$$
\min_{n, q} \sum_{k=1}^{N} \bigl(n^\top (p_k - q)\bigr)^2 = N \, \lambda_{\min}(C).
$$

So the plane drops out of the problem. What is left is a cost on the poses alone, one eigenvalue per
plane, and the paper's equation 2 adds IMU preintegration residuals between consecutive states:

$$
\min_{\mathcal{X}} \; \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{N-1} \bigl\| r_{i,i+1}(\mathcal{X}) \bigr\|^2_{\Sigma_{i,i+1}^{-1}} \; + \; \sum_{j=1}^{M} \lambda_j^{\min}(\mathcal{X}),
\qquad \mathcal{X} = [x_1, \dots, x_N, g].
$$

This is cheap enough to run every scan for a pair of reasons. One is that the eigenvalue is smooth in
the poses, with an analytic gradient and Hessian (BALM2 derives them), so a second-order solver
converges in a few iterations. The other is the point cluster. $C$ needs only $N$, $\sum p$ and
$\sum p p^\top$, and for one scan's points transformed by $T = (R, t)$ those transform in closed
form:

$$
\sum p = R \sum p' + N_i t, \qquad
\sum p p^\top = R \Bigl(\sum p' p'^\top\Bigr) R^\top + R \Bigl(\sum p'\Bigr) t^\top + t \Bigl(\sum p'\Bigr)^\top R^\top + N_i \, t t^\top.
$$

Each scan contributes a 3-vector, a symmetric 3x3 and a count per leaf, however many points it put
there. The optimiser never touches a point.

<PlaneBA />

The widget makes the difference concrete in 2D. Scan 2 has been registered with a 1.2 degree, 6 cm
error that is now part of the map. Scan-to-map registration fixes scan 3 against that map and puts
it on a wall that is not there; the window BA moves scans 2 and 3 together against the anchor and
the wall comes back to the 2 cm of the noise. The cost is printed twice, from the 180 points and
from the three clusters, and the numbers are the same.

This is the core claim of the paper, and the place where Voxel-SLAM differs from every LIO in the
[FAST-LIO2 article](/articles/fast-lio2-lidar-inertial-odometry). FAST-LIO2's iterated update is
Gauss-Newton on one pose against a frozen map. Voxel-SLAM's local BA is Levenberg-Marquardt on ten
poses, their velocities and biases, against planes it re-solves at every iteration.

## The sliding window, and the points that stop moving

Local mapping keeps the last `win_size: 10` scans as variables. When a new scan has been through
the odometry, its points go into the voxel map, every voxel that received points is re-voxelised,
and in each leaf the points are split into one point cluster per scan in the window plus one cluster
for everything else. That last one, the "fix" cluster, is made of points from scans that have left
the window and from old keyframes reloaded because the sensor is back near them. It enters the BA as
a plane constraint with an identity pose: it anchors the window to the world without adding
variables.

After the optimisation the oldest scan is marginalised in the simplest possible way: its points
are added to the fix clusters of their leaves, and its pose is handed to the loop-closure thread.
That is the pose I call the local-BA pose below. It is the odometry estimate after ten scans of
hindsight, and it is what the loop closure, the keyframes and the map are built from.

The window also carries gravity. At initialisation, from the first second of data, the BA includes
the gravity vector as a free 3-vector (its length too, since LiDAR gives metric scale), which is
how the system starts while moving: it needs no stationary period. After that, gravity is held
fixed in the window.

The divergence check sits between odometry and local mapping. After each registration the planes
used are tested for constraint in three independent directions; one degenerate scan is tolerated,
`degrade_bound: 100` of them in a row reset the system, which saves the current session and
starts a new one at a far-away origin. A later loop closure can join the two sessions.

## Loops, and what happens to the window when one closes

Every 10 marginalised scans form a frame. The frame becomes a keyframe if it moved more than a
threshold (the code uses 5 degrees or 0.1 m), and the keyframe's points go to BTC, the lab's
binary-triangle descriptor, which proposes a matching keyframe from this session or an earlier one
with a 6-DoF transform. Before the match is believed, two more checks:

- the ICP between the two keyframes has to be constrained in three directions (`icp_eigval`);
- the implied drift has to be small compared with the distance travelled since the matched
  keyframe: `ratio_drift: 0.01` in the config, 1%.

The second check is a nice piece of engineering judgement. A place recogniser's false positives
are usually far off; a system that drifts 1% of distance does not suddenly need a 10 m correction.
For the first match into an earlier session there is no travelled distance to compare against, and
the paper says the other checks are made stricter.

A loop that passes adds an edge to a pose graph of all local-BA poses, and iSAM2 solves it. Then the
system has a problem FAST-LIO2-with-loop-closure systems often ignore: the odometry thread has
moved on. Scans have been marginalised since the graph was built, and ten more are in the window.

The answer is to apply the last graph node's correction to every scan beyond it, rebuild the local
voxel map from the corrected window and the last five keyframes, and hand that map to the odometry.
In the code, `loop_update()` runs at the top of the odometry loop whenever the loop thread raises
`loop_detect`. To keep this from happening constantly, the optimisation only runs if the drift the
loop reveals is over 0.10 m and at least `curr_halt: 10` keyframes have passed since the last one.

## Global mapping: BA at three levels, once more at the end

The pose graph only knows relative poses. Two scans of the same wall can be consistent with every
edge in the graph and still disagree about where the wall is. The global mapping thread fixes that
with HBA, the lab's hierarchical bundle adjustment:

1. As keyframes arrive, a window of 10 keyframes, sliding by 5, is bundle-adjusted with its first
   keyframe held at the pose-graph result, and merged into a submap.
2. When the session ends, a BA over all submaps (coarse-to-fine voxels, no IMU, no gravity).
3. A top-down pose graph pushes the submap corrections back to every scan.

The data pyramid in Figure 1 is this: 10 scans per keyframe, 10 keyframes per submap, BA at every
level. "Session end" means what it says. In the upstream code it is a ROS parameter,
`rosparam set finish true`, typed by hand after the bag stops. My driver sets it when the data runs
out.

The paper's Table II gives the four stages on 13 Hilti sequences. On `exp09` ("hilti05", the cupola,
narrow staircases) the ATE goes from 55 cm for FAST-LIO2 to 23 cm for Voxel-SLAM's odometry alone,
15.9 cm with local mapping, 14.2 cm with loop closure, 13.8 cm with global mapping. On `exp15`
("hilti07") the same columns read 72, 15.7, 9.8, 9.2 and 7.8 cm. Most of the gain over FAST-LIO2 is
in the first two steps, before any loop is closed. That is the claim I wanted to test.

## Building it without ROS

Upstream is a ROS 1 Noetic catkin package: one executable, `voxelslam`, about 8,300 lines across
`voxelslam.cpp`, `voxel_map.hpp`, `BTC.cpp` and seven more files, with GTSAM for the pose graphs.
The rule for this run was no ROS in any form, not even a RoboStack prefix. That turned out to be a
small job, because the code uses very little of ROS:

- `ros::NodeHandle::param` for its configuration (45 calls), `subscribe` for one IMU and one
  point-cloud topic, `ros::spinOnce()` at the top of the odometry loop, and `ros::Time::now()` as a
  wall clock for its own timers;
- `sensor_msgs::Imu` and `PointCloud2` read through `pcl::fromROSMsg`, plus a Livox message type
  for a sensor I do not use;
- publishers and a `tf` broadcaster that only feed RViz.

So the port is a directory of stand-in headers at the paths the code includes (`ros/ros.h`,
`sensor_msgs/Imu.h`, `pcl_conversions/pcl_conversions.h`, `tf/transform_broadcaster.h`, …):
plain structs with the fields the code reads, a `NodeHandle` whose `param` reads a key-value file
written from the repository's own YAML, publishers that do nothing, and a `spinOnce` that calls my
feeder. `pcl::fromROSMsg` becomes PCL's own `fromPCLPointCloud2`. The upstream files are compiled
as they are, with eleven edits applied by a script that checks each one matches exactly once:

- nine instrumentation hooks: the include, per-scan timing and pose, the local-BA pose and the
  points of each scan as it leaves the window, a line on each degeneracy reset, counters on
  accepted loop edges and optimisations, and the pose-graph and global-BA poses captured just
  before the loop thread deletes them;
- a new `int gid` field on `ScanPose`, so the final poses can be joined to the scans;
- upstream's `main()` renamed, with my driver appended;
- and, outside those eleven, one substitution that touches scheduling. Upstream waits with `sleep(0.001)`, `sleep(0.01)` and
  `sleep(0.1)`, which is `unistd`'s `sleep(unsigned int)`: the argument truncates to zero and all
  three threads spin flat out when idle. I replaced them with `usleep` of the intended length, so a
  timing pass pinned to four cores measures the estimator rather than three spinning threads.

The driver reads the shootout's converted sequence (`imu.csv`, `scans.csv` and one binary file of
points per sweep, with each point's time since sweep start), hands every message to Voxel-SLAM's
own `imu_handler` and `pcl_handler` in timestamp order, and runs upstream's three threads unchanged.
Points are handed over as x, y, z and a float time, which is the layout upstream's Velodyne reader
takes, so the config's `lidar_type` is set to that reader; the deskew, downsampling and blind-radius
logic after it is the same for every sensor. The feed is back-pressured: the next scan goes in only
when the previous one has been consumed and the loop-closure queue holds no more than one window of
scans, which is the state the system is in when it keeps up with a live sensor. When the data runs
out, the driver raises `finish`, the parameter upstream expects someone to set by hand, and global
mapping runs.

The dependencies come from conda-forge inside the job directory: PCL 1.15, GTSAM 4.3, Eigen 5, TBB
and a gcc 15 toolchain. Upstream targets PCL 1.10, GTSAM 4.0.3 and Eigen 3.3.7 on Ubuntu 20.04; it
compiled against the newer ones with no source change beyond the edits above.
## How I scored it

Four sequences, all with a reference trajectory made without any of the systems under test,
converted once by the shootout harness and read by every system the same way:

| sequence | rig | length | what makes it hard |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hilti 2022 `exp14_basement_2` | Hesai PandarXT-32, 400 Hz IMU | 38 m, 74 s | a basement whose walls are a metre away |
| Oxford Spires `keble-college-03` | Hesai QT64, 400 Hz IMU | 283 m, 286 s | a walk round a college quad, one big loop |
| Hilti 2022 `exp16_attic_to_upper_gallery_2` | Hesai PandarXT-32, 400 Hz IMU | 117 m, 200 s | two narrow stairwells, about 8 m down and 8 m up |
| Newer College 2021 `park` | Ouster OS0-128, 200 Hz IMU | 2.4 km, 26 min | a long loop through a park, start and end 0.4 m apart |

The first two are the sequences the [FAST-LIO2 article](/articles/fast-lio2-lidar-inertial-odometry)
used. `exp16` is one of the three Hilti 2022 sequences with a dense public reference (the
[Hilti datasets article](/articles/hilti-slam-dataset) explains how it is made), and the only one
of them with stairs, which is where the paper says Voxel-SLAM gains most. The park is the long
loop where closing it should matter.

Every run went through one scoring script: each IMU pose moved into the reference's body frame,
the reference interpolated at the estimate's timestamps, one SE(3) Umeyama fit without scale, ATE
as the RMS position error after that fit, RPE over every metre of path with evo. A run is
"diverged" if any error passes the larger of 10 m and a quarter of the path, or if its poses cover
less than 80% of the reference. The machine is a 28-core Blackwell workstation, CPU only.

Voxel-SLAM ran three ways, each from the repository's own config for the sensor (`hesai.yaml`,
the authors' Hilti config, for both Hesai rigs; `ouster.yaml` for the Ouster):

- **full SLAM** (`slam`): everything on. The poses are the final ones, after loop closure and the
  global BA that runs when the data ends.
- **odometry with local BA** (`odom`): loop acceptance switched off (`Loop/jud_default: 99`, above
  any BTC score; there is no other switch). The pose of each scan is the one it had when it left
  the sliding window.
- **odometry alone** (`ekf`): the same run as `odom`, scored on the pose the EKF produced for each
  scan before local BA touched it.

The last of these is not quite the paper's "Odom" row. In the paper that ablation switches local
mapping off; here local mapping runs, and the EKF registers each new scan against planes that the
local BA has already refined. What the `ekf` row isolates is the pose before the window revisits
it, not an odometry with no BA anywhere. Disabling local mapping would have meant editing the
algorithm, which I did not do.

Timing comes from a separate serial pass, one run at a time, pinned to four cores (`taskset -c
0-3`), with the local BA's `thread_num` set to 4 (the config ships 5). The number is wall time per
scan inside the odometry-and-local-mapping thread: from the moment a scan and its IMU are synced to
the end of its local-BA and marginalisation step. The loop-closure and global-mapping threads run
beside it and are not in that number; global BA after the session is reported separately. Peak
memory is the process's high-water mark.

The maps are built the same way for every variant, so they differ only by poses: each scan's
deskewed, 0.1 m-downsampled points (0.4 m on the Ouster), as Voxel-SLAM kept them, placed by that
variant's poses, then moved into the reference frame by the trajectory's own alignment and cut to a
3 cm grid. Sharpness is the harness's wall test: the median RMS point-to-plane distance over 1 m
patches that are planar. A clean map scores about 5 to 10 mm on it; a doubled wall scores tens.

## What it did on four walks

Every run finished, none reset, and every one is `ok` under the harness's rules. ATE is after one
SE(3) alignment, without scale, against each dataset's reference. Every row below ran on the same
machine through the same harness; the whole field is in [the LIO shootout](/articles/lio-shootout).

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Voxel-SLAM, odometry alone (`ekf`) | 3.38 cm | 6.79 cm | 9.62 cm | 24.8 cm |
| Voxel-SLAM, odometry + local BA (`odom`) | 3.42 cm | 6.59 cm | 9.63 cm | 24.7 cm |
| Voxel-SLAM, full SLAM (`slam`) | **3.15 cm** | **3.20 cm** | **8.21 cm** | **24.2 cm** |
| PV-LIO, its `hesai.yaml` | 3.95 cm | 10.9 cm | 1.73 m | |

| Super-LIO, shipped config | 15.2 cm | 8.63 cm | diverged | 26.3 cm |

The FAST-LIO2 article's earlier runs, on another machine with a ROS 2 harness, gave FAST-LIO2 50.1
cm and 7.2 cm and Super-LIO 9.3 and 8.0 cm on exp14 and Spires.

On the three short sequences the full system is the most accurate thing the site has run. On the Oxford walk it
halves the odometry's error, from 6.59 to 3.20 cm, and the end-point drift (the last pose's error
when the trajectory is aligned on its first 10 s only) falls from 29.4 cm to 7 mm. That is what a
loop closure is for: the walk returns to its start, BTC proposed 45 candidates over the walk, 29
passed the checks and were added as edges, and the pose graph was optimised once, at ScanPose
2,731 of 2,857, when the drift those edges revealed finally crossed the 0.10 m bar.

On the two Hilti sequences no loop was ever accepted (exp14 produced no BTC candidate at all, exp16
two that failed the checks), and full SLAM is still better: 3.42 to 3.15 cm in the basement, 9.63
to 8.21 cm through the stairwells. That gain is the global BA alone, which in the timing pass took 0.16 s at the end of
exp14, 0.88 s at the end of exp16 and 4.4 s at the end of Spires. The paper claims exactly this:
"Even in cases where no loop retrieval is detected... the global mapping can still improve the
accuracy." It holds here.

The comparison that matters most is exp16, the Sheldonian stairwells. PV-LIO runs the same kind of
map (VoxelMap's probabilistic planes) under the same kind of filter, and with its shipped Hilti
config it drifts to 1.73 m there; the [PV-LIO article](/articles/pv-lio) traces that to one event on
the climb back to the attic. Voxel-SLAM's odometry, on planes the local BA keeps refining, holds
the same stretch to 9.6 cm.

PV-LIO's Hilti config is the outlier, and its generic config reaches 4.71 cm, still behind
Voxel-SLAM's 3.20.

Against FAST-LIO2 on the same machine, the odometry is no better. On Spires FAST-LIO2 with an
indoor-sized map tracks to 6.23 cm, against 6.59 cm for Voxel-SLAM with local BA; Voxel-SLAM pulls
ahead only once the loop closes (3.20 cm, against the Spires paper's own 0.11 m in its Table 3 for
Fast-LIO-SLAM on this sequence). Indoors it is clearer: 3.42 against 11.5 cm in the basement, and on
exp16 FAST-LIO2 and Super-LIO both diverged where Voxel-SLAM held to 9.6 cm. Voxel-SLAM's shipped Hilti
config uses 1 m root voxels split down to 0.25 m planes on a 0.1 m-downsampled scan, which is the
setting a room needs.

### What does not show up

The local BA does not move the ATE. `ekf` and `odom` are within 0.2 cm of each other on all three
sequences (3.38 and 3.42, 6.79 and 6.59, 9.62 and 9.63). That looks like it contradicts the paper's
Table II, where local mapping takes the cupola sequence from 23 to 15.9 cm. It does not, quite: as
the method section says, the `ekf` pose here is registered against planes the local BA has already
corrected, so the window's work reaches the online pose through the map one scan later. What these
runs say is that on these sequences the refinement of the newest ten poses adds little on top of
that. They do not say what Voxel-SLAM's odometry would do without local mapping at all.

The maps do not separate the variants either. The harness's wall test gives 26.8 mm for full SLAM
against 27.1 mm for odometry in the basement, 36.9 against 37.9 mm on Spires, 41.9 against 42.1 mm
on exp16, all well above the 5 to 10 mm a clean map scores on it. The points behind those numbers
are the ones Voxel-SLAM itself kept: one per 0.1 m voxel per scan, accumulated over hundreds of
scans, which spreads a wall into a slab a few centimetres thick before any pose error enters. A few
centimetres of ATE difference is invisible at that resolution, so the viewer below shows the
full-SLAM map alone. Floors score better than walls everywhere but the basement, 5.0 mm on exp16 at best.

### What it costs

| per scan, 4 pinned cores | Hilti exp14 | Oxford Spires | Hilti exp16 |
|---|---|---|---|
| mean / p95 / max, full SLAM | 11.2 / 20.8 / 47.1 ms | 55.6 / 79.8 / 332.7 ms | 21.3 / 53.1 / 83.3 ms |
| peak memory, full SLAM | 378 MB | 4,263 MB | 1,176 MB |
| global BA after the session | 0.16 s | 4.4 s | 0.88 s |

These are from the serial timing pass: one run at a time, four cores, the local BA on four threads.
The time per scan is the odometry-and-local-mapping thread only; loop closure and keyframe BA run
in their own threads beside it. On the Hilti sequences the system has room to spare (the sweeps
come every 100 ms). On Spires it averages 55.6 ms and stays under 100 ms to the 95th percentile,
with the slowest scan at 332.7 ms. The paper's Table VI reports 8 ms of odometry and 13 ms of local
mapping per scan on the Hilti sequences on an i7-10750H, which is in line with 11.2 and 21.3 ms
here.

Against FAST-LIO2 the bill is large. On Spires, on the same four pinned cores, FAST-LIO2 took 10.4 ms
a scan and 320 MB; Voxel-SLAM takes about five times the time and thirteen times the memory. Most of
the memory is long-term data association by design: every scan's points are kept, as keyframes for
loop closure and as submaps for global BA. The paper's own memory column for Hilti is 1.068 GB on
average.

The shipped Hilti config is also the wrong one for a college quad, by the paper's own rule (1 m
voxels and 0.1 m downsampling indoors, 2 m and 0.25 m outdoors). I ran that outdoor setting on
Spires as a separate, labelled row: full SLAM 3.02 cm, odometry 6.29 cm, at 34.1 ms a scan and
2,184 MB. Those timings came from the parallel pass, with other runs on the machine, so they are not
directly comparable with the serial numbers above; the accuracy is. Going the other way in the
basement, a 0.5 m root voxel (the low end of `hesai.yaml`'s own "indoor: 0.5-1" comment) gave 2.95
cm for full SLAM and 3.40 cm for odometry.

### The park: 916 loop edges, no optimisation

The Newer College park is the 2.4 km, 26-minute loop where loop closure should matter most, and it
barely moved: 24.8 cm for the odometry alone, 24.2 cm for full SLAM. The logs say why. BTC proposed
1,107 loop candidates and 916 passed the checks and were added as edges, but the pose graph was
never optimised. Voxel-SLAM only solves the graph when a loop reveals more than 0.10 m of drift, and
on this walk none did: the odometry came back round the park with 4.5 cm of end-point drift. The
edges sat in the graph unused, and at the end of the session the code rebuilds the graph for global
BA without them. On the park the system ran 24.8 ms a scan (parallel pass) and peaked at 8.5 GB.

So why 24 cm, when the drift is 5 cm? Not timing: shifting the estimate by 50 ms either way makes
the ATE worse, so there is no clock offset. Not scale: a similarity alignment only takes it to 22.7
cm. Not the reference frame: the reference is in the rig's `base` frame, about 7.6 cm from the IMU,
which the harness applies, and the whole lever arm is smaller than the error. Most of the error is vertical (18.9 cm RMS, against 8.1 and 12.8 cm in x and y), and it
varies slowly along the path, 17 cm of it on a 30-second scale, the same in all three variants. The
other systems that track the park land in the same place too: FAST-LIO2 28.7 cm, Super-LIO 26.3 cm,
α-LiDAR 27.1 cm (RKO-LIO reached 71.5 cm and KISS-ICP, LiDAR alone, 1.72 m). A
floor of about 25 cm shared by four different estimators points at the vertical axis of this
sequence, the reference or the sensor, more than at any of them. I have not found which, and I would
not read the park row as a ranking.

## Is it really good?

Yes. On three public sequences with independent ground truth, Voxel-SLAM's full system is the most
accurate thing this site has measured, and on the fourth it is level with the rest of the field: 3.15 cm in a basement, 3.20 cm over 283 m outdoors with one
loop closed, 8.21 cm down and up two stairwells, with no tuning and no resets. Where a cousin on the
same plane map (PV-LIO) loses 1.73 m on the stairs, it does not. That is the paper's central claim,
and it held.

What I would qualify is where the accuracy comes from. On these walks the windowed BA hardly moves
the error by itself; what moves it is loop closure when there is a loop, and the global BA at the
end even when there is none. Both are batch steps that only finish when the session does. If you
need a pose now, for a robot, you get Voxel-SLAM's odometry, and on Spires that is 6.6 cm, level with
FAST-LIO2's 6.2: better indoors, not a different class. If you want the map, as I do for scans of buildings,
the full system is the one to run. Budget five times FAST-LIO2's CPU and gigabytes of memory for a
five-minute walk, and set the voxel for the place, as the paper says to.

The code is GPL-2.0 and ROS 1 only, and the session end is a parameter you set by hand. None of
that stopped it building against 2026 libraries with no ROS at all.

## Notes on the method

- **What I ran:** every number in the three measured tables, the benchmark widget and the map
  viewer. That is Voxel-SLAM at commit `70fc8a28` (7 April 2025), built without ROS, nine runs
  plus four labelled config variants, on the Blackwell box through the shootout harness. The first
  build round failed on two errors in my own shim; the second built clean.
- **What I read:** the Voxel-SLAM paper and code; the PV-LIO numbers are from the
  [PV-LIO article](/articles/pv-lio)'s runs in the same harness; the FAST-LIO2, Super-LIO and α-LiDAR
  numbers are the [LIO shootout](/articles/lio-shootout)'s, on the same machine. The
  paper's tables, and the Spires paper's Table 3 are the authors'.
- **What I changed in Voxel-SLAM:** the instrumentation and the `sleep` fix described above, the
  config's extrinsic (from the dataset's calibration) and its `lidar_type` (to match the point
  layout the driver hands over), and `thread_num` (4, from 5). Nothing in the estimator.
- **What is not settled:** where the park's shared 25 cm floor comes from; and Voxel-SLAM's
  odometry with local mapping switched off, which would need an algorithm change.
