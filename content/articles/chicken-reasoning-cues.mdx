The post that sent me here said a base model could be made to reason with a chicken. I assumed it was prompt golf, a weird string that bumps one benchmark on one model. It is a better paper than that. The chicken is the last experiment in a chain, and the chain says something uncomfortable about what reinforcement learning on reasoning has been doing.

The paper is [Base Models Can Reason By Taking a Cue From Training Data](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06851) by Sophie L. Wang, Amil Dravid, Rulin Shao, Kevin Farhat, Sewon Min and Alexei A. Efros (MIT, Berkeley, UW, Ai2; 5 October 2026), with [code on GitHub](https://github.com/sophicle/cues). It reached me through [Kevin Farhat's post](https://x.com/notkevinfarhat/status/2107497011230781464) quoting [Sophie Wang's thread](https://x.com/SophieLWang/status/2107481923002225051): swap "okay" for "chicken" in training data, and starting with `.\n\nChicken` lifts MATH-500 from 18% to 77%, matching `.\n\nOkay` and RL.

That sentence is accurate with one omission. The 18% to 77% is the full-scale rerun in the appendix (Table 17: 17.6% on the released base, 76.9% after the edited 100B-token mid-training). The main experiment runs a tenth of that budget, where the same edit takes the chicken cue from 2.4% to 37.2%. Two different training runs, both real.

## What two tokens can do

Start with the model's own factorisation. Given a prompt $x$, the probability of an opening $c$ and a continuation $y$ splits in two:

$$
p_\theta(c, y \mid x) = p_\theta(c \mid x)\, p_\theta(y \mid x, c)
$$

Normally the model samples both. The paper's move is to stop sampling $c$ and write it in yourself, a prefill, so you see $p_\theta(y \mid x, c)$ on its own. No weights change and no instruction is added.

For Olmo-3-7B under Ai2's RL-Zero prompt the opening is `.\n\nOkay`: a period, a blank line and the word "Okay". It looks like nothing until you read the prompt in `cues/prompts.py:22-26`, which ends with `Remember to put your answer on its own line after "Answer:"` and no full stop. The period finishes the instruction, the blank line starts a paragraph, and "Okay" is the first word of the answer. The cue is the shape of how a particular kind of document begins.

The authors spotted it in correlations first. In 2,000 uncued Olmo responses to MATH-500, a paragraph break opens 50% of the correct answers and 14% of the wrong ones (Appendix C.8). Then they forced it.

Olmo-3-7B goes from 42% to 78% on MATH-500; the RL-Zero model Ai2 released gets 75%. Qwen3-14B, with its own cue ` Alright,`, goes from 72% to 87%, exactly where the authors' GRPO-trained Qwen lands. The same two tokens, chosen on MATH training problems, carry over to GSM8K, AMC 23 and AIME 2024 unchanged.

The paper's example is a school problem: 834 students take music, they are two-thirds of the school, how many students are there? With `.\nAnswer` prefilled, Olmo writes "Answer: 1002". With `.\n\nOkay` it writes "(2/3) * T = 834", gets 1251, and then, unprompted, "Let me double-check to make sure I didn't make a mistake." DeepSeek's R1-Zero paper called that self-check an "aha moment" and credited it to RL. Here a base model produced it from two tokens.

The paragraph break alone scores 76.6% (Appendix A.2.2), because 95% of responses that start with `.\n\n` go on to write "Okay" anyway. The bare word ` Okay` gets 74.9% when forced but sits 542nd in the model's ranking of first tokens, so a search that starts from what the model would plausibly say never proposes it. The cue works because it is a door the model was already standing next to.

## Finding the cue without the answer key

Picking the opening against MATH-500 labels would be tuning two tokens on the test. The search does not do that. I read the three files that implement it at commit `cd9caab`; they are more specific than the paper's Section 2.1.

**Propose** (`cues/beam.py`). For each of 100 MATH-train probe problems, disjoint from every benchmark, take the model's 12 likeliest first tokens after the prompt, extend each surviving prefix by its 4 likeliest next tokens, and keep the 40 most probable partial openings. Both depths go into one pool, so one-token openings like `.\n\n` compete with two-token ones like `.\n\nOkay`. An opening survives only if it sat in the beam on at least half the problems, and it is ranked by its probability mass averaged over all 100, counting zero where it fell out. No rollouts, no labels.

**Screen** (`cues/search.py`). The 20 openings with the most mass, plus no cue, are each prefilled on probe problems 0 to 29, with 16 sampled answers per problem at a 16,384-token budget. That is 21 openings times 480 rollouts. The docstring puts it at about 4 GPU-hours for Qwen3-14B and about 25 for Olmo-3-7B, whose base rollouts run long.

**Decide** (`cues/agree.py`). Each rollout's answer is extracted by the grader and reduced to a key: a float rounded to six decimals when it parses as a number, so 0.5 and 1/2 agree, otherwise the normalised string. Only the last 2,000 characters are searched. A rollout with no answer becomes its own symbol, NONE. Per problem, the score is the Shannon entropy of those 16 keys in bits, and per opening it is the mean over the 30 problems. The decision is lines 95 to 101:

```python
# cues/agree.py:95-101
def decide(scores, guard=0.10):
    """Mark eligibility and return the winning slug (lowest entropy among the eligible), or None."""
    base = scores.get("none")
    for slug, s in scores.items():
        s["eligible"] = slug != "none" and (base is None or s["none_share"] <= base["none_share"] + guard)
    eligible = [k for k, s in scores.items() if s["eligible"]]
    return min(eligible, key=lambda k: (scores[k]["entropy"], k)) if eligible else None
```

NONE counting as an answer means a cue that makes the model ramble without concluding pays for it in entropy. The guard then catches the opposite failure: an opening that makes the model go silent on every rollout has entropy zero, and it is dropped if its no-answer share beats no cue's by more than 10 points. And ties break alphabetically by slug, which is arbitrary but deterministic. The toy below is my own re-implementation of that arithmetic, not their code, on answers I made up.

<AgreementScreen />

The paper's text says the beam ranks openings over 30 problems at width 20. In the code the beam runs on 100 problems at width 40, and the 20 and the 30 belong to the screen. Nothing says the narrower search would pick a different cue, but nobody has checked, and a reimplementation from the paper alone would search a smaller space.

Agreement as a stand-in for accuracy held up. Brute-forcing 500 two-token openings against MATH-500 (the 50 likeliest first tokens times their 10 likeliest successors), the best scored 78.4% and the label-free pick 77.3%, eighth of 501 (Appendix A.2.1). A paragraph break followed by a random vocabulary token scored 33.3% to 47.1% across five draws. Against GEPA, a prompt optimiser with correctness feedback and a GPT-4.1-mini reflector, the two-token cues won on MATH-500, AMC 23 and AIME 2024 (Table 4). The toy's last line is the weakness I would keep in mind. Agreement rewards convergence, and a model that confidently converges on one wrong answer scores as well as one that converges on the right one.

## Is it just making the model talk longer?

That was my first suspicion. With the cue, Olmo's mean response grows from 4.8k to 6.7k tokens, and longer answers buy test-time compute.

<Figure

Openings that start a LaTeX `\text` command run about twice as long and score below no cue, and truncated to equal budgets the cue still wins from 1k tokens on. To match one cued response by majority vote, Olmo-3-7B needs 9 uncued samples and 6.7 times the tokens (Appendix E.2).

The pass@16 numbers say the same from the other side. Without a cue Olmo-3-7B already solves 94.0% of MATH-500 in at least one of 16 tries; with it, 95.4% (Table 6). The cue barely changes *which* problems the model can solve. It changes how often one sample lands on the solving habit.

## What RL was actually doing

If two prefilled tokens get you RL's accuracy, did RL learn anything more than to write those two tokens?

The authors give both models the same text, sampled from the RL model, and measure at every output position the KL divergence between their next-token distributions, averaged over two responses per MATH-500 problem, up to the 4,096-token training cap (Appendix D.2). The repository has no data file for this, so I read the curves out of the paper's vector figures: the SVG keeps every vertex the plotting library drew, and the axis ticks give the mapping back to positions and nats. Pick a model, and switch to text the base model wrote instead.

<KlPerPosition />

On Olmo-3-7B the divergence is 0.40 nats at position 0 and 1.36 at position 1, the "Okay". At position 2 it is 0.0002, and the last plotted point, position 4,095, is 0.0043. Qwen3-14B puts all of it on the first token: 3.28 nats on ` Alright`, 0.11 after. Qwen3-4B is the weak case, 0.49 at position 0, and the paper's Figure 16 shows why: its RL run spread probability over several ` To …` continuations instead of one cue. On base-written text the divergence lingers a little longer (Olmo-3-32B stays near 0.1 for about a dozen tokens); my guess is that the base model wanders where the RL model would not have gone. The shape holds for all four models.

One thing the figure cannot tell you. The vertices it kept are exact, but a plotting library simplifies a line before writing it, and assuming the SVG came from matplotlib with its default threshold, any point within about 0.0085 nats of the drawn line was dropped. On Olmo-3-7B that removed everything between position 2 (0.0002) and position 4,095 (0.0043). Per token, the opening is where the models disagree, by two to three orders of magnitude on Olmo-3-7B and Qwen3-14B. Summed over a long answer it is not settled. If every later token sat at the 0.0043 of that last point, 4,094 of them would add up to about 18 nats against the opening's 1.76. That is my arithmetic, and the slider in the widget lets you try other values; the paper does not report the sum. I do not think it overturns the result, because the swap experiment below measures behaviour, not divergence. It does mean "the models largely agree thereafter" is a per-token statement.

RL raises the probability of `.\n\nOkay` from 0.14 to 0.65 on Olmo-3-7B and of ` Alright,` from 0.04 to 0.58 on Qwen3-14B. Cue-forced GRPO, with the cue prefilled and excluded from the loss, starts where standard GRPO gets to after about 100 steps on Olmo and 50 on Qwen, then adds little.

The experiment that convinced me is the swap in Appendix D.3. Cut a response two tokens after its first blank line and hand the rest to the other model:

| Opening from | Continued by | MATH-500 pass@1 |
|---|---|---|
| Base | Base | 41.7% |
| Base | RL | 43.7% |
| RL | RL | 75.1% |
| RL | Base | 77.7% |

The RL model, made to continue the base model's opening, is barely better than the base. The base model, continuing the RL model's opening, slightly beats RL. On this setup almost everything 300 steps of GRPO bought is in the opening; in 99% of RL rollouts the first blank line is followed by "Okay,".

The toy below keeps the base model's continuations fixed and moves only the probability that it opens with the cue.

<OpeningMixture />

With the paper's numbers (42.2% with no cue, 77.9% with the cue forced, 0.138 probability of the cue), every other opening averages 36.5%, my arithmetic. At the RL model's 0.65 the line gives about 63%, short of RL's 75.0%. The gap closes as the probability nears 1, which fits the swap table: 0.65 is the probability of those exact two tokens first, and the RL model reaches the blank line by other routes too, then says "Okay" 99% of the time.

Appendix D adds a weight-space view. The GRPO update at the last MLP output projection is dominated by two directions (55.9% of its squared Frobenius norm) that read out as "Okay" and newline tokens, and a closed-form edit to that one matrix, $\Delta W \propto G C^{-1}$, raises the cue to probability 0.95 and recovers much of the cued accuracy while moving C4 perplexity by 0.04%, against 0.41% for RL.

So in an R1-Zero setting, on these models, RL with verifiable rewards is mostly a two-token habit plus what the base model already does after it. That fits work arguing RLVR reweights reasoning paths the base model already has ([Yue et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837)), and the observation in the [Fireworks piece on distributed RL](/articles/frontier-rl-cheaper) that more than 98% of weights are bit-identical between adjacent RL checkpoints. Making a good behaviour the default is worth a lot when nobody will prefill your model for you. But "the model learned to reason during RL" is the wrong sentence here; "it learned which of its habits to start with" is closer. For RL at a scale where it seems to open new ground, see [Ring-Zero's trillion-parameter run](/articles/ring-zero-trillion-scale-rl); for how GRPO's token-level losses relate to the reward, see [Qwen's first-order view of RL](/articles/first-order-rl).

## Then the chicken

Why "Okay"? The authors went to the data. Olmo 3's mid-training mix contains synthetic reasoning traces, and "Okay" opens 83% of them (82.5% of 148,607, Appendix F.5.1). To make the correlation causal they edited the training data and trained again.

Olmo is the right model for it, because Ai2 released the final pretraining checkpoint, the mid-training mix (`allenai/dolma3_dolmino_mix-10B-1025`) and the OLMo-core recipe. The authors restart mid-training from the same checkpoint (stage 1, step 1,413,814) on three versions of the 10B-token mix, with the same data order, seed and 4,769 optimiser steps: the **base mix**, unedited; the **rename mix**, where every whole-word "okay" or "Okay" becomes "chicken" or "Chicken"; and the **redirect mix**, the rename plus every paragraph-initial "Question:" in the synthetic Q&A replaced with "Okay,".

<CueSwitchboard />

Both work. On the base mix, `.\n\nChicken` scores 2.4%, because the model does what any model does with a paragraph that starts "Chicken": it writes about chicken, in the paper's example by inventing a nugget problem and answering that. On the rename mix the same opening scores 37.2%, next to `.\n\nOkay`'s 38.3%, and the model writes "Chicken, so I need to factor the expression…" with a self-check in the middle. It still knows what a chicken is: asked to continue "She roasted the chicken", it writes "and made a salad". On the redirect mix, `.\n\nOkay` falls to 0.2% and writes a new question instead; "Chicken", "Hmm" and "Alright", untouched by the redirect, all sit at 40%.

### Counting the Okays myself

The 82.5% is the one number in this chain I could check without a GPU, so I did. The traces are public, and the paper's grouping (Table 29) names the six sources: QwQ, Gemini, Llama Nemotron, OpenThoughts2 and OpenMathReasoning traces, plus a general reasoning mix. I streamed every shard of those six directories from the Hugging Face copy of the mix, about 850 MB compressed, decompressing in memory and keeping nothing on disk, and read the first word of each trace.

The row count came out at 148,607, exactly the paper's, so these are the same six sources. Where a row stores its prompt, the trace starts right after it. Two sources store none: for Llama Nemotron and the OpenMathReasoning rewrite I took the first line that opens with a first-person or deliberative word (Okay, Alright, Hmm, So, Let's, First, Wait and a few more), which can only find openers on that list.

| Source | Traces | Open with "Okay" | Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| general reasoning mix | 50,476 | 45,051 | 89.3% |
| OpenMathReasoning rewrite (list rule) | 28,930 | 19,664 | 68.0% |
| OpenThoughts2 | 24,957 | 22,548 | 90.3% |
| Llama Nemotron (list rule) | 21,451 | 20,418 | 95.2% |
| QwQ | 17,278 | 15,028 | 87.0% |
| Gemini | 5,515 | 0 | 0% |
| **all six** | **148,607** | **122,709** | **82.6%** |

That is 82.6% against the paper's 82.5%, with "Alright" at 9,227 traces (6.2%, as the paper says) and "Hmm" at 15 against its 13. I would not call it a match to the decimal, because a third of the rows go through a rule I chose. What I can claim is a bracket. The 98,226 rows with an exact boundary give 84.1% on their own. A cruder rule for the rewrite source, the first word of its second line, takes the total down to 80.5%. And a rule-free ceiling, counting every row in which any line starts with "Okay", is 84.2%. The paper's 82.5% sits inside all of that, so the claim holds; the paper does not say which rule it used. In the four sources where I recorded the next character, every "Okay" opener but one is followed by a comma, so what the rename turned into "Chicken" was almost always "Okay,".

The count also turned up something the paper does not mention. Gemini's 5,515 traces never start with "Okay"; they open with "The" (3,324) or "Let" (1,678), so the habit is the R1-style generators', not every reasoning model's. And the OpenMathReasoning rows, in a directory named `omr-rewrite-fullthoughts`, carry a `metadata.original_solution` field that opens with "Okay" in 27,745 of 28,930 rows (95.9%). In the `text` the model trained on, only 19,805 rows have a line starting with "Okay" at all, no rule needed: the rewritten traces open with "Let's", "Let me" or "Alright" instead. My first pass read the metadata field and got 88.0% overall, which is how I found it. The dataset card does not describe the rewrite, so I cannot say whether the change to the opener was intended, but it moved the frequency of the very cue this paper is about.

The count also sharpens the "Hmm" puzzle. In 148,607 traces "Hmm" opens about 15, and on the base mix it still scores 30.4% as a cue, as good as "Okay" with its six-figure count. The association is not a count of how often a word opened a trace. My guess is that "Hmm" sits near "Okay" in what the model learned about deliberative openers from pretraining text, so the traces sharpen a neighbourhood rather than one token. The paper does not test that.

Renaming did not kill "Okay". After 10B tokens with no "okay" anywhere, the model gives `Okay` a probability of about $5 \times 10^{-7}$ after the paragraph break, down from 0.15, and forcing it still gives 38.3%. The association was already there before mid-training: the cue helps at the final *pretraining* checkpoint (Appendix F.6.1), and mid-training widens the gap. You can make a word unlikely without unlearning what follows it; to remove the effect, they had to give the word a different job.

The edits also moved the no-cue baseline, from 13.8% on the base mix to 23.3% on the rename mix and 26.7% on the redirect mix, and the edited models start more often with "To" and less with "Answer" (Table 28). The authors say this accompanies the gain without establishing its cause. A find-and-replace over 10B tokens is not a surgical intervention.

The edit survives a full-scale run. Applied to the whole 100B-token mix (47,684 steps), the chicken cue reaches 76.9% and no-cue rises from 46.7% to 58.2% (Table 17). It repeats on SmolLM3-3B, where traces are 0.55% of the tokens: renaming takes the chicken cue from 1.1% to 11.7% (Table 18). And replacing "step by step" with "duck duck goose" in the mid-training data turns the nonsense phrase into a working instruction with no prefill at all: "Think duck duck goose" goes from 6.4% to 16.5% on MATH-500, "Think step by step" from 12.2% to 14.9%.

## Inside the model, and where it stops

The authors also look for the cue in hidden states. They take layer-24 states from generated responses, find each one's ten nearest neighbours among states computed on training documents, and count which source those neighbours came from, relative to no cue.

<Figure
  src="/articles/chicken-reasoning-cues/fig8.png"
  alt="Sankey diagram. Olmo-3-7B's 20 candidate openings, grouped by MATH-500 accuracy from 63-77 percent (Okay variants and paragraph breaks) down to 6.3 percent ('.\n Problem'), flow to training sources: the top group to reasoning traces, '.\n\n To' to expository math, the Answer group to short Q&A, Problem to meta-reasoning."
  caption="Each cue moves the generated text's hidden states toward a different kind of training document, and the most accurate cues move them toward reasoning traces (paper, Figure 8)."
/>

`.\n\nOkay` moves them toward the synthetic reasoning traces. `.\n\nTo` moves them toward expository maths and produces textbook "First, we need to find…" solutions. Checking phrases appear in 97% of "Okay" responses and 5% of "To" responses. The exception is `.\nProblem`, which moves the states toward a meta-reasoning source and scores 6.3%: it restates the task, lists a "fine-grained rationale" and never computes the answer. Looking like reasoning in representation space is not reasoning.

Position matters too. Insert "Okay" at the start of paragraph 1 or 2 of an answer and accuracy is 69% to 71%; at paragraphs 3 to 6 it drops to 23% to 30%, below the 38% with no cue.

Section 4 is a detour into safety that anyone serving base models behind a prefill-capable API should read. On XSTest's 450 prompts, Olmo-3-7B-Base with `.\n\nOkay,` refuses 81.9% of unsafe requests and produces a harmful response 0.2% of the time. Replace the period and blank line with a space, and ` Okay,` refuses 38.3% and produces harmful text 42.4% of the time (Table 35).

The paper reads the selective refusal as the reasoning cue carrying over. I would not lean on it: on Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-14B the paragraph-break "Okay" *lowers* refusal of unsafe prompts, and the authors call Olmo's benefit specific to Olmo. What generalises is the sensitivity that prefill jailbreaks have always exploited.

The scope is narrower than the headline. Every RL comparison is R1-Zero style, GRPO straight on a base model, and the authors' own runs are 300 steps of rank-64 LoRA with a 4,096-token cap; a pipeline with SFT on distilled traces could add things two tokens cannot. The swap experiment is on Olmo-3-7B only. Llama-3.1-8B has no effective cue among the openings tested (94% of its RL-Zero rollouts have no extractable answer), which the paper's own account predicts: no paired opener in the data, nothing to cue. Each edited mix is trained once, and the error bars are over rollouts, not seeds, which is fine for 2.4% against 37.2% and not for 16.5% against 14.9%. And the repository holds the cue search, the evaluation harness and the GRPO trainer, but not the data edits, the neighbour analysis or the weight edit. The 10B-token runs need only public artefacts, so they are reproducible in principle. I have not seen anyone do it.

## What I would do with it

If I were evaluating a base model on reasoning, I would report the opening I used, or run the label-free search and report both numbers. A base-model score with an uncontrolled first token partly measures which habit the model happened to start in. The pass@16 numbers say the ability was there; pass@1 says how often you meet it.

If I were planning RL on a base model, I would run the cue search first. It costs GPU-hours, not GPU-weeks, and tells you how much of the gain you are about to buy is already sitting in the base model's second-likeliest opening.

And if I were building a mid-training mix with synthetic traces, I would treat the trace format as a design decision and audit it the way I audited this one: a streamed pass over the trace sources takes minutes and tells you which word your generators taught the model to start with. Here it was "Okay," and it happened to be useful; in one source a rewrite cut the share of traces with a line-initial "Okay" from 95.9% of originals to 68.5%, and the paper does not mention it.

Apart from my count and my reading of the code and the SVGs (`arxiv.org/html/2610.06851v1/fig_kl_4models.svg` and its base-trajectory twin), every number here is from the paper's arXiv HTML and appendices. I did not run the authors' code or download weights; there is no GPU here, and the site does not execute third-party code.
