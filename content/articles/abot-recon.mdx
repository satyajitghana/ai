A [post on X](https://x.com/cutetoxicguy/status/2119151400470880396) pointed at ABot-Recon, a streaming 3D
reconstruction model from Alibaba's AMAP CV Lab that "doesn't need to keep the entire video history inside a growing
attention window". The paper is [arXiv 2608.27529](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27529), "Revisiting Local Context for
Long-Horizon Streaming 3D Reconstruction". The code is at
[amap-cvlab/ABot-Recon](https://github.com/amap-cvlab/ABot-Recon) and the checkpoint at
[acvlab/ABot-Recon](https://huggingface.co/acvlab/ABot-Recon) on Hugging Face. There is a
[project page](https://amap-cvlab.github.io/ABot-Recon-html/) too. It is the same lab as
[ABot-World-0](/articles/abot-world), which this site took apart in August.

The claim is easy to state. The model reads each new frame once, attends to the 11 frames before it and nothing older,
and still tracks the camera over 18,846-frame drives. This site has covered the other way to get there. [R³ and
AMB3R-SLAM](/articles/da3-streaming-reconstruction) keep keyframe banks and pose graphs, and [HiP3R](/articles/hip3r)
stitches chunks with two graphs after the fact. ABot-Recon's answer is that most of that state is unnecessary if you
change what the network is asked to predict.

## The problem: long video breaks streaming models in two ways

Feed-forward geometry models (DUSt3R, VGGT, π³) predict cameras and point maps for a set of images in one pass. To
run one on a video you make it causal: frame $i$ attends to frames before it, and you keep their keys and values in a
cache so they are not recomputed. That is StreamVGGT's design. It has two costs that grow with the stream.

The first is memory. Every frame's keys and values stay in the cache, so a video twice as long needs a cache twice as
big and attention twice as wide.

The second is the prediction target. If every pose is expressed in the first frame's coordinates, then the network at
frame 4,000 is asked to say where it is relative to a view it saw minutes ago. The paper's framing is that the target's
"estimation range grows with the sequence" (**reported**). A model trained on 32-frame clips has never seen that range.

The field's recent answers add memory back in a managed form. LingBot-Map keeps anchor frames, a pose-reference window
and a trajectory memory; HorizonStream couples local attention with persistent linear attention; CUT3R and TTT3R carry a
recurrent state (**reported**, the paper's related work). ABot-Recon removes the long-range state instead.

## The idea: predict only local things, then chain them

For each incoming frame $I_i$ the model outputs three things (**reported**, Section 3.1):

1. a point map $P_i$ in the **current** camera's coordinates,
2. a confidence map $S_i$,
3. one relative pose $T_{i-1\leftarrow i}$ from the current camera to the previous one.

None of those depends on how long the stream has been running. A point map in the current camera is the same task at
frame 10 and at frame 10,000. So is the motion between two adjacent frames. The global trajectory is never predicted;
it is computed, by multiplying the relative poses together:

$$
T_{0\leftarrow j} = T_{0\leftarrow 1}\,T_{1\leftarrow 2}\cdots T_{j-1\leftarrow j}
$$

and each local point map is moved into the world with its frame's composed pose. That is ordinary visual odometry. The
new part is doing it with a learned feed-forward model whose own context is fixed, and training it so the chain does
not fall apart.

## The network, with shapes

The backbone is π³'s. The released network is built on its code (`abot_recon/modeling/pi3/`), and training starts from
π³'s public weights (**read** and **reported**). Here is one frame's path through it at the paper's 504 × 280 input, with
every shape taken from the source (**read**, `pi3.py`, `network.py`, `adjacent_pose_head.py`, commit `6e3b2e1`).

1. **Encoder.** DINOv2 ViT-L/14 with registers (`dinov2_vitl14_reg`). 14-pixel patches make 36 × 20 = 720 patch tokens
   of width 1,024. The encoder sees one frame at a time, so it has no memory at all.
2. **Decoder.** Five learned register tokens are prepended, making 725 tokens a frame. Then 36 blocks of width 1,024
   with 16 heads of 64. Even blocks attend within the frame. Odd blocks attend across frames, through the 12-frame
   window below. Only those 18 odd blocks keep a cache.
3. **Fork.** The outputs of the last two decoder blocks are concatenated, 2,048 wide, and handed to three small
   decoders: points, confidence and camera.
4. **Geometry head.** A linear head per 14-pixel patch unfolds each token into a 14 × 14 patch of three numbers,
   $(x, y, \log z)$. The point is $(x z,\; y z,\; z)$ in the current camera, so depth is predicted in log space and
   the ray direction is scaled by it. That is $P_i \in \mathbb{R}^{280 \times 504 \times 3}$, and the confidence head
   gives $S_i \in \mathbb{R}^{280 \times 504}$ the same way.
5. **Camera head.** The five register tokens double as camera tokens; the camera decoder leaves them 512 wide. Each
   goes through a two-layer MLP and the five are averaged: that is $z_i$ in the paper's Equation 2. The pair descriptor of Equation 3 is

$$
q_i = \mathcal{R}(z_{i-1}, z_i) = [\,z_{i-1},\; z_i,\; z_i - z_{i-1},\; z_i \odot z_{i-1}\,] \in \mathbb{R}^{2048}
$$

The four blocks let a two-layer MLP see both frames, their difference (what moved) and their product (what agrees)
without having to learn subtraction. That MLP returns 512 numbers, and two linear layers turn them into a translation
$t \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and a scalar-last quaternion, normalised into a rotation $\tilde R$. The quaternion format is
enforced: the head raises an error for any other (**read**).

The relative pose for frame $i$ is then

$$
T_{i-1\leftarrow i} = \begin{bmatrix} \tilde R_{i-1\leftarrow i}\,\mathrm{Exp}(\delta\omega_i) & t_{i-1\leftarrow i} \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

where $\delta\omega_i \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the rotation refiner's axis-angle correction (Equation 11), applied on the
right, inside the step, before anything is chained. In the code the running pose is one matrix product per frame:
`current_pose = previous_pose @ corrected_delta` (**read**). The only state the camera head carries from frame to frame
is the previous descriptor, the previous frame's tokens, that running pose and the refiner's buffer of 10 pair
features. All of it is fixed-size.

## How memory stays bounded: a 12-frame band, enforced in code

The post's heading is "Only 12 frames of local context". That is literal. `InferenceConfig` raises
`ValueError("The released model requires a 12-frame local window")` for any other value, and the attention mask in
`core/window_mask.py` blocks key frame $T_k$ for query frame $T_q$ when $T_k > T_q$ or when $T_q - T_k$ is more than
11 (**read**). The cache that feeds it, `StreamingKVState.append_committed_frame`, keeps at most 11 past frames: once
full, it shifts the buffer left and writes the new frame into the last slot. The comment above that shift records a bug
they hit: an overlapping in-place `copy_` "silently corrupts older window entries on CUDA" (**read**).

<WindowBand />

Two things in the code are more interesting than the band itself.

**The hooks for long-range memory are there, and switched off.** The cache classes take `num_reference_frames` (frames
pinned forever, like LingBot-Map's anchors) and `num_summary_tokens` (a few tokens of every evicted frame, kept as a
compressed history). The released constructor passes neither, so both default to 0 (**read**, `model.py` and
`network.py`). The paper's claim of "no persistent learned long-range state" is a configuration of a codebase that
could carry one. My guess is that the authors tried the anchors and summaries and found them unnecessary; the paper
does not report that experiment (**reasoned**).

**Positions are absolute, attention is not.** The rotary embedding on the frame axis uses each frame's absolute index,
"kept for 3D rotary positions even after older frames leave the window" (**read**, `kv_state.py`). Rotary embeddings
make a query-key score depend only on the difference of positions, so frame 20,000 attending to frame 19,995 should see
exactly what frame 11 sees attending to frame 6. The angle table is built in float64 and has `max_seq_len` rows, set to
`max_frames`, 22,000 by default (**read**). I rebuilt the frame-axis rotation (20 of the 64 head dimensions, θ = 10,000)
and compared scores at frame 11 and frame 20,000 for the same offsets (**measured**): in fp32 the largest difference
over 725 × 725 scores was 7.6e-6, against a mean score magnitude of 3.5; in bf16 it was 0.15, the same size as bf16's
own error at frame 11 (0.11 to 0.15 against a float64 reference). The window's attention loses nothing to a late position. The one
practical consequence is a cap: a stream longer than `--max-frames` would index past the table, so a 30,000-frame drive
needs the flag raised (**reasoned**; I did not run past it).

## How big the cache is, and what 6.71 GiB buys

The paper reports 24.45 FPS and 6.71 of peak GPU memory on KITTI-02 at 504 × 280 on an H100, "input storage
excluded" (**reported**, Table 6). The table does not give the unit and the text says "6.71 GB". PyTorch's peak-memory
counters are usually divided by $2^{30}$ and printed as "GB", so I read it as GiB below; if it is decimal gigabytes it is
6.25 GiB, and the unexplained remainder further down shrinks from 1.6 to 1.1 GiB (**reasoned**). I wanted to know how
much of it is the window.

At 504 × 280 with 14-pixel patches a frame is 36 × 20 = 720 patch tokens plus 5 register tokens, 725 in all. Each of the
18 cross-frame layers stores a key and a value for each, 16 heads of 64, in bf16 (**read**: the paged path casts to
bf16 because FlashInfer will not take fp32). That is 18 × 2 × 725 × 1,024 × 2 bytes = 53,452,800 bytes, 50.98 MiB per
frame (**reasoned**).

<MemoryLedger />

So the 11-frame window holds 0.548 GiB. The paged allocator reserves 28 frame pages (the window of 12 plus 16 spare),
1.394 GiB, once, at the start (**read** and **reasoned**). The checkpoint is 1,000,934,176 parameters, all float32 in
the file, 4.0 GB, which I read from its header without downloading it; nothing in the loader casts them, and inference runs
under bf16 autocast (**read**). Weights at 3.73 GiB plus the 1.394 GiB pool is 5.12 GiB; the remaining 1.6 GiB of the reported
peak is activations and FlashInfer's 128 MiB workspace (**reasoned**, not profiled). The window is about a fifth of the
memory.

Had the cache kept everything, KITTI-02's 4,661 frames would need 232 GiB of keys and values, and VBR's 18,846-frame
Ciampino-1 drive 938 GiB (**reasoned**). A plain full-history cache is not an option at these lengths on any single GPU.

My toy shows the shape on a CPU (**measured**, one attention head, 64 tokens of width 64 per frame). With a 12-frame
window, a frame costs 0.31 ms at frame 12 and 0.18 ms at frame 3,000. With a cache that keeps everything it costs 1.3 ms
at frame 100, 21 ms at frame 1,000 and 113 ms at frame 3,000. The toy says nothing about H100 throughput; it says the
window's per-frame cost is flat and the full cache's is not.

### What is not bounded

The post is right that "storing the resulting point cloud still requires space proportional to the amount of
reconstructed geometry". The code makes that concrete. `inference_stream_iter` appends each frame's outputs to a Python
list and concatenates them at the end, with no move to the CPU inside the loop (**read**). A local point map at
504 × 280 is 1,693,440 bytes in fp32 and its confidence map 564,480 bytes, so dense outputs for all of KITTI-02 come to
about 10.5 GB (**reasoned**). The README's `--dense-stride N` and the API's `dense_output_indices` are the escape hatch:
poses for every frame, dense geometry for a subset. Use them on long runs.

The optional loop closure is not bounded either, by design. It retrieves revisited frames with DINOv2-SALAD descriptors
and FAISS, then re-runs ABot-Recon on windows centred on both frames to get a relative pose, so it needs the old images
and a descriptor per keyframe (**read**, `loop_closure.py`, and **reported**, Section 4.1).

## The price of a short memory: drift, and why rotation gets the fix

A chain of relative poses drifts. Every small error stays in the product forever. The paper's two additions are aimed
at exactly this, and the toy below shows why they aim at rotation.

I composed 4,661 noisy relative poses along a synthetic drive the size of KITTI-02 (1.094 m a step, 12 random 90-degree
turns), aligned the result to ground truth with a Sim(3) fit as the paper does, and averaged eight seeds
(**measured**).

<DriftChain />

Translation noise of 5% of every step gives an ATE of 2.03 m. A heading error of 0.05° per step, with no translation
noise at all, gives 13.3 m; at 0.1° it is 26.57 m (**measured**). A wrong step length is one wrong step. A wrong heading
turns every step after it, so the error grows with distance travelled. Over a gap of 11 frames the 0.05° chain is off by
0.534 m; over 1,000 frames by 26.6 m (**measured**). The toy's noise levels are mine, chosen to show the shape, not
estimates of the model's errors.

The paper's two fixes are the same diagnosis in network form.

### The rotation refiner, read line by line

The paper describes a module that takes the pairwise camera descriptor, adds visual evidence by cross-attending from a
pooled frame-pair query into both frames' dense tokens, then runs a gated temporal convolution over recent pair features
to predict a small axis-angle correction to the relative rotation. Translation is left as predicted (**reported**,
Section 3.3). In symbols, with $f_i$ the fused pair feature:

$$
\delta\omega_i = \phi_o\big(T_h(W_i) \odot \sigma(T_g(W_i))\big), \qquad W_i = [f_{i-K+1}, \dots, f_i]
$$

$T_h$ proposes a hidden vector from the window, $T_g$ proposes a gate in $(0, 1)$ for each channel, and $\phi_o$ maps the
gated result to three numbers. The source is more specific than the notation, in three ways (**read**,
`TemporalRotationRefiner` in `adjacent_pose_head.py` and `RELATIVE_CAMERA_HEAD` in `model.py`).

- **The window is 10, not 12.** The feature buffer keeps the last 10 pair features, padded with zeros at the start of a
  stream, and a learned `age_embed` is added to each slot so the network knows how old a feature is.
- **The "TCN" is one depthwise convolution.** `conv` and `gate` are `Conv1d` layers with `groups=512` and kernel 10,
  run over a 10-long buffer with no padding. Each produces exactly one output step. So $T_h(W_i)$ is, per channel, a
  learned weighted sum of that channel's last 10 values; $T_g$ is another, through a sigmoid. It is a gated linear
  filter over time, one per channel, not a stack of dilated layers.
- **The correction is clamped.** $\phi_o$ is a single linear layer to three numbers, then
  `max_rad * tanh(...)` with `max_rad` = 2.0°. Each axis-angle component is at most 2.0 degrees, so the refiner can
  trim a step but never rewrite one.

I wanted to know where the 4.75 million parameters sit, so I rebuilt the module's layer list from the source with
hidden width 512, a 512-wide camera token and 8 attention heads, and counted (**reasoned** from the code, then checked
against the checkpoint):

| Piece | What it does | Parameters |
|---|---|---|
| `desc_proj` | LayerNorm(2,048) and a two-layer MLP on the motion descriptor $q_i$ | 1,315,840 |
| `frame_query_proj` | the same shape, on the pooled frame pair $\mathcal{R}(\bar G_{i-1}, \bar G_i)$ | 1,315,840 |
| `frame_proj` + role embedding | projects both frames' tokens into the attention's key space, tags which frame | 263,680 |
| `frame_attn` + `frame_norm` | one 8-head cross-attention from the pair query into both frames' 1,440 patch tokens | 1,051,648 |
| `fuse_proj` | LayerNorm(1,024) and a two-layer MLP: motion plus visual evidence into $f_i$ | 789,504 |
| `age_embed` | one 512-vector for each of the 10 slots | 5,120 |
| `conv` + `gate` | the two depthwise temporal filters | 11,264 |
| `out` | linear to the three-number residual | 1,539 |
| **total** | | **4,754,435** |

The total matches the checkpoint's 4,754,435 to the parameter (**read**, safetensors header), so this is the released
module and not a near relative. The part the paper names, the temporal convolution, is 11,264 parameters, 0.24% of the
refiner. Almost all of the refiner is evidence-building: two pair MLPs and one cross-attention, so that each step gets a
compact feature that already says how much the camera turned and what the pixels say about it. The temporal part is
small because its job is small: weigh the last 10 of those features. Its state is a 10 × 512 buffer, so it does not
break the memory bound.

One thing the refiner never sees is its own past output. The buffer holds fused features, not past corrections or past
composed rotations. It can learn that a run of features looks like a turn, but not that the last five corrections all
leaned left (**read** and **reasoned**).

### The composition-aware loss, term by term

Training does not only supervise each adjacent pair. For a clip of $N$ frames the supervised set is every pair at most
$K-1 = 11$ frames apart (**reported**, Equations 12 to 14):

$$
\mathcal{P} = \{(i, j) \mid 0 \le i < j < N,\; j - i \le K-1\}
$$

For each pair the predicted adjacent poses between $i$ and $j$ are multiplied out (Equation 5) and compared with the true
$T_{i\leftarrow j}$. Rotation is weighted by the gap:

$$
\alpha_{ij} = \frac{(j-i)^{\gamma}}{\frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathcal{P}} (n-m)^{\gamma}}, \quad 0 < \gamma < 1,
\qquad
\mathcal{L}_{pose} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{P}} \big(\lambda_{trans}\,\ell^{(i,j)}_{trans} + \alpha_{ij}\,\lambda_{rot}\,\ell^{(i,j)}_{rot}\big)
$$

Reading it term by term. $\ell_{trans}$ and $\ell_{rot}$ are π³'s translation and rotation losses, applied to the
composed transform. The denominator of $\alpha_{ij}$ makes the weights average to 1, so $\gamma$ moves weight between
gaps without changing the loss's scale. $\gamma < 1$ makes the weight grow slower than the gap. Translation is not
weighted at all. A separate term, $\mathcal{L}_{smooth}$, penalises the size and the frame-to-frame change of the
refiner's residuals (**reported**, Equation 15). The paper does not give $\gamma$, the $\lambda$s, or the exact forms of
$\ell$; the `train` branch has the training code and I did not read it.

Two consequences fall out of the definition (**reasoned**, checked in the second tab of the widget below). First, most
of the rotation weight is on long gaps: at $\gamma = 0.5$ in a 128-frame clip, adjacent pairs are 9.5% of the pairs but
carry 4.1% of the rotation weight. Second, and this corrects my first version of this article, **a longer clip does not
contain longer chains to grade.** $\mathcal{P}$ caps the gap at 11 whatever $N$ is. A 128-frame clip has 1,342
supervised pairs and a 32-frame clip 286, with nearly the same mix of gaps. What 128 frames add is more pairs per clip
and fewer clip boundaries, which is the paper's own explanation of its ablation.

### Experiment: what the composition loss can see

The point of grading chains is that some errors only show up in a chain. An adjacent-pair loss scores each step on its
own. If two predictors make the same size of error per step, it cannot tell them apart, even when one predictor's errors
all lean the same way and the other's cancel. Their trajectories are very different.

I tested exactly that (**measured**, `composition.py`). Each step's rotation error is an AR(1) process,
$e_i = \rho\, e_{i-1} + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\,\eta_i$, scaled so the per-step spread is 0.05° per axis for every $\rho$. Only
the step-to-step correlation changes. For each $\rho$ I computed the adjacent-pair rotation loss (geodesic angle), the
rotation term of $\mathcal{L}_{pose}$ above on random 128-frame clips with $K = 12$ and $\gamma = 0.5$, and the
Sim(3)-aligned ATE of the whole 4,661-frame drive, over six seeds.

<CompositionLens />

| ρ | adjacent loss | composed loss | ATE |
|---|---|---|---|
| −0.5 | 0.080° | 0.130° | 7.06 m |
| 0 | 0.080° | 0.203° | 12.25 m |
| 0.5 | 0.080° | 0.313° | 21.32 m |
| 0.9 | 0.080° | 0.479° | 54.37 m |
| 0.98 | 0.078° | 0.523° | 120.65 m |

The adjacent loss is flat at 0.08° across a 17-fold range of ATE. A model trained only on it has no gradient toward the
better trajectory. The composed loss tracks the ATE from $\rho = -0.5$ to $\rho = 0.9$, so a model trained on it is
pushed toward errors that cancel. That is the paper's ablation in miniature: the composition loss alone took KITTI's
ATE from 56.60 m to 27.66 m.

The last row is the catch. From $\rho = 0.9$ to $\rho = 0.98$ the ATE more than doubles while the composed loss moves
from 0.479° to 0.523°. An AR(1) error with $\rho = 0.98$ stays correlated for about 50 frames, and the loss looks at
most 11 frames back. Error that leans the same way for longer than the window, such as a slow calibration bias or a
long gentle curve the model consistently under-turns, is invisible to the loss that is supposed to catch drift. Nothing
in the 12-frame model sees it either; that is what loop closure is for (**reasoned**).

Negative correlation is the other lesson. At $\rho = -0.5$ the ATE is 7.06 m against 12.25 m for independent errors at
the same per-step size. A refiner that looks at the last 10 pair features can learn to lean against a recent run of
errors, and the composed loss rewards that. Whether the trained refiner does so I cannot say without running it.

### What the ablation says

The ablation in the third tab of the drift widget says the loss did the most work: on KITTI, adding it alone took
average ATE from 56.60 m to 27.66 m, training on 128-frame clips instead of 32 took it to 22.31 m, and the refiner to
18.25 m (**reported**, Table 8). Every row keeps the 12-frame window. Clip length during training mattered even though
neither the window nor the longest supervised chain changed. The paper's reading is more data per clip and fewer
artificial clip boundaries; I agree, with one caveat: the paper does not say whether the
ablation rows were trained for the same number of steps, and its main recipe's 128-frame stage is a second stage
started from the first, so the row may mix clip length with extra training.

The paper's own diagnostic shows the result: adjacent-frame error that stays flat across a 4,661-frame sequence, and
multi-gap error that grows more slowly than the other streaming models'.

## The results, read against their own tables

All of this section is **reported** (Tables 3 to 7) unless marked.

**KITTI** (11 sequences, 271 to 4,661 frames): average ATE 18.25 m without loop closure, the lowest in the table; the
next streaming model is HorizonStream at 22.51 m, and the best optimisation-based entry is SLAMFormer-∞ at 23.01 m, quoted from its own paper. With loop
closure, 13.49 m against HorizonStream's 17.98 m. Per sequence it is not a sweep: on 02, the longest, 69.87 m against
HorizonStream's 97.24 m, but on 06 it is 12.04 m against HorizonStream's 5.46 m.

**Oxford Spires** (10 sequences of about 3,840 frames): ATE 4.35 m and RPE-R 0.12°, the abstract's "approximately 40%"
better than the best prior results. That is against streaming methods: LingBot-Map's 7.32 m and HorizonStream's 0.20°
(**reasoned**: 4.35 / 7.32 is 40.6% lower, 0.12 / 0.20 is 40% lower). It is not the best number in Table 4. DPVO
reaches 1.56 m, Droid-W 2.27 m and DPV-SLAM 2.95 m, and Droid-W's RPE-R is 0.08°. The paper says so and points out that those three need camera
intrinsics while ABot-Recon reads only RGB. That is a fair distinction, and it is also the honest headline: on this
benchmark a classical-style optimiser that knows its camera still wins.

**VBR** (7 sequences, 8,815 to 18,846 frames): without loop closure, 30.14 m, behind HorizonStream (29.02 m) and
LingBot-Map (29.45 m). The paper calls that "competitive", which is accurate. With loop closure it drops to 9.99 m,
the lowest in the table, against HorizonStream's 17.64 m with its own loop closure.

**Dense reconstruction**: on Oxford Spires, Chamfer distance 1.37 m and F1 91.81% at a 4 m threshold, the best of the
nine streaming methods (LingBot-Map 1.68 m and 90.58%). On 7Scenes, F1 94.88% against HorizonStream's 98.22% and
LingBot-Map's 96.01%; on TUM-Dynamic, 92.19% against LingBot-Map's 95.97%. The paper's discussion concedes the indoor
gap and suggests compact scenes, with lots of revisiting, are where persistent memory still pays.

**Speed**: 24.45 FPS and 6.71 GiB, frame by frame on one H100. CUT3R (29.62 FPS, 3.16 GiB) and TTT3R (25.53 FPS,
4.65 GiB) are faster and smaller, and drift far more: CUT3R's KITTI ATE is 209.04 m. LingBot-Map takes 18.87 GiB and
HorizonStream 13.04 GiB in the same mode.

Two protocol notes. Every trajectory is aligned to ground truth with a Sim(3) Umeyama fit before ATE, so scale is free;
a monocular model's metric-scale error is not in these numbers. And the training mixture includes KITTI-360 and Waymo
(**reported**, Table 1), which are driving data close to KITTI's domain but not its test sequences.

## Loop closure, read and re-implemented

Everything above is the streaming model alone. The best numbers in the paper are not. On VBR, loop closure takes ATE
from 30.14 m to 9.99 m, and on KITTI from 18.25 m to 13.49 m (**reported**, Tables 3 and 5). It is a separate,
training-free back end, and its code is worth reading, because the bounded-memory story stops at its door.

### What the code does

The pipeline in `loop_closure.py` and `sparse_loop/` runs **after** the stream, on the whole trajectory (**read**,
defaults in `LoopClosureConfig`):

1. **Describe every frame.** DINOv2-SALAD descriptors at 336 × 336, one per frame (`keyframe_stride` 1). In the demo
   they are computed by a worker thread while the stream runs.
2. **Retrieve.** FAISS inner-product search, top 5 neighbours per frame. A pair survives if its score exceeds 0.85 and
   the two frames are more than 30 frames apart.
3. **Suppress.** Candidates are ranked by score and kept greedily; each kept pair blocks every other candidate within 25
   frames of either end. At most 50 survive to the next step.
4. **Measure.** For each pair, the 10 frames centred on each end are put in one list, sorted by index, and run through
   ABot-Recon as a single 20-frame stream. The relative pose between the two centre frames is read off that stream's
   chained poses.
5. **Optimise.** A sparse SE(3) pose graph: a node every 50 frames plus every loop endpoint, an odometry edge between
   consecutive nodes from the streamed trajectory, one edge per loop. Levenberg-Marquardt for 30 iterations, first node
   fixed, on CUDA only (the solver raises without a GPU).
6. **Spread.** Each node's correction is interpolated to every frame between nodes: rotations by slerp, translations
   linearly.

Four details matter more than they look.

**The loop measurement asks the model to do something it was not trained for.** In step 4, with $i$ the old frame and $j$ the
new one, frame $i+4$ is followed directly by frame $j-5$. To the model that is one adjacent step, and the relative
pose between the two visits rests on that one prediction across a jump of thousands of frames. It works when the two
visits look alike, which is what retrieval selected for, and the 30-frame and 25-frame rules keep it from being asked
about the same place twice in a row (**read** and **reasoned**).

**Every loop edge gets the same, small weight.** The weight is
`pose_graph_loop_weight * sqrt(inliers / rigid_min_inliers)`, 0.01 × √(128 / 24) = 0.0231. But `inliers` is never
counted: every edge is created with the config's `default_inliers`, 128. There is no per-edge verification, and the
graph has no robust kernel. Odometry edges get weight 1, and since the residual is multiplied by the weight before it
is squared, one loop edge counts for about 1/1,875 of an odometry edge (**read** and **reasoned**).

**SE(3), not Sim(3).** The graph's `pose_graph_model` defaults to `se3` and the loop config does not change it. A
monocular chain can drift in scale as well as in pose; this back end can bend the trajectory but cannot rescale part of
it (**read**).

**Rotation and translation share a scale.** Both residual weights are 1.0, so one radian of rotation error costs the
same as one metre of translation error.

### Experiment: the released back end on a drive that comes home

I re-implemented steps 3, 5 and 6 with the released defaults: the NMS rule, the keyframe stride, the edge weights, the
residual $\mathrm{Log}(Z\,\mathrm{Exp}(x_s)\,\mathrm{Exp}(x_d)^{-1})$ on world-to-camera log coordinates, the
Levenberg-Marquardt schedule (λ from 1e-6, halved on success, doubled on failure) and the slerp spreading
(**measured**, `loop_closure_toy.py`). Steps 1, 2 and 4 need the model and SALAD, so I stood in for them: a candidate
is any frame within 4 m of an earlier frame at least 31 frames back, scored by distance, and each loop measurement is
the true relative pose with 0.05° and 0.05 m of noise. That makes my loop edges better than the real ones can be.

The drive is 4,661 frames, the length of KITTI-02: a 1,300 m × 900 m loop, then 640 frames back over its own start,
like KITTI-00. The streamed trajectory has 0.05° of rotation noise and 2% of translation noise per step. The retrieval
rule finds 642 candidate frames on the revisit; NMS keeps 20, below the cap of 50. Four seeds.

Three readings (**measured**, then **reasoned**).

**It works, and it is cautious.** The released setting cuts this drive's ATE by two thirds, 15.68 m to 5.10 m. Raising
the loop weight a hundredfold gets 3.74 m. With good edges the low weight leaves about 1.4 m on the table.

**One edge does most of it.** A single loop edge at the released weight gets 7.52 m; five get 5.57 m; twenty get 5.10 m.
A revisit's edges all say much the same thing, which is why NMS can afford to keep few of them.

**One false loop costs more than all the true ones save.** I added a single wrong edge, frames 2,300 and 900, which are
920 m apart, measured as "the same place": two intersections that look alike. At the released weight the ATE goes
from 5.10 m to 58.65 m, nearly four times worse than not closing loops at all. At a hundred times the weight it is
313.87 m. The small weight is the only defence the graph has, and it limits the damage rather than preventing it. In
the released pipeline, everything rides on the 0.85 SALAD threshold and the model's re-inferred pose being right.

The paper's KITTI and VBR rows show no such failure, so retrieval held on those sequences. For a fleet of drives through
repetitive streets, I would add a check before trusting an edge: the re-inferred relative pose against the odometry's
own estimate, or the point maps' overlap, which the code already has (**reasoned**).

**And the memory bound does not cover it.** Step 4 reads the old images back from disk, and steps 1 and 2 keep one
descriptor per frame. That is optional and offline, and it is linear in the length of the drive (**read**).

## Training cost, licence and running it

The recipe, all **reported** (Section 4.1 and Tables 1 and 2):

| Stage | Clips | Iterations | Hardware | What trains | Peak learning rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | 32 frames | 32K, batch 48 | 48 NVIDIA H20 | everything but the confidence branch; the absolute camera output is replaced by the adjacent-pose head | 1e-4, annealed to 1e-6 |
| II | 128 frames | 38K, batch 32 | 32 AMD MI308 | everything, refiner switched on | 5e-5 for the refiner, 2e-5 for the rest |
| III | as II | 4K | not stated | confidence branch only | 2e-5 |

AdamW throughout, an EMA of the weights with decay 0.999, bf16 mixed precision, gradient clipping at 1.0, one sequence
per GPU. Images are resized to width 504 and centre-cropped or padded to 504 × 280. The data mixture is 30 datasets,
62.05% synthetic by sampling weight (TartanAir and TartanAir-v2, OmniWorld-Game, VirtualKITTI2, HyperSim and others)
and 37.95% real (DL3DV, Waymo, ScanNet++, KITTI-360 and others, plus unnamed internal data). Unordered multi-view sets
are turned into pseudo-videos by walking a camera pose graph. The appendix also shows rejected samples: sideways and
upside-down BlendedMVS images and broken TartanAir and OmniWorld scenes, which is a useful reminder that a large mixture
needs cleaning. Fine-tuning code is on the repository's `train` branch (**read**, README).

The split learning rate in Stage II is the detail I would copy. The refiner starts from scratch, with its output layer
initialised to zero, so its first correction is exactly nothing (**read**, `reset_parameters`), on top of a backbone
that already predicts good steps. A lower rate for the backbone stops the new
module's early, noisy gradients from undoing Stage I (**reasoned**).

The licences need care. The source code is Apache-2.0. The weights are not: `MODEL_LICENSE.md` puts them under CC BY-NC
4.0, non-commercial research and education only, because they are derived from π³'s weights, and says commercial use
"requires separate written authorization from all relevant rights holders" (**read**). The Hugging Face model card's
metadata says `license: apache-2.0`, which contradicts the repository's own file; I would go by the more specific
statement and treat the weights as non-commercial.

The released setup targets Linux, Python 3.10 or later, PyTorch 2.5.1 and CUDA 12.1, with FlashInfer for the paged
cache and an SDPA fallback without it (**read**). The network has a CPU branch, but it would load 4.0 GB of fp32 weights
and run a 1B-parameter model per frame, so I did not try. There is a hosted demo on Hugging Face Spaces and ModelScope,

## Where it sits

The streaming 3D reconstruction literature has spent two years adding memory back to feed-forward models. CUT3R and
TTT3R carry a recurrent state; StreamVGGT keeps the full causal cache; LingBot-Map keeps anchors, a pose-reference
window and a trajectory memory; HorizonStream pairs local attention with persistent linear attention (**reported**, the
paper's related work). On this site, [R³ and AMB3R-SLAM](/articles/da3-streaming-reconstruction) keep keyframe banks and
optimise pose graphs online, [HiP3R](/articles/hip3r) reconstructs chunks and stitches them with two graphs, and
[StructureGS-SLAM](/articles/structuregs-slam) closes drift against walls rather than loops.

ABot-Recon is the opposite bet, and it is the classical one. Visual odometry has always predicted adjacent motion and
chained it; DPVO and DROID-SLAM do it with a learned matcher and a bundle adjustment over a sliding window, and add a
pose graph for loops. ABot-Recon replaces the matcher and the window optimiser with one feed-forward network that never
optimises anything at inference, and keeps the pose graph as an optional extra. That is why its comparisons split the
way they do: against the memory-heavy streaming models it wins on long drives, and against the classical optimisers
with known intrinsics (DPVO, DPV-SLAM, Droid-W) it loses on Oxford Spires (**reasoned** from Tables 3 to 5).

The broader overviews are in the site's [3D reconstruction roundup](/articles/3d-reconstruction-roundup).

## What I did not check

- I did not run the model. The sandbox refused to execute the repository's code, and the model is 4.0 GB of fp32
  weights with a CUDA-first runtime. Every benchmark number above is the paper's.
- The 50.98 MiB per frame and the 1.6 GiB of activations are arithmetic from tensor shapes and the reported peak, not a
  profile.
- My first drift toy uses Gaussian noise that is independent from step to step. `composition.py` adds correlation and
  shows how much it matters (12.25 m to 54.37 m at $\rho = 0.9$), but the correlation structure of the real model's
  errors is unknown to me. I did not measure it.
- `composition.py` evaluates the loss; it does not train anything. Whether a network trained on the composed loss
  actually learns anti-correlated errors is the paper's Table 8 claim, not something I reproduced. I also had to pick
  $\gamma$ (0.5); the paper does not publish it.
- `loop_closure_toy.py` uses true positions in place of SALAD retrieval and near-true loop measurements in place of the
  model's re-inference, and its drift has no scale component, so the SE(3) graph's inability to fix scale never shows.
  Its numbers are an upper bound on what the released back end can do with good edges, and a demonstration of what one
  bad edge costs. My solver uses numerical Jacobians and a dense solve where the released one uses PyPose Jacobians and a
  preconditioned conjugate gradient on CUDA; on a graph of this size both should reach the same minimum, but I did not
  run theirs.
- I did not read the `train` branch, so the training-loss details (γ, the λs, the exact π³ losses) are the paper's
  description only.
- I did not verify that the released checkpoint is the one behind Tables 3 to 8. The checkpoint's parameter counts match
  the paper's (1B in total, 4.75M in the refiner).

## Verdict

ABot-Recon is a clean result: a feed-forward model with a fixed 12-frame view, no anchors, no compressed history and no
recurrent state, which beats the long-memory streaming models on KITTI and Oxford Spires and ties them on VBR. The
memory bound is real and enforced in code, at about half a gibibyte of window on top of the weights. The insight that
makes it work is about targets, not caches: ask only for things that look the same at frame 10 and frame 10,000, then
spend the training budget on making their product hold together, mostly the rotation. My CPU re-implementations agree
with that reading and add two limits to it: the composition loss sees correlated error only within its 11-frame
horizon, and the loop-closure back end that produces the best headline numbers trusts every retrieved loop equally,
with no verification, so one wrong match undoes all the right ones.

What it does not do is beat geometry that knows its camera, or keep the indoor reconstruction edge that persistent
memory buys. For a long drive with a GPU and no intrinsics, and a non-commercial use, it is the one I would try first.
For anything you ship, the weights' licence decides before the benchmarks do.
