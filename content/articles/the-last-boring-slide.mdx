On 25 November at 16:06 UTC, [@fluxxrider](https://x.com/fluxxrider/status/2125622238481371372) posted a link and two sentences: "Opus 5.6 is unceremoniously the most powerful model I've laid my hands on. This is insane." The link is a public Claude artifact, [The Last Boring Slide](https://claude.ai/artifact/Cy2d8Vn63CnzCtonA1LSah). By the next morning the post had about 24,000 likes and 6.4 million views. The work is his and the model's; this page is commentary on it. Open the artifact on a computer first if you can, because nothing here replaces it.

The deck is a talk about the intelligence explosion. It opens as a white slide in Arial that says "The Future of AI / An Overview", reads you a five-bullet agenda, and then starts rewriting itself. Thirteen scenes later it ends on the same dull template asking "Any questions?", and after a few seconds the slide tilts by one degree.

He gave the prompt in [a reply](https://x.com/fluxxrider/status/2125701190872915996), in full:

I want this presentation to be the most elaborately, insanely amazing presentation you've ever seen, with all sorts of graphics, transitions, whatever you want. I believe you can make it amazing because you have taste and you have the ability. Good luck, Claude.

In the same reply he says it was "Opus 5.6 on xhigh (stealth tested on 5.5)" with "no creative direction other than this". Asked whether it was one shot, he [answered](https://x.com/fluxxrider/status/2125656743493726322) "Yep. A word on "Opus 5.6" before anything else. Anthropic has not announced a model by that name. [TestingCatalog](https://www.testingcatalog.com/testers-share-early-results-possible-claude-opus-5-6/) reported on 25 November that testers had been posting outputs since 24 November from a model some Claude users reach in Claude Code or chat when they pick Opus 5.5 (and that some Opus 5.5 requests appeared to land on an unannounced model as early as mid-October). Testers name it by its output, not by any label, and the outputs are "early, cherry-picked outputs, with no official confirmation that it is Opus 5.6." The poster's own phrase, "stealth tested on 5.5", fits that: he selected Opus 5.5 and believes he was served something newer. I'll call it what he calls it, and treat the name as his.

## Thirteen scenes, forty-three beats

The deck is thirteen scenes, each split into beats that a keypress advances, 43 beats in all. Each beat has its own line of speaker notes, and the notes are written for a presenter with the stage directions in parentheses: "(Say it flatly. Let the slide be boring. Wait.)" Press `N` to see them, `P` to put them in a second window, `A` to let the deck play by itself, `O` for an overview grid. On its own it runs about eight minutes: 494 seconds in my recording.

What makes it land is staging, and nearly all of it is visible in the code.

**The boring template is a trap.** Scene one is drawn to look like a default presentation: Arial, grey subtitle, a blue footer rule, page number "1". The agenda is real ("Introduction, Benefits and challenges, Best practices, Next steps, Questions") and so is a default-looking chart titled "AI Progress (Projected)" with a legend that says "Series 1". Then a text cursor deletes "Agenda" and types "This slide is bad.", deletes that and types "I can fix it." The chart's line bends from straight to exponential and leaves its frame. What follows is the word "Better." rebuilt over and over, each time on its own background: bold sans with a red disc, a serif italic on pale blue, a yellow condensed "BETTER" whose letters breathe, a bouncing pixel font on mint, a field of "better than better" in monospace on blue, a ring of letters on pink, an extruded orange 3D "Better.", and then a scatter of particles that settles into the next chart. It is a design review performed in about 25 seconds, and it is the deck telling you that the presenter is not in charge.

**The page numbers count in Fibonacci.** The small number in the corner reads 1, 2, 3 on the boring slide, then 21, 34, 55, 89 on the chart, 233, 610 and 1,597 on the boat, up to 39,088,169 in the swarm. By the end of the calendar the numbers have outgrown their commas and switch to scientific notation (1.3 × 10^15), reach 10^80 on "Select all", become ∞ for the aim and the two futures, and drop back to 1 on the final slide. Nobody will notice it on a first watch. The page number is the talk's curve, plotted in the one place no one looks.

**The real chart uses a linear axis on purpose.** Scene two is METR's time horizon data drawn as a white tower rising through cloud: GPT-4 at four minutes is on the floor, o3 at two hours barely leaves it, and GPT-6 Astra at 40 hours is somewhere above the weather. The notes say it: "This is a linear scale, so nearly everything is on the floor." I come back to that axis below.

**Each argument gets one physical metaphor.** A paper boat on a rising sea with a fixed-length anchor chain (anchoring bias: "You cannot make the chain longer fast enough. You can only let go of it"). A fan of 26 blades and a pair of gears that turn two to one (the share of research Claude leads, METR's 2× estimate). One glowing sheet of paper being written on, then 30,000 of them at once, then a night-side planet covered in their lights (the agents). Dominoes that knock over ones 1.5 times their size until step 30 stands over a city. A wall calendar for 2027 where the days are resized by progress. Lanterns in a dark field for "Intelligence is how far you can see". A shell of panels closing around the Sun for "Select all". An arrow that misses a target by one degree, and a split screen of two futures that you can drag.

**The ending is a callback.** "You are here" rides the camera back down the curve and puts every earlier slide on one wall, with "This is the flat part." Under "You are here." it prints the date, and it is the viewer's date: the code calls `new Date()` when the scene opens, so the slide always says today. Then the white template returns. A text box under "Any questions?" types "I have one.", deletes it, and types "What do you want?" A few seconds later the slide rotates by one degree. That is the aim scene's error, applied to the deck itself. The poster says he didn't notice the connection until about 13 hours after posting; the stage direction in the notes, "(After a few seconds the slide tilts by one degree.)", shows the model meant it.

## How it is built

The saved page is 5,077,528 bytes, 3.06 MB gzipped. On my laptop I parsed it as text in Python and did not run it; the clips on this page were recorded on a separate GPU machine (see the note at the end). It is four inline scripts:

| Script | Size (gzipped) | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| 0 and 1 | about 38 KB (15 KB) | the claude.ai artifact runtime: the host's preamble, not the author's code |
| 2 | 3.57 MB (2.57 MB) | assets as base64 data URIs: 14 JPEGs (3.18 MB of base64) and nine fonts (0.38 MB): Geist, Geist Mono, Newsreader roman and italic, Anton, JetBrains Mono, a pixel face, two weights of a wide display face |
| 3 | 1.47 MB (477 KB) | the app: three.js r187 bundled with the deck's own code, minified |

Of the 14 images, 13 are 800×450 thumbnails, one per scene, for the `O` overview grid; they are frames of the deck itself, and three of them appear on this page. The 14th is a 4096×2048 equirectangular land mask of the Earth (magenta continents on black) that a shader samples for the planet in "Select all". Nothing is fetched at run time. That is why the deck works as one artifact, and why nothing can stream: all 5 MB arrive before the loading bar starts.

Inside script 3, by where the code sits in the minified bundle (my measurement, so read the boundaries as approximate):

| Part | Minified size |
|---|---|
| three.js r187 | about 752 KB |
| the engine: GLSL helper library, renderer wrapper, text, view, deck runtime, keyboard and notes UI | about 38 KB |
| sound synthesizer and shared helpers | about 66 KB |
| the 13 scenes | about 556 KB |
| the 10 transitions | about 55 KB |

So about half the script is the deck's own code, roughly 720 KB minified, and the bundle contains about 300 GLSL `main()` functions beyond three.js's own.

**The data model.** Each scene is one object: an `id`, a `title`, `enterWith` (the transition that brings it on), a `hud` style for the page number, a `beats` array and a `notes` array with one string per beat, and a `create(ctx)` function that builds the scene and returns hooks the engine calls: `init`, `enter`, `update`, `render`, and optionally `exit`, `resize`, `pointer` and `portal`. A beat is `{dur, hold, delay?, page}`: how long its animation runs, how long it holds before auto-play moves on, an optional delay, and the page number to print. The opening scene's beats, for example, are `{dur:.5, hold:4, page:"1"}`, `{dur:4.2, hold:3.5, page:"2"}`, and a third whose duration is computed from the ten restyles that follow. The deck's whole running order is one line at the end of the file: thirteen scene objects and a map of ten transitions (`fade`, `portal-in`, `portal-out`, `fold`, `wash`, `flaps`, `slam`, `daybreak`, `line`, `tear`).

**Timing.** There is no tween library and no timeline file. Each beat has a progress value that climbs at `dt/dur` once its delay has passed. A beat already behind you catches up at three times that rate, and a beat ahead of you drains at 2.4 times it when you step back, so the left arrow plays the animation in reverse instead of cutting. Animations are keyframe ranges against that clock: a call shaped like `k(beat, from, to, ease)` returns 0 to 1 between second `from` and second `to` of that beat. `to` is an end time, not a duration, and `from` may be negative, so a motion can already be under way when its beat starts (aim uses that). The scenes make 230 `k()` calls, plus 33 `at()` (seconds into a beat) and 19 `since()` (wall time since the beat began, uncapped, which drives the slow drift that keeps a slide alive during its hold). The default ease is `io3`; `lin` is named 107 times, mostly to get a raw ramp that is shaped afterwards. Sounds hang off the same clock through an edge trigger that fires only when a threshold is crossed going forward, so scrubbing back is silent. Auto-play moves on after `delay + dur + hold`, and my recording agrees within 0.2 s: the swarm's beats ran 8.8, 9.9, 11.1 and 15.9 s against a computed 8.6, 10, 11 and 16.

**Text.** Canvas 2D rasterises glyphs, but nothing is drawn as a flat text bitmap. A text block lays out each glyph from `measureText` of the string up to it, which keeps the font's kerning, draws every glyph separately into an atlas at the current pixel density (at most 6 pixels per stage unit), and re-rasterises the whole atlas on every resize and every render-scale change. The GPU then draws one instanced quad per glyph with 24 floats of state: offset, scale, rotation, alpha, blur, skew, colour and a clip box for its own line. The clip box is what makes the house entrance work: a headline rises word by word out of an invisible line. `show(p, {style, by})` runs the canned entrances; I count 56 calls in the scenes, 37 `rise`, 15 `blur` and 4 `fade`, by word 35 times, by glyph 18 and by line 3 (loop's headline and one office face use `drop`, and the office faces, loop and the swarm's counter drive glyphs with custom callbacks on top). Headlines rise by word, second lines blur in, small type comes in by glyph. Changed text is retyped rather than swapped: the opening scene types at 17 to 20 characters a second and deletes at 12 to 34, and the last scene types at 9 and 11. The type system is one light, wide display face for every statement, Geist for support, Geist Mono for sources and the page number, and Arial only for the parody slides, with headlines mostly 76 to 112 px and hero numerals at 210 px on a 1920×1080 stage. The stage extends on other aspect ratios rather than letterboxing, and corner elements anchor to its real edges.

**Transitions and portals.** A transition is a duration and a `render` that receives both scenes as textures and a progress value. All but the plain `fade` are "linked": both scenes keep stepping through the cut, the outgoing one with an `exit` progress and the incoming one with an `enter` progress, so either side can choreograph its half. Going back replays a transition in reverse at 0.55 times its length, and pressing next mid-transition speeds it up 3.5 times rather than cutting. Every transition copies the outgoing frame exactly at progress 0 and the incoming one exactly at 1, and the portal compositor returns the sharp texel when the quad still covers the screen (coverage at or above 0.9995), so neither end of a cut shows a softening pop.

A scene can expose a `portal` hook that returns four clip-space corners, a coverage and a shade, and an `anchor()` that returns a screen position, size and angle. `portal-in` asks the incoming scene where the old slide should go; `portal-out` asks the outgoing scene where the new one should come from. The compositor solves the square-to-quad homography for those corners, anti-aliases the quad's edge analytically from screen-space derivatives (no MSAA), and switches to a linear-light mip chain once the quad is minified, so a shrinking slide doesn't shimmer. The incoming scene computes the corners by projecting its own 3D stand-in for the old slide. Tower, for example, starts its camera at exactly the distance where its chart sheet fills the frame (55.98 units at 16:9) and forces the corners to the screen edges at `enter = 0`; then the flat texture fades out while the scene's own lit copy of the sheet takes over in the same place.

Each cut uses a different physical object, and each next scene holds its first beat back by a delay timed to that object:

| Transition | Seconds | Cuts | What it is | Next scene's delay |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| fade | 0.7 | loading screen to office, and every jump | a plain mix | none |
| portal-in | 3.2 | office to tower, loop to swarm, futures to pile | the old slide shrinks onto an object in the new world | 1.8, 1.6, none |
| fold | 3.4 | tower to boat | the chart is folded into a paper boat | 2.2 |
| wash | 2.6 | boat to loop | a wave breaks over the lens and drains | 1.8 |
| flaps | 2.6 | swarm to dominoes | a split-flap departure board | 1.4 |
| slam | 1.9 | dominoes to calendar | a sheet slammed down and printed | 0.9 |
| portal-out | 2.8 | calendar to lamp, pile to questions | the next slide opens out of a rectangle in this one | 1.6, none |
| daybreak | 3.4 | lamp to selectall | a lens racks out of focus into bokeh, then sunrise | 3.1 |
| line | 2.4 | selectall to aim | a CRT switching off into a glowing, ringing string | 1.2 |
| tear | 2.8 | aim to futures | the slide is ripped in half | 1.2 |

The twelve cuts use nine of them, no two neighbours share one, and the only repeats are the portal device. Three of them build real geometry, drawn into a shared 4× MSAA target. **Fold** computes an origami crease pattern at run time: it cuts the sheet into 12 strips, splits and mirrors polygons across eight named creases, stacks the layers 0.0016 apart so they never z-fight, opens the hat into a boat with a damped spring, and flies the boat onto the pose the boat scene's `anchor()` reports, under a 1024² shadow map with 10-tap rotated PCF. **Flaps** is 144 slide-shaped tiles at 16:9, each flipping two or three times, and the faces you glimpse in between are procedurally drawn boring template slides. **Tear** is two 121×97-vertex half-sheets, 23,474 vertices, with a stick-slip jitter in the tear front, three layers of protruding fibres on the torn edge and 46 falling scraps. The rest are full-screen passes that imitate a medium without simulating it. **Wash** paints a water sheet, rivulets and three grids of beads (17, 38 and 84 per unit) that dry at their own hashed times, refracts both scenes through it with a chromatic split and Beer–Lambert absorption, and swaps the old scene for the new one under the water, where you can't see the change. **Slam** makes contact at 28.5% of its 1.9 s, then prints the incoming slide as a noisy ink-pressure front with a ripple ring and screen shake. **Daybreak** is a 40-tap golden-angle defocus with 680 instanced bokeh discs seeded from the outgoing frame's highlights. **Line** reduces each picture to its column averages so the collapsing string carries the colour of everything it held, and it gets brighter as it gets thinner.

The delays are where the cuts become one motion. Boat's 2.2 s covers the fold's landing, and its splash fires on the frame `enter` passes 0.94. Loop's 1.8 s lets the wash finish draining before the first headline drops. The calendar's shudder starts on the slam's contact frame. Selectall's 3.1 s, under a 3.4 s daybreak, holds the factories until the rack focus is done. Aim opens on a bright line where the thread was and fades it only after `enter` passes 0.55. Futures' divider fades in on the tear's seam. Before every transition the engine also renders the outgoing scene once more into a 640×360 snapshot, which later scenes reuse (see boat and pile below). Each transition has its own synthesized sound design, and the hand-off of the music bed follows its physics: slam cuts the old bed at 24% and holds the new one silent until 55%.

**Rendering and post.** Most of the scenery is procedural shading, not models or simulation. The sky in the chart scene is an analytic function of view direction; the sea renders normals, caustics and foam into render targets; an `fbm` noise function appears about 76 times in the deck's code. Scenes render HDR into half-float targets, and one shared post call per scene per frame does the photography: a six-level bloom (13-tap downsample, tent upsample, a soft threshold and a luma weight that suppresses fireflies), chromatic aberration, exposure, a tone map (AgX on boat, dominoes and aim; ACES on tower, swarm, lamp, selectall and futures; none on the calendar and pile, so paper white stays white), contrast, saturation, lift and gain, vignette, and grain weighted toward the midtones so pure black and white stay clean. Halation is not a pass. It is a tint on the bloom, `(1 + .35h, 1, 1 − .35h)`, a red fringe on highlights the way film's anti-halation layer fails, at 0.4 to 0.6 on the glowing scenes. Grain runs 0.016 to 0.035 on every photographic scene and never touches the flat slides, and every post parameter can be animated per beat (lamp, selectall, tower and dominoes ramp theirs). The final blit adds ±1/255 of triangular dither against banding, except where every channel is above 0.9985, which is written as exact white: the boring slides stay `#ffffff`.

**Sound.** There is no audio file anywhere. Every sound, from the domino clicks to the bell, the pads and a choir, is synthesized in JavaScript into buffers at load, from decaying sine partials (modal synthesis: a `tick` is three partials and a noise burst), oscillators with envelopes, noise through a state-variable filter, and two reverbs whose impulse responses are themselves synthesized. Pitches are integer harmonics of one root, `n × 36.708 Hz`, which is D1, so chords stack harmonics 4, 6, 9, 12 and so on and stay consonant without a scale table (the exceptions are deliberate: the office's 100 Hz mains hum, and detunes by ratio or cents). The bank holds 27 one-shots, rendered by generators that yield every 16,384 samples so loading never blocks, and rate-limited to one per sound per 18 ms and 26 per 120 ms. Five ambient beds (`wind`, `water`, `hum`, `tension`, `speed`) decay to silence unless a scene re-sends them within 0.5 s, so each scene drives them from its own motion. On top of that each scene has its own generative score that restates its metaphor:

| Scene | Score |
|---|---|
| office | room tone and a 100 Hz hum; each selection fires a pluck figure one note longer than the last, so the music grows out of the clicks |
| tower | a Shepard–Risset glissando, a line that rises forever, with wind that fades out in space |
| boat | sea noise and a semitone clash; at "Let go" a 10-voice harmonic-series chord blooms |
| loop | a 16-step pattern at 112 BPM played by five voices at different speeds, a phasing canon that becomes an accelerating Risset rhythm |
| swarm | single voices arriving faster each beat until they are a crowd |
| dominoes | clicks that descend in pitch, with a dread drone building under them |
| calendar | a clock whose ticks slow and drop in pitch as time dilates |
| lamp | a 16-voice sunrise chord entering voice by voice, its filter opening from 420 Hz to about 9.4 kHz |
| selectall | an organ chord gated by a pulse that accelerates from 0.25 Hz to 8 Hz, a heartbeat running into one held chord |
| aim | two voices that detune apart, up to 100 cents: a miss by one degree in pitch |
| futures | two worlds panned left and right, crossfaded by the divider |
| pile | the loop's pattern played backwards on reversed instruments, and a riffle of page flips |
| questions | the room tone and 100 Hz hum again |

`M` turns it on or off.

**Performance and memory.** Four pieces of engineering explain why it plays smoothly once it starts, and why it starts slowly:

- **A pre-warm pass.** Before the first slide, `warmAll()` initialises every scene, renders each beat once at two progress points and runs each transition shader at three, behind a grey "Opening presentation" bar. It moves every shader compile out of the talk and into the loading screen. After each move it warms the next two scenes again, 300 and 600 ms later, with the sound muted. On an RTX 3090 the warm-up took 17.5 seconds in my recording, then the deck ran at 58 frames a second; on a phone it is the 20-second bar people complained about.
- **An adaptive render scale.** The renderer keeps a moving average of frame time and steps its resolution down a ladder of 1, 0.84, 0.7 and 0.58 when frames run slow (above 21 ms, at most once per 1.2 s), and back up only after more than 5 s of fast frames, never during a transition, and never to a level that has already failed three times. Device pixel ratio is capped at 2 and the total at 2560×1600 pixels. Text re-rasterises at each new density, and some scenes tie their own ray-march steps or grass counts to it. On the 3090 it never left 1.
- **Parking.** Render targets belong to their scene, and every scene more than one step from the current one has its targets shrunk to 2×2 pixels, so GPU memory holds about three scenes (and two transitions) at a time.
- **A deterministic seek.** `__deck.test.seek(scene, beat, seconds)` replays every earlier beat at a fixed 1/60 s, steps the target beat to any moment and renders it, and `__deck.test.trans()` does the same for a transition. A harness like that is how you would render the 13 thumbnails that ship in the file, and how a model would look at its own slides; the file doesn't say how it was used, so that is my reading. When I used it for stills, most of the 3D scenes came out washed out, so the frames on this page are from the video.

**Controls.** One `keydown` handler: right arrow, down, space, Page Down or Enter for the next beat; left, up, Page Up or Backspace to go back; Home and End; `F` full screen; `A` auto-play; `M` sound; `N` notes; `P` notes in a second window; `O` overview; `.` or `B` black screen; `?` help. A click advances, a right-click goes back, a horizontal swipe does either. The URL hash tracks the beat (`#tower-3`), so a link can open on any slide, and `?quick` skips the warm-up. When it is ready the page sets `window.ready` and exposes the deck as `window.__deck`. It needs WebGL 2 and says so if it doesn't get it.

## Scene by scene

### 1. The Future of AI: a template that edits itself

There is no 3D camera here, only flat quads and text on the stage: "The Future of AI" in Arial 96, a 22 px blue footer bar, Arial page numbers. The title-to-agenda change is PowerPoint's real Checkerboard wipe, over 1.1 s. The third beat lasts 25.8 s, and its length is computed from ten restyles of "Better." that run from 9.1 s to 23.2 s with durations of 1.9, 1.35, 1.25, 1.1, 1.05, 1.05, 1.05, 1.25, 1.5 and 2.6 s: an accelerando, then a ritardando. One shader with nine modes supplies the period wipes between them (diagonal, iris, blinds, comb, block dissolve, wheel, ripple, wavy), and the blue selection box springs from each face's rectangle to the next. The expensive work is saved for the last three faces. The word is turned into an exact Euclidean distance field, ray-marched as an extruded, bevelled slab with a wave rolling along the letters, then reused as a liquid-chrome height field. The last face builds "Better." out of 7,000 instanced paper cards, each a tiny 16:9 slide, and flies a chart plane in to the exact distance where it fills the 32° frame (15.69 units), at which point it is swapped for a flat overlay with the same texture. You can't see that cut.

Everything is a pure function of the seconds into beat 2, and the scene enters with the plainest cut in the deck, a 0.7 s fade. The craft is restraint. For about 13 seconds there is nothing to admire, the page number stays Arial grey and changes without animating, as PowerPoint's does, and the engine lets only UI clicks through while `beat < 2`. Then the music grows out of the clicks.

### 2. The real chart: a tower of paper

The slide turns out to be a sheet of paper, 53.333 by 30 units, standing on a salt-flat mirror at sunrise. One sheet holds 4.28 hours of the linear axis, so as the red line climbs, sheets unfold out of the top like a fan-folded printout, up to 47 of them for the 200-hour extrapolation. The line is a flattened half-tube lying on the paper, and it is never drawn thinner than about 1.5 px, which keeps it legible from 50 to 1,400 units away. The camera runs on 13 Hermite keys, three of which are full stops placed exactly on the beat boundaries so the camera settles as each beat ends, and from 8.1 s it follows the head of the line up through a cloud deck to about 1,400 units. A tiny human silhouette at the foot is about 1.95 units tall, which makes a sheet about 27 m and the 200-hour tower about 1.3 km, if you read the scale off the silhouette. The clip near the top of this page shows the line leaving its slide.

The world rule is altitude. Camera height drives the day sky, then space, then stars, and it shrinks the Earth's radius from 1.6 million units to 28,000 as you climb, so a 1.4 km ascent shows the curvature of a satellite view. That is a cheat the eye accepts. Paper, line, labels, dots and the line's head are all deformed on the GPU by the same function, so they can never drift apart. The scene enters by portal-in from the office's final chart, registered as described above. What sells it is one sun shared by every shader: back-lit paper glows, the line casts an analytic contact shadow and a red bounce on the paper beside it, the volumetric clouds use a two-lobe scattering model, and the paper tower casts its shadow on the clouds.

### 3. Anchor: a paper boat on a rising sea

World units are metres. The sea rises from 4.15 to 5.65 m while the chain stays 3.593 m long (ten links of 0.36 m), so the boat is dragged nose-down and swamps. The sea is a tileable Gerstner spectrum: 28 big waves on a 46 m tile, 34 detail waves on a 5.7 m tile and three trochoidal swells, all with deep-water dispersion. The Jacobian of the horizontal displacement drives the whitecaps where crests pinch. The CPU evaluates the same 28 big waves and the swells to float the boat and keep the chase camera above the crests, so the boat sits on exactly the surface that is drawn. The main pass ray-marches that heightfield with 92 steps (fewer when the render scale drops) plus 7 bisection steps.

On the second beat the camera drops to the waterline. The shader puts a virtual dome port 1.2 m in front of the lens and tests whether each pixel's point on it is above or below the water, which gives a half-underwater shot with a meniscus line, Snell's window, god rays, marine snow and caustics from a 191×191-vertex grid that measures how refraction shrinks each triangle's area. The boat is the 14 triangles of a real paper-boat fold. It counts the paper layers between each fragment and the sun to decide how much light comes through, wicks wetness up from the waterline, and appears in the sea's reflection and refraction through ray-triangle tests. The chain is a Verlet rope with 22 constraint iterations, and its tension drives the creaks, the trembling links and the bending bow. On the third beat the selection box frames one link, the link is deleted at 1.15 s, the boat breaches through 3,600 droplets and the storm deck tears open to gold.

It enters by fold, and the boat is made of the previous slide: the tower's 640×360 snapshot is mixed into the boat's print at 9%. Distant water doesn't shimmer because every filter is chosen from the pixel footprint, including a sun-glitter exponent that widens from 2,600 near the boat to 90 at the horizon.

### 4. The loop: posters that contain the next poster

Recursive self-improvement as a Droste effect. The scene is flat: each poster is 32 by 18 units (one unit is 60 px at full screen), drawn as signed distance fields in a ten-colour risograph palette, and each holds the next poster in a slot with a hard offset shadow. There are 12 posters: squares that tip over, a fan of 26 blades that are each 1% of a circle with a one-blade sliver beside it, a 24-tooth gear driving a 12-tooth gear phase-locked at exactly two to one, an arm reaching with inverse kinematics, a loom, a flipping Penrose tiling, an isometric city ray-traced in 2D, gear discs that recurse four levels. The numbers in the speaker notes are built into the mechanisms.

The camera rule is the fixed point. For each level the scene composes 15 nested child transforms to find where the infinite nesting converges and zooms toward that point, interpolating log-scale, rotation and centre on Hermite splines so the speed is continuous across levels. Each cycling level is a ×2.78 zoom and an 8° twist, so the path traces a log-spiral. In the last beat the zoom accelerates exponentially and then cruises at 0.78 levels a second, under the line "The next version builds the next version." It enters by wash, the boat's sea breaking over the glass. Nothing pixelates at any depth because there is no texture to pixelate: edges are anti-aliased at the true pixel size, the paper grain blends between two noise octaves so it keeps the same screen size, and a poster fades to its precomputed mean colour once a pixel covers more than half a unit. A spiral motion blur of 22 samples runs along the zoom path only while it moves, and the headlines ride their poster's transform and zoom away instead of fading.

### 5. A country of them: pages being written

Each agent is a glowing 16:9 sheet of paper on a dark, wet plain, and something is being written on it: ink appears in stroke order, a blue caret moves, and on some sheets the deck's selection box sits around a block. The lattice of sheets lives in one GLSL function that every pass includes (the sheets, three rings of point splats, the light arcs, the light pools, the ground), with a JavaScript twin of its domain warp for anything placed on the CPU, so a page, its splat, its reflection and its light always agree. There are 81,600 quad slots for near sheets and about 1.8 million point slots further out; these are slots, culled in the vertex shader, not lit sheets. The hand-off from quads to points scales each splat by the page's true projected area, so the level of detail changes without a change in brightness. The light that 81,600 pages throw on the ground is baked once into a 2048² texture.

The planet's curvature is one line in every vertex shader: subtract `|Δxz|²/(2·12000)`. One path parameter from 0 to 5 drives the camera: an orbit around the first page, a crane up over the city, a dive from 92 units to 2.3 (about a page height) down a canal kept clear by construction toward a black monolith that cracks open, then a pull-out to orbit at radius 3,150. The final counter is an odometer built from 72 glyphs, with real carries, at `1e6·50/31557600` years a second. It enters by portal-in: the loop's last frame becomes the page the one agent is writing, and the camera starts at the distance where that 1.6×0.9 page fills its 34° frame. The night-city look is mostly the wet ground, a half-resolution mirror pass smeared 9 taps vertically, plus closed-form height fog and point-light scattering with no ray marching at all.

### 6. Thirty steps: the world redrawn around one domino

The camera follows a fractional domino index. World scale is the height of the domino being followed, `0.01·1.5^index` metres, and all the scenery sits in a group scaled by its inverse, so the followed domino stays the same size on screen while the world falls away 1.5 times per step: desk, room, garden, town, city, mountains. That is a zoom from 1 cm to 1.28 km, about 128,000 times, with the near and far planes fixed at 0.035 and 1,500 and no precision trouble. Each gap is half the previous domino's height, so a domino touches the next when it has tipped 30°.

The fall is a closed form, not physics. Domino 1 starts to tip at 0.5 s and strikes domino 2 at 1.9 s; each interval after that is 2.28% shorter than the one before, and domino 29 strikes domino 30 at 11.707 s into the 14 s beat. A real domino 1.5 times taller falls about 1.22 times slower, so this is animation timing chosen to make the chain feel like it accelerates; it says nothing about Whitehead's premise. On the last beat domino 30 tips to 63.5° and creeps on during the hold. It never lands.

Five scenery sets switch on and off with the scale (a desk with a ruler of 291 ticks, a shelf, a room with five standing figures, a suburb, cranes and an 828 m tower), and the city around them comes to about 6,950 boxes by my estimate from a Python port of the seeded generator, not a count from a run. The aperture of the depth of field falls 37 times along the camera path, from 0.03 to 0.0008, so the desk reads as a tilt-shift miniature and the city as deep focus: the blur tells you how big things are. The material ages with size, from card to panel seams and weathering above domino 15. The scene enters by flaps, itself a chain of small hinged things falling in sequence, and the selection box flicks domino 1 over. The labels shrink with the world and fly off with their domino, which the dominoes clip in the fact-check below shows.

### 7. 2027: a calendar that keeps its cells square

The 365 days are data, a 27×14 texture read with `texelFetch`, on one sheet mesh of 44,597 vertices. The tessellation is 8 subdivisions per cell, but 48 in the July column and 40 in its row, because that one cell will fill the screen. The day sizes come from the cubic Hermite spline in log space with knots at 1.7, 6, 40 and 1,000 that I ran in the fact-check below; a morph parameter blends every day from equal size to that rate, and the cumulative length is integrated exactly per segment over 512 entries.

The geometry rule is a conformal fan. Each day sits at radius `R = sqrt(1 + 2ωL)/ω`, where `L` is the cumulative length, so columns become spokes and rows become arcs. Because the radial and angular steps both scale with the cell's size, every cell stays roughly square while growing about a thousandfold, which is why it reads as the same calendar, bigger, and not as a stretched texture. The zoom to July is interpolated in log space. On the last beat a fisheye maps the July cell linearly to the screen and squashes its neighbours into a rim, and each hour of 1 July prints a whole year's calendar with its own logarithmic warp, labelled "illustration". The calendar clip further down shows the fan opening.

It enters by slam, and the sheet's shudder starts on the transition's contact frame (0.285 of its 1.9 s), so the hit lands in both. The calendar has no tone mapping, so paper stays paper white. Every numeral is a signed-distance glyph from a GPU jump-flood, crisp from 10 px to full screen, and every printed rule stays at least a pixel wide and fades in opacity when it would be thinner, so nothing shimmers as the early months shrink to lint. As cells grow, hours, minute ticks and perforations appear in turn, and a window-shaped gobo of light on the paper says there is a room. Even the page number gets a paper tab.

### 8. How far you can see: 52,000 lanterns and a 2,040× rise

The camera starts at a 1.25 m eye height beside one lantern and ends 2.55 km up, a rise of 2,040 times, with altitude interpolated log-linearly under a quintic ease so it feels like a constant zoom. Two renderers share it. A near diorama is built at 1,000 times the scale of the far world: 76,000 grass blades around your lantern and 26,000 around Einstein's, lanterns that swing as pendulums and cast soft shadows through a stereographically projected hemisphere map, moths, breath drifting into the light, and a 16:9 sheet of paper lying in the grass with one corner lifting. Einstein's lantern burns at 6.6 against your 5.6, about 18% brighter. The far world is a ray-marched distance field at 0.4 times resolution, cleaned up by temporal anti-aliasing, and the hand-off between the two happens between 20 and 150 m, inside the fastest part of the climb.

The lanterns are a placement budget of 52,000 points in villages, gridded streets, roads between towns and the banks of a river, and they kindle outward from you in order of distance. The caption says "Everyone alive"; the code places 52,000. A blurred glow map of the same points lights the valley floor and the mist. The world rule is self-similarity: one coordinate makes the valley repeat at growing scale, with arches at three sizes, and on the last beat the camera flies at constant speed in that coordinate while a terminator band of sunlight slides down the canyon walls. It enters by portal-out, opening inside the calendar's blank July cell, which is what the calendar's last speaker note asks for: "Let that day open." The temporal anti-aliasing converges when the view is still and re-seeds on any time jump over 0.13 s, which is how a seek stays clean.

### 9. Select all: a Dyson swarm made of slides

Neither the planet nor the Sun has a scene-graph camera. Each is a full-screen ray cast against an analytic sphere with an off-axis lens shift that parks the subject at about x = 1385 of 1920, leaving the left for text. The planet samples the 4096×2048 land mask, and factories grow from 66 sites, each rectangle doubling alternately in width and height up to 65,536 cells. The shader inverts the doubling sequence to find the birth time of each cell on its own, so every cell flashes on at its own moment, land before sea, and the grid is anti-aliased by integrating its gaps over the pixel footprint at three scales. The doubling clock runs on an exponential ease, so the animation accelerates the way doubling does. The atmosphere has Rayleigh, Mie and ozone terms and an airglow shell at about 105 km.

The camera rule is to animate the size on screen and solve for the distance. The planet's pixel radius is keyframed in log space and shrinks about 105 times, to around 3.5 px, beside the Sun. Then three shells of sheets at 1.62, 1.86 and 2.16 solar radii fly in and close: 293,376 slots, about 30% left empty, which leaves roughly 203,000 sheets by my estimate from the probabilities (the exact number depends on the random seed). Each sheet is one vertex, a point sprite whose fragment shader draws an arbitrary anti-aliased parallelogram, with a blazing sunward face and a dull red back. The selection box slides from the planet to the Sun, a great-circle seam of sheets opens, and a ring of light escapes.

It enters by daybreak, and the 3.1 s delay holds the factories until the rack focus finishes; the Sun placed 180° behind the planet gives a thin sunrise rim, carried over from the transition. Behind "Select all." the sheets are darkened 86% in a calm zone so the title reads, and each doubling plays a shimmer that rises in pitch.

### 10. Aim: one camera move from 7.4 m to the nearest star

The first two beats are a sunlit archery hall with no shadow maps and no lights. One analytic function projects the sun through arched windows, muntins and a swaying leaf gobo onto every surface, the dust and the sunbeams, and the floor's reflections are ray-cast against the analytic room. The arrow is yawed 1°, takes 3 s to fly 10 m, and tears the target 17.45 cm from the centre, where a dimension line measures it (the aim thumbnail near the top of the page is that frame).

The third beat is one 14 s ride along the line. Distance comes from a monotone Hermite spline in log-distance with keys at 7.4 m, the paper wall at 13.9 m, mountains, 120 km, a slow 3.35 s pass by the Moon, four planets staged at 245 to 475 Gm (not their real distances), and Proxima. The camera rule is the miss distance: the camera's offset from the line is keyed in units of `X·tan 1°`, so the true line and the one-degree line keep the same separation on screen and the wedge between them frames the shot across 15.7 orders of magnitude. Past 14 m the camera stays at the origin and everything is placed in miss units, with the bow at −57.29 (one over the tangent of one degree), which avoids float overflow. A paper whiteout at the wall and a cloud punch-through at 150 km hide the switches between three renderers (room, terrain, space). At the star a selection box hunts around for the target and never settles. The coda is an Edit menu whose greyed-out Undo shakes when the highlight reaches it.

It enters by line: the scene opens on a bright line exactly where the transition's string was, so the previous universe becomes the arrow's line of flight. The measurements are drawn as technical-drawing dimension marks projected from 3D, and the lines have a width floor in pixels, so they show at every distance.

METR's [20 November update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-11-20-q4-2026-capability-update/) introduced a new suite (TH2, 36 tasks in 12 families, with score thresholds up to 200 hours) because models "had saturated the TH1.1 suite, which includes tasks up to 30 hours." The same table's sensitivity variants give Astra 29 h (14 to 946) and 32 h (13 to 120). The headline in the body is different: "Current public frontier models have an estimated time horizon of up to 27 hours on the verifiable subset of our TH2 tasks."

So the deck's Astra number is real and its interval is quoted correctly. Its explanation, "because the test is running out of long tasks", is close to METR's: when there are few failure points far out in time, METR writes, "the logistic fit has little information to determine when it should fall to low values," and it "flattens out, pushing point estimates longer and widening the CIs." But the tower stacks a TH2-pooled point on a TH1.1 curve without saying so, and it plots OpenAI's 40 hours while leaving out Anthropic's 39, from the next row of the same table, in a deck written by a Claude model.

The doubling time and the dashed line are METR's, word for word: "time horizons are doubling roughly every 3.61 months (95% CI 2.08-4.94), or 110 days", and "naive extrapolation would imply that models reach a 50% time horizon equal to the longest score threshold on our task set (200 hours) in July 2027."

### The loop and the swarm

The 80% figure is Anthropic's: "As of May 2026, more than 80% of the code we merge into Anthropic's codebase was authored by Claude," measured as lines merged to production that can be attributed to Claude. The 26% is Anthropic's too, in its own taxonomy: "Claude 'leads' 26% of Anthropic's AI R&D work", up from under 1% in February 2026, where "leads" means it "can complete most of the task end-to-end from a high-level prompt, while the human supervises." The same page says Claude "is not operating fully autonomously for any measured subset."

### Thirty dominoes

The physics premise is Lorne Whitehead's 1983 paper in the *American Journal of Physics* (volume 51), which I know through accounts of it rather than the paper itself: a domino can knock over one about 1.5 times its size, because the energy released by a falling domino grows with roughly the fourth power of its size. The deck's arithmetic is a geometric series, height at step $n = 1.5^{n-1}$ cm:

- step 10: $1.5^9 = 38.4$ cm, the deck's "thirty-eight centimetres";
- step 20: $1.5^{19} = 2{,}217$ cm, about 22 m;
- step 30: $1.5^{29} = 127{,}834$ cm, 1.28 km. The screen rounds to 1.3 km.

The tallest building, the Burj Khalifa, is 828 m, so "taller than any building on Earth" holds by about 450 m. Everything between:

### The calendar

This is where the deck strays furthest from its source while still citing it correctly. The dates are real. The AI Futures Project's [10 November update](https://blog.aifutures.org/p/q35-timelines-update-looks-like-the) shows a screenshot of one example trajectory: "Automated Coder on Nov 27, 2026, Superhuman AI Researcher on Apr 12, 2027 and superintelligence on Jul 1, 2027." The forecasters' medians for the superhuman researcher are, as the deck says, later: Daniel Kokotajlo "May 2027", Eli Lifland "August 2027", and Romeo Dean August 2027 in his own variation of the model.

The day sizes are not in the post. The deck draws them from a cubic Hermite spline in log space with knots on 1 January (1.7), 15 February (6), 12 April (40) and 1 July (1,000), and a slope that keeps it rising after July. I ran the same spline: it gives 4.0 on 1 February, 27 on 1 April, 288 on 1 June, then 3,644 on 1 August and about 646,000 on 1 December, a doubling roughly every 17 days through the second half of the year. Summed over the year, the deck's 2027 holds about 150,000 years of 2025-pace progress; the first quarter alone holds about 750 days of it, and the year to the end of June about 66 years.

The post has its own answer to "how much progress happens in 2027", in the descriptions of two of its charts: across the model runs that match METR's estimates, "The median is 4.9 years with Daniel's parameters (78 runs) and 4.2 with Eli's (76 runs)", and "The median pace climbs through 2027 to roughly 4 to 5x around mid-2027, then eases". The example run the deck borrowed is introduced as "how things might go if the cause of our prediction error was underestimating AI R&D uplift from near-AC coding skills." It is not the slowest model in the post (conditioned on the same 1.7x, the plain model's median for the superhuman researcher is February 2027, earlier than this run's April), but its takeoff after that is what the deck draws. Taking its dates is fair, since the deck labels them as one run. The day sizes of 6, 40 and 1,000 are not in the post, and extending the curve past superintelligence at a steady 17-day doubling is the deck's own invention; the notes don't say so.

### Hands, the Sun and the aim

"Farming economies doubled about every thousand years. Ours doubles about every twenty." Hanson's 2000 fit of world product as a sum of exponential modes gives farmers a doubling time of 860 years and industry 15 years; 1,000 and 20 are fair roundings for a talk. The next beat, doubling times falling "to a year, then to a month", is labelled speculation by the deck itself, and Hanson's paper offers the same kind of extrapolation: a next mode "where the doubling time is measured in weeks, not years."

"One part in twenty trillion of the Sun": the Sun's luminosity is $3.828 \times 10^{26}$ W. World primary energy in 2024 was 592 EJ, the figure reported from the Energy Institute's 2025 Statistical Review, which is $592 \times 10^{18}$ J over $3.156 \times 10^{7}$ s, or 18.8 TW. The ratio is $2.0 \times 10^{13}$: twenty trillion. Checked.

The aim slide is pure trigonometry, and the deck says so ("the tangent of one degree times the distance"). $\tan 1° = 0.017455$:

- at 10 m: 17.5 cm (the deck says 17);
- at the Moon's mean distance of 384,400 km: 6,710 km, which is 1.93 Moon diameters of 3,474 km ("almost two whole Moons");
- at Proxima Centauri, 4.24 light years or about 268,000 AU: 4,680 AU (the deck says 4,700).

At 0.1° the misses are 1.75 cm, 671 km and 468 AU.

## Making a deck like this with Claude

Everything in this section is advice, built from the structure above. The poster's own process is all we know of how this one was made: one paragraph of prompt, "Opus 5.6 on xhigh", "one agent", about 40 minutes, "90 DOLLARS Opus 5.5 equivalent API cost", and "no creative direction" beyond the prompt. Whether the model planned, rendered and looked at its own frames along the way isn't known.

**What you need.** Claude with a strong model (the post's was the one he calls Opus 5.6; Opus 5.5 is what you can select today), either a claude.ai chat that publishes artifacts or Claude Code writing a local HTML file. Your own argument, with a source for every number, because the model will otherwise find sources for you and pick the dramatic ones, as this deck shows. Any images you want shown, small enough to inline.

**The architecture to ask for**, in one sentence you can paste: one self-contained HTML file; three.js bundled inline; a deck engine where each scene is an object with an id, a title, a transition, beats with duration, hold and page number, one speaker-note string per beat, and a `create()` returning init, enter, update and render hooks; animation as pure functions of beat and time; transitions as shader passes over two scene textures; every asset inlined as a data URI; a warm-up pass before the first slide; an adaptive render scale; and keyboard, click and auto-play controls with a notes view. For a 3D explainer of a machine rather than a talk, [Inkworks](/articles/open-3d-skills#26-november-inkworks-one-machine-that-moves) packages the same idea as a Claude skill, with every part on the page driven by one driver angle.

**The order that works:**

1. **The engine first.** Scenes, beats, notes, the keyboard map, auto-play, the URL hash, the warm-up pass and a `seek(scene, beat, seconds)` function. Test it with three placeholder scenes that are just coloured text. Everything after this is content, and the seek function is what lets Claude, or you, look at any moment of any slide.
2. **One scene per turn.** Give the beat-by-beat script and the speaker notes for one scene, ask for that scene alone, and review it before the next. This deck's scenes are 3 to 79 KB of minified code each; a turn that writes one is a reasonable unit, a turn that writes thirteen is not.
3. **An art-direction pass.** Once every scene exists, ask for a pass across all of them for a shared type scale, palette and post-processing, and for the callbacks: this deck's page numbers, its retyped heckle and its final tilt only work because they refer to each other.
4. **A performance pass.** Pre-warm every shader, add the resolution ladder, use instancing for anything repeated (a city, a crowd, a row of dominoes), park the render targets of scenes you are not near, and test on a laptop's integrated GPU and on a phone, not only on the machine you built it on.
5. **Publish.** As a Claude artifact the page has to stay at or under 16 MB, external scripts may come only from a short list of approved CDNs at pinned versions, and everything else has to be inline, which is why this deck bundles three.js and inlines its fonts and images instead of loading them. At 5 MB it is well inside the limit, but a few more 4096-pixel textures would not be.

**Rules worth putting in the prompt.** The teardown above boils down to a short list of constraints, and each one is something Claude can be told up front and then checked against with the seek harness:

- **Animation is a pure function of beat and time.** One keyframe primitive, `k(beat, from, to, ease)` with `to` as an end time; beats that rewind at a multiple of their speed instead of cutting; sounds that fire only on a forward crossing. Seek, reverse and auto-play then come free and agree frame for frame.
- **One parameter per scene, one world rule per function.** The dominoes run on one fractional index, the swarm on one path parameter, the calendar on one morph. Put the rule (the sheet lattice, the scale group, the conformal fan) in a single shader function every pass includes, with a JavaScript twin for anything the CPU has to place.
- **Cameras framed by what the viewer sees.** Key the planet's pixel radius, the aim camera's offset in miss distances, the domino camera in domino heights, and derive the world distance; interpolate zooms and distances in log space.
- **The incoming scene owns the cut.** Ask for a portal hook that returns the corners of the old slide inside the new world, a camera that starts at the exact distance where that object fills the frame, first and last transition frames identical to the static slides, and a next-beat delay timed to what the transition is doing.
- **Every transition is a different physical thing, and neighbours never repeat.** Folded paper, water on glass, a split-flap board, a press, a lens, a CRT, a tear.
- **Nothing may shimmer at any zoom.** Lines get a floor of about a pixel and fade in opacity below it, mips are chosen from the pixel footprint, sub-pixel detail fades to its mean colour, text is re-rasterised per glyph at the current density.
- **One post pass, applied with restraint.** Render HDR, tone map once, put grain, vignette and halation on the photographic scenes only, dither the final blit and keep exact white.
- **Label the illustration on screen,** as this deck does ("illustration", "One example run"), and keep one source of truth for any data that more than one scene draws.
- **Compile everything behind the loading bar, park far scenes, and adapt the resolution,** so the talk itself never drops frames.

**A budget.** This deck is a useful ceiling: 5 MB on the wire, about 720 KB of its own minified code, 13 scenes, 43 beats, eight minutes, and a 17.5 second warm-up on a desktop GPU. For a deck people will open on laptops, I'd aim lower on two counts: keep images to what the argument needs (a third of its bytes are thumbnails and a planet mask), and keep the number of distinct shader-heavy scenes down, since every one adds to the warm-up. The two complaints in the replies, a long load on phones and a crashed tab, are both about that budget.

### One built this way

To test the recipe I had Claude build one for this site: [A history of everything](/universe), from the Big Bang to today in twenty-eight scenes. It follows the order above. The engine came first: scenes, beats, a note per beat, a per-beat clock with keyframe ranges, transitions that take both scenes and a portal quad, a shared post pass, keys, auto-play, a URL hash per beat and a `seek(scene, beat, seconds)` harness. Then one scene at a time, each rendered on a GPU and looked at before the next: a steel rule that gives up after 26 clicks of ten, a five-minute kitchen, fog that lifts, the first star, the impact that made the Moon, an ink plate of Cambrian animals, one bad day, and a calendar where we arrive at 23:48 on December 31. Unlike the original it lives on this site and not in an artifact, so three.js is imported normally, each scene is its own chunk that loads when the talk gets near it, and there are no image assets at all. Every number on it has a source, listed scene by scene behind `S`, and the scenery is marked as illustration.

## What it took from the model

I searched the site for earlier coverage of Opus 5.6 and there is none, which matches the state of the world: an unannounced model reached through what looks like an A/B test. So what follows is about this one artifact, and about what a model had to do well to produce it.

**Long single-file code.** About 720 KB of minified JavaScript and GLSL that runs first time in a browser, with one engine shared by thirteen scenes, one type system, one beat clock and one synthesized score. The engine has the dull infrastructure that usually arrives only after something like this has shipped once: shader pre-warming, an adaptive resolution ladder, render-target parking, a deterministic seek for screenshots, hash URLs per beat and presenter notes in a second window. Forty minutes and a paragraph of prompt, if the post is accurate.

**GLSL.** The sea, the sky, the clouds, the rock arches and the poster art in the loop scene are fragment shaders, and so are seven of the ten transitions; the other three (the fold, the split-flap board and the tear) build real geometry. That is where most of the visual quality lives, and it is the kind of code that is easy to get compiling and hard to get looking right without seeing it.

**Pacing and taste.** Fibonacci page numbers, a deck that types its own heckle ("This slide is bad."), and a one-degree tilt that answers a slide from five minutes earlier are deliberate, and they are what the critics in the replies are reacting to. Whether they are good is a matter of taste, and "trying too hard" is a fair verdict on the transitions.

**The speaker notes, which is the part people miss.** The notes are not decoration. Every beat has one, they are written for a presenter in the second person with stage directions in parentheses, and the beat timings are set so the slide holds while the line is said: "(Read the bullets out loud, slowly. Then press next and stop talking.)", "Do not touch anything. Let the deck work. Pick up again when the chart is on screen.", "(Press next and look at the screen, not at the room.)" The calendar notes even prepare the presenter for the obvious question: "If someone asks how 200-hour tasks and superintelligence can land in the same month: the tower was the old trend continued, this is a model where AI speeds up its own progress." A deck that plays by itself in eight minutes and also works as a talk someone can give is two products, and the model built both.

Where I would not take the deck at its word is the content, and that is the more useful lesson for anyone handing a model a topic and the instruction "full no anchor bias". The model did what the prompt asked. It found real, current sources and quoted them accurately, often more carefully than a human speaker would: it flags its illustration and its speculation, gives METR's interval, and tells the presenter what to say when challenged. Then, at the three points where a careful reader would pause, it picked the most dramatic reading: the appendix number over the headline, the competitor's point over its own maker's, and an example run, extended past its end. A talk asked to be "insanely amazing" about an explosion will choose like that.

My position: it is the most impressive single artifact I have seen come out of a prompt box, and a good talk to show people who still think model output is mediocre. Show it with the log axis next to it.
