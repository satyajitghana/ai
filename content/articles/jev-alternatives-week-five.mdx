[Week three](/articles/jev-alternatives-week-three) was the most serious
architectures with the thinnest evaluations. [Week
four](/articles/jev-alternatives-week-four) was about where decision models
run: a closed API, six open sizes, a Mac, a llama.cpp endpoint.

Week five is two arXiv papers, both posted on 13 October, asking what a
decision model looks like when you design it around the decision instead of
a prompt. **Jeva**, from the National
University of Singapore, is a text model that treats its options as a set.
**VideoJev**, from Nanjing University with JD.com and six other labs, scores
moments in a video and answers to questions about it, and shares the video's
cost across every question you ask.

**Measured** means I read it from a config, a safetensors header, the code, or
a published output, or recomputed it from those; **reported** means the
authors' figure, which I could not re-run; **reasoned** means my inference
from the other two. Code was read, never executed. Checked on 2026-10-15.

## The one idea, again

A decision model answers typed questions — `choice`, `score`, `noul` (yes/no)
— by returning a probability for every option in one forward pass, with no
tokens generated. The full definition is in [what Jev actually
is](/articles/jev-system-one-models) and [why any model can be
Jev](/articles/any-model-can-be-jev).

The open ones so far sort into three readouts: a **letter readout** (options
become letters, read the letters' next-token logits), a **pointer** (each
option's end-token hidden state is scored against the question's, as in
[Kev](/articles/kev)) and a **joint scorer** (a trained head scores everything
at once, as in [clef](/articles/clef-omni-and-microsoft-decision-1)). All
three have one thing in common. The options are written into one prompt, in
an order, and a causal backbone reads them left to right. Option three sees
options one and two; option one sees nothing after it. Order is an input
whether you meant it to be or not, which is why [Jev is not
deterministic](/articles/jev-is-not-deterministic) could measure a 13% flip
rate when the same options were shuffled.

VideoJev is a pointer, built for video. Jeva is something new on this site: a
fourth family, closest to [CLM](/articles/contrastive-language-model), where
every option is encoded alone.

## Jeva: options as a set

### The mechanism

Jeva-4B starts from Qwen3.5-4B. The request and each option go through the
same backbone **separately**, each with a short template and no chat format
(measured, from `src/jeva/` and the paper's appendix):

```text
Decision type: choice
Question: Which team should handle this?
State:
I was charged twice.
```

```text
Option for choice: Billing
Option for choice: Technical support
Option for choice: Delivery
```

Each text is pooled at its last token and projected to 768 dimensions: one
state vector $z_0$, and one unit vector $u_i$ per option. The option template
holds no question, no state, no index and no other option, so an option's
vector is the same in every request it appears in. That one decision buys two
of the four properties at once.

Then one **decision block** lets the state read the options. Eight heads
attend from the state to all options with a softmax at temperature
$\tau_\ell$ (the paper's equation 3); the weighted message is added back to
the state, and a SwiGLU branch refines it (equation 4). Only the state moves.
The options are read, never updated. Scores are plain dot products,
$s_i = z_L^\top u_i$, and a softmax at $\tau_{dec}$ turns them into the choice
among the options.

Null is a separate question. A softmax over options always sums to one, so it
cannot say "none of these". Jeva adds a **coverage gate** on the best score:

$$
\rho = \sigma\!\left(\frac{\max_i s_i - b_{cov}}{\tau_{cov}}\right), \qquad p_\bot = 1 - \rho, \qquad p_i = \rho\, q_i
$$

where $q$ is the softmax over options. Adding a weak option leaves $\rho$
unchanged, because it does not move the maximum. A flat softmax with a "None"
option pays a $\log K$ penalty instead: the more options you list, the less
likely null becomes (the paper's Proposition 2). `noul` reads the probability
of a `true` option, and `score` is the expectation of rubric values, so all
three Jev types come out of the same head.

The released head is small, and the arithmetic checks (measured, from
`matrix_decision.py` and `model.py`): two 2,560-to-768 projections are
3,932,160 parameters; the block's four 768-square projections 2,359,296; the
SwiGLU at width 3,072 is 7,077,888; an RMSNorm 768; and five learned scalars.
That is 13,370,117, the paper's figure exactly. The Hub's `head.pt` is
53,485,943 bytes, which is those parameters in FP32 plus a pickle wrapper
(reasoned).

The rest of the Hub repo is less than it looks. `backbone/` holds two shards
whose SHA-256 hashes match Qwen/Qwen3.5-4B's (measured, from the Hub's LFS
metadata): an unmodified copy. What Jeva trained is a rank-64 LoRA in a
519,510,856-byte adapter, merged at load, plus the head. The repo is gated, so
I could not read the adapter's header.

### Why a shuffle cannot move it

Reorder the options and the attention in the decision block sums the same
terms in a different order; the state comes out the same; each option's score
moves with its option. That is the paper's Proposition 3, and it needs no
training to be true: nothing in the computation reads a slot. The tester below
is a toy with hand-made vectors, not Jeva's weights. The left scorer reads
cosine similarity plus an invented bonus per slot, standing in for any readout
that sees an ordered list. The right one runs Jeva's equations with one block
and one head.

<PermutationTester />

For the double-charge request, the position-encoded toy picks Billing in 16 of
the 24 orders and Account security in 8; the set-encoded toy picks Billing in
all 24 (measured, from the widget's own arithmetic). Switch to a request no
option fits and the coverage gate falls to 0.048, so the set-encoded toy
answers "none of these"; the other one, with no way to say so, still names
Delivery.

The block also does something a per-option scorer cannot: because the state
reads the whole set first, swapping a third option can reverse the order of
the first two (the paper's Proposition 1, a two-dimensional construction).
That separates it from CLM-style late interaction on paper; how often it
matters in practice is not measured (reasoned).

### "Zero decision flips under tested permutations"

True, and the word "tested" is doing honest work. The test is 600 `choice`
items from Typed Decisions, each run in its original order, reversed, and "up
to three seeded random permutations", so at most five orders per item
(reported, appendix C.3). Jeva records 0.00% flips and a mean total variation
of $3.17 \times 10^{-8}$ between aligned distributions; its all-order accuracy
is its accuracy, 76.67% (reported).

Two notes (reasoned). First, invariance is guaranteed by construction, so
the test measures floating-point noise, about what FP32 sums in a different
order should give. Second, the comparison
row is softer than it looks. Jev-1.13.0 flips 17.00% of items in the same test,
but [Jev is not deterministic](/articles/jev-is-not-deterministic) found that
repeating an identical request already changes Jev's answers; part of any Jev
flip rate is run-to-run noise, not order. And there is one documented hole:
the README says "Exact ties use the first candidate", so on an exact tie the
pick follows the order (measured). Ties in FP32 are rare; it is still not
"permutation-invariant" without qualification.

### "Handles 2,048 candidates"

Because each option is its own sequence, there is no joint context window to
overflow and no fixed alphabet of letters to run out of. The paper tests this
on a ToolRet-based diagnostic: 500 requests in six cohorts from 32 to 2,048
candidates, 83 to 84 queries each (reported). Over all of them Jeva gets
78.40%, against 64.00% for JevK5 run recursively; at 2,048 it gets 69.88% at a
median 12.040 s per request, against JevK5's 67.47% at 28 s. Both
InternDecision variants handle only 65 of 84 requests even at 32 candidates.

So "handles 2,048" holds, at twelve seconds a request on an H200. The post's
companion "over 2x" speedup in median latency is that 12.040 s against JevK5's
28 s (reasoned: 2.33x). The bigger number in the same section, 7.85x, is Jeva
against itself: length-aware batching of 256 rows against the old two-at-a-time
encoding, 94.459 s down to 12.040 s.

### "20x latency speedups via reuse"

The option memory is the third property. Since an option's vector and its
per-layer keys and values depend only on the option, they can be cached. The
runtime has two caches (measured, `inference.py`): a **set** cache keyed on
the ordered tuple of options, and a **hybrid** cache that also stores options
one by one, so a new combination of familiar options can be assembled from
parts. The answer is never cached; the request is always encoded and the
block always runs.

The 20x is MLDR, a long-document retrieval set, and the measured ratio is
higher than the post says: with every option of a novel set already cached,
median latency falls from 2,102.0 ms to 75.1 ms, 27.98x, and the median paired

long documents, so encoding them is nearly all of the cost. Reuse is 100%:
at about 50% reuse the speedup is 1.95x. And the priming run that filled the
cache is "timed separately and excluded". On Typed Decisions, with short
options, the shared-bank median goes from 93.1 ms to 46.6 ms, about 2x. No
decision changed across cache conditions; the largest probability difference
was 0.00736 (reported).

## VideoJev: one video prefix, many decisions

### The mechanism

VideoJev takes Qwen3.5-4B, and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B for the large model, and
replaces the language-model head with a scorer (measured, from
`modeling_videojev.py` and `config.json`). Two new tokens, `<|option_end|>`
and `<|decide|>`, mark where to read: one after every candidate, one after the
list. Two linear projections map those hidden states to 512 dimensions,
normalise them, and score

$$
s_i = 10\, u^\top v_i + b
$$

with $u$ the decision readout, $v_i$ a candidate, and a fixed scale of 10. The
final transformer layer's MLP is deleted: the code swaps the last decoder layer
for an attention-only one, following an earlier finding that pre-MLP features
carry similarity better. The ablation says removing it costs nothing (0.2
points better on both task means, reported).

This is the pointer family: video first, then the instruction, the query, and
the candidate list in one causal sequence. Two kinds of candidate share the
head:

- **Answer options** for multiple-choice questions, normalised by softmax.
- **Time intervals** for grounding. The backbone already merges frames in
  pairs, so each frame pair becomes one candidate, written as its timestamp
  text. Each gets an independent sigmoid, "is this moment part of the event?",
  and a span is decoded by finding the contiguous run with the largest sum of
  logits shifted by a fixed threshold of 0.25 (Kadane's algorithm, linear in
  the number of intervals).

The card's own example checks against that rule (measured, recomputed from
its printed output). A 10-second clip at 2 fps gives ten interval
probabilities. The fourth through ninth are above 0.83; the tenth is 0.330,
which is above 0.25, so it joins the span; the third is 0.026, which does not.
The decoded interval is 3.0 to 10.0 seconds, as printed.

Parameter counts (measured, from the safetensors headers by range request):
VideoJev-4B is 4,471,106,561 parameters in BF16. Against Qwen3.5-4B's
4,659,865,088, the 120,599,552-parameter multi-token-prediction layer is gone,
so is the last layer's MLP (70,781,440 with its norm), and the head adds
2,622,465. VideoJev-35B-A3B is 33,791,740,273, of which 31,406,948,352 are
experts; its head is 2,098,177. The language backbone is fully fine-tuned;
the vision encoder stays frozen.

### What "interaction" means

The title promises "Video Understanding and Interaction". What the paper
builds is cheaper repeated questions about one video, two ways (reported,
section 2.1):

- **Cached inference** encodes and prefills the video once, then runs each
  question as its own short suffix.
- **Joint inference** puts all the questions after one video prefix and reads
  every `<|decide|>` in a single forward pass. Later questions can attend to
  earlier ones (no answers are inserted), because custom masks would lose
  FlashAttention.

Joint is the default for every headline number, and it scored slightly
better than Cached in 13 of 14 model-and-benchmark pairs. At ten
questions per video, joint forward time is 0.39 s for grounding and 1.64 s for
QA, against 0.85 s and 2.17 s cached (reported). That is the "interaction":
asking about the same footage again costs little. No agent loop is evaluated;
that is listed as future work (measured, from the paper).

One consequence of the pointer design, which the paper handles and does not
measure (reasoned): option order is an input again. Each candidate sees the
ones before it, and in joint mode each question sees the questions before it.
Training applies a fixed random permutation to each question's options to
discourage position shortcuts; inference "keep[s] the supplied order". No
order-sensitivity test is reported, so there is no VideoJev number to set
beside Jeva's zero.

### The numbers, and where it loses

Grounding is the clean win. Mean mIoU over Charades-STA, ActivityNet Captions
and QVHighlights (TimeLens-refined annotations) is 65.3 for VideoJev-4B and
67.7 for 35B-A3B, 6.7 and 10.8 points over their backbones, and 3.3 points
over TimeLens2-4B, the strongest 4B baseline (reported). Gemini-2.5-Pro's
mean is 60.4 in the same table. Per-query latency on all of Charades is 0.276 s
and 0.336 s against the backbones' 0.763 s and 1.054 s: the 2.8x and 3.1x in
the abstract.

- **"Over 100x"** is against Thinking mode: 129x and 100x. Against the
  Instruct backbones, which answer with one letter, VideoJev is 1.21x and
  1.59x faster (reasoned, from the table). The real gain over Instruct is
  accuracy: 8.0 and 5.7 points of mean.
- **It loses to Thinking** on Video-MME and MLVU at both sizes, and on the 4B
  mean (74.3 against 74.9). It wins LVBench at both sizes.
- **It loses to the closed models** where they report: Gemini-3-Pro's 87.7 on
  Video-MME and 76.2 on LVBench, Seed2.0-Pro's 76.4 on LVBench.
- **The architecture helps a little, not a lot.** Fine-tuning plain Qwen3.5-4B
  to generate answers on exactly the same data lands 1.3 points behind on
  grounding mIoU and 1.9 behind on QA (reported, Table 3). Most of the gain
  over the backbone is the data; the head adds about two points.
- **It is overconfident.** Mean ECE over the four QA benchmarks is 8.65
  points for 4B and 8.13 for 35B-A3B (reported). The paper says so directly
  and notes that Jev trains for calibration and VideoJev does not. Confidence
  still ranks well: on LVBench, the 591 answers at 99% confidence or more are
  95.8% right, against 73.4% overall.

## Against the Jev definition

Both deliver the core of a System One model: a probability per option, read
in one pass, nothing to parse. Neither speaks Jev's wire format (reasoned,
from the code). **Jeva** covers `choice`, `noul` and `score` through a
`system_one()` call much like Jev's, and adds the output Jev never had, an
explicit null; the repository has no HTTP server, and llama.cpp's six readouts
have no type for a set reader. **VideoJev** has `choice` and a new type, a time
span, and no `noul` or `score`.

The bigger gap is calibration, which is Jev's whole pitch. Jeva reports Brier
and AUROC but no ECE; VideoJev reports an ECE and says it is too high. Neither
ships a fitted temperature.

## If you are choosing this week

All reasoned:

- **Routing over a big, stable catalogue** (tools, policies, documents):
  Jeva's design is the right shape. Option vectors cache, there is no option
  limit short of memory, null is a real output, and order cannot move the
  answer. Check the licence on the weights first, and measure the speedup on
  your own reuse rate; below 90% it is single digits.
- **Short options, a few at a time**: the cache buys about 2x and the edge
  over Jev on Typed Decisions is 4.2 points, on the authors' run. Worth a test.
- **Questions about video**: VideoJev-4B for grounding is the clearest result
  of the week. For QA, it is Instruct latency with near-Thinking accuracy, but
  fit your own temperature before you threshold on its confidence.

## The take

Jeva gets permutation invariance, a null answer and an option cache from one
choice, encoding options apart, and proves them rather than training for them.
That is why its "zero flips" is unremarkable once you read the code: it could
not have been anything else. Its "20x" is the
honest-but-best case of a cache, long documents fully reused. VideoJev's
contribution is plainer and more immediately useful: pay for the video once,
score time like you score answers, and stop generating timestamps.

Neither published a calibrated model. The architectures are moving faster
than the evidence, again.
