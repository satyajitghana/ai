Every LiDAR pipeline I have shipped downsamples somewhere. A voxel filter before
the network, because the GPU on the robot is a quarter of the one the model was
trained on, or because the latency budget is 80 ms and the scan has 200,000
points. Nobody retrains the segmentation model when they do that. They change
`grid_size` in a config and hope.

[Invaria](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.15923) (Chun-Peng Chang, Shaoxiang Wang,
Alain Pagani, Dariu Gavrila and Holger Caesar, TU Delft and DFKI; arXiv 15 May
2026, accepted at NeurIPS 2026) is a paper about what that hope costs. The
number that made me read it was not theirs. It was in their Table 1, in the
Point Transformer V3 row: trained on ScanNet at a 2 cm grid, PTv3 scores 77.6
mIoU at 2 cm and 20.5 at 6 cm. Three times coarser, three-quarters of the
accuracy gone. Even the denser direction hurts: at 1 cm it scores 50.5.

The paper's claim is that a point-cloud encoder can be built to shrug that off,
and that doing so also makes it smaller and cheaper. I read the paper end to end
and the project page; the code says "coming soon", so where I quote code below
it is the Pointcept implementation of PTv3 that Invaria is built on, not
Invaria's own. The short version: the mechanism is real and well argued, the
robustness numbers are large, and the comparison is set up in a way you should
understand before you quote the 56%.

## The grid size is a unit of measurement

Start with what a modern backbone does to a point cloud before any learning
happens. In Pointcept, which hosts PTv3, Sonata, Concerto and Utonia, the
`GridSample` transform divides every coordinate by the grid size and floors it:

```python
# pointcept/datasets/transform.py:864-865 (Pointcept @ 1342eda)
scaled_coord = data_dict["coord"] / np.array(self.grid_size)
grid_coord = np.floor(scaled_coord).astype(int)
```

From that point on, the network mostly lives in `grid_coord`. The sparse
convolutions count their kernels in cells: the stem is a `SubMConv3d` with
`kernel_size=5` (`point_transformer_v3m1_base.py:506-509`), and every block's
positional encoding is another with `kernel_size=3` (`:285-288`). Pooling
right-shifts the serialized code and the grid coordinates by the stride
(`grid_coord=point.grid_coord[head_indices] >> pooling_depth`, `:429`). The
Pointcept README even notes that replacing precise coordinates with grid
coordinates doesn't hurt accuracy, which is true right up until somebody
changes the grid.

So a 3×3×3 kernel at 2 cm reaches 6 cm of chair. At 6 cm it reaches 18 cm.
The paper writes this as a generic neighbourhood aggregation, where an operator
$\Omega$ pools the features within a fixed kernel $\mathcal{K}$ of grid
distance:

$$
\mathcal{F}_c = \Omega\left(\left\{ f_i \;\middle|\; \frac{d(c,i)}{\tau} \le \mathcal{K} \right\}\right)
$$

The division by $\tau$ is the whole problem. Tripling $\tau$ is, from inside
the network, the same as shrinking every object in the room to a third of its
size. The model was never shown a 15 cm chair, so it doesn't find one.

Their second failure mode is density, and it bites the operators that count
points rather than cells. A $k$-nearest-neighbour group always has $k$ members,
so its physical reach grows as the cloud thins: the paper's Eq. 8 puts the
reach at $\mathcal{R} \propto \tau \cdot \sqrt[3]{k}$. PTv3's serialized
attention is the same story in a different costume. Its patches are 1,024
consecutive points along a space-filling curve (`enc_patch_size=(1024, …)` in
`configs/scannet/semseg-pt-v3m1-0-base.py:23`), so a coarser cloud packs a
bigger chunk of the room into every attention window. A ball query with a fixed
metric radius goes the other way: the reach is fixed and the number of points
inside it collapses.

These are the same ingredients behaving in opposite directions, which is easier
to see than to read. The toy below is a side view of a room, grid-sampled at
the $\tau$ you pick, with a query point on the chair seat.

Push the grid from 2 cm to 6 cm with the ball selected and the seat's
neighbourhood loses more than half its points; the reach doesn't move. Switch
to k-NN and the count is pinned while the circle swells. Switch to the 5×5 grid
kernel and it spans 22% of the chair's width at 2 cm and 65% at 6 cm. Then
leave $\tau$ at 2 and shrink the scene to a third: the grid kernel now spans
65% of the chair again. That coincidence is the paper's point. A coarser grid
and a smaller object are the same event to a grid-native network.

One detail in the paper's appendix is off, and it matters for intuition.
Appendix D says tripling $\tau$ should cut the point count by $3^3$, 27-fold.
That holds for a filled volume. A depth sensor or LiDAR samples surfaces, and a
surface sampled on a 3× coarser grid loses closer to $3^2$, about 9-fold, of its
points. In the 2D toy the scene is made of lines and the count drops with
$\tau$ to the first power: 481 points at 2 cm, 158 at 6 cm. The ScanNet clouds
in the test are thinner than the baseline's training data, but not by 27×.

## What Invaria changes

The paper's own framing is three components: receptive field calibration,
next-resolution prediction, and a latent alignment loss. Having read them, I
think the cleanest way to hold them in your head is that the first one is a
measurement and the other two are training pressure. The measurement fixes
scale. The pressure has to fix density.

### Measure the grid instead of declaring it

Receptive field calibration throws out the configured grid size. Given a cloud
$\mathcal{P}$ of $N$ points, it draws $N_a$ random anchors, finds each anchor's
distance to its nearest distinct neighbour $d_i$, and sets the internal grid to

$$
\psi(\mathcal{P}) = \alpha \cdot \rho\left(\{d_i\}_{i=1}^{N_a}\right)
$$

where $\rho$ is a reduction and $\alpha$ a constant. Algorithm 1 in the appendix
is exactly this, eight lines long. The appendix also explains why $\rho$ is the
mean rather than the minimum, and it is the kind of detail that tells me
somebody tried it. The minimum looks principled, because every pair of points
should land in separate cells. But anchor distances are measured between
arbitrary positions, not cell centres; with 26 neighbouring cells in 3D, two
points either side of a shared face or corner can be nearly touching, so the
minimum comes out far too small.

Calibration buys one thing completely and another not at all. If the whole
scene shrinks by 3× and keeps its points, every nearest-neighbour distance
shrinks by 3×, the measured grid shrinks by 3×, and the grid coordinates come
out identical. Scale invariance by construction. Pick "calibrated grid" in the
toy and step through the three scales: the share of chair the kernel sees
doesn't move.

Now leave the scale alone and resample at 6 cm instead of 2. The mean
nearest-neighbour distance is now about 6 cm, the measured grid is about 6 cm,
and the grid coordinates are exactly as coarse as the baseline's. Calibration
gives a resolution shift no help at all. Whatever robustness Invaria shows at
6 cm has to come from training.

The ablations agree. Without calibration (Table 6a) Invaria still scores 69.7
at 6 cm, but falls to 55.7 at 1 cm. Without next-resolution prediction
(Table 6b) it collapses at the coarse end, to 21.7 at 4 cm and 9.5 at 6 cm,
which is worse than plain PTv3. So: training does the heavy lifting on
resolution, and calibration mainly rescues the denser-than-training direction,
where a declared 2 cm grid would otherwise misjudge a 1 cm cloud.

### Predict the next resolution up

Training sees each scene at several densities. On ScanNet the appendix gives
the subset sizes as $n_0 \cdot h^i$ with $n_0 = 4096$ and $h = 4$, so 4,096,
16,384 and 65,536 points. The model takes the sparser one, encodes it with a
PTv3-style U-Net, and a small **predictor** densifies: it adds points at the
next resolution's real coordinates (or interpolated ones when there are none),
seeds their features from the nearest encoded point, and refines them through a
few blocks of sparse convolution, MLP and norm. The loss is segmentation
(cross-entropy plus Lovász-softmax) on the predicted features at the next
resolution, summed over levels with weights that grow toward the finest
(Table 6d found increasing weights slightly better than uniform):

$$
\mathcal{L}=\sum_{m=1}^{M-1}\lambda^{(m)}\left(\mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}}+\mathcal{L}_{\text{LS}}\right)+\beta\,\mathcal{L}_{\text{align}}
$$

The inspiration they cite is next-scale prediction in image generation, VAR and
Infinity. The point is not to generate points. A model that must produce
correct labels for a denser cloud than it was given cannot get away with
features that only work at one density.

The alignment term pairs each bottleneck token at one resolution with its
spatially nearest token at another and minimises cosine distance between them.
It is the least important of the three: removing it (Table 6e) costs between
0.5 and 0.8 points at every resolution.

## Where the smaller model and the fewer tokens come from

Two efficiency claims ride along: a 45% smaller model and 40% fewer input
tokens. They have different origins and different amounts of evidence.

The model size is the easy one. Invaria's encoder, per Appendix B, has stage
depths (2, 2, 2, 2, 1) with channels (32, 64, 128, 256, 512). PTv3's ScanNet
config has depths (2, 2, 2, 6, 2) with the same channels and heads
(`semseg-pt-v3m1-0-base.py:20-22`). The decoder is identical. So Invaria is
PTv3 with four blocks removed from the 256-channel stage and one from the
512-channel stage.

I counted what that is worth from the Block definition in Pointcept. Each block
holds a 3×3×3 sparse convolution plus a linear layer as positional encoding,
the qkv and output projections, and an MLP with ratio 4: about $40C^2$
parameters at width $C$. Four blocks at 256 channels come to about 10.5M, one
at 512 another 10.5M, so roughly 21.0M of PTv3's 46.1M. The paper's two models
differ by 20.6M (46.1M against 25.5M), which leaves a few hundred thousand
parameters for the predictor. The "45% smaller" is 25.5 against 46.1, and it is
a shallower PTv3. I think it's a sound design choice: Table 6f shows one
bottleneck layer scoring 76.2 against two layers' 76.0. It just isn't a new
kind of efficiency, and the 45% is measured against the smallest transformer
in the table. Against the 39.2M sparse-conv UNet it is 35%.

Invaria-s goes further by swapping in the LitePT design, which keeps attention
to the deep stages and uses convolutions early: 16.3M parameters. Appendix B
calls its FLOPs saving "~7%"; Table 3 lists 230 GFLOPs against Invaria's 267,
which is a 14% saving. One of those is a typo, and I can't tell which.

The token claim is the one I'd most like to see a table for. The mechanism is
the asymmetric inference in panel 4 above: because the model was trained to
produce dense features from a sparse input, at test time the U-Net encodes a
subsample and the predictor fills in the rest. Fewer points into the
transformer stages means fewer tokens, and attention cost grows with tokens.

The paper also argues that sparser input is *better* for the encoder, not just
cheaper, because a fixed-$k$ operator on a dense cloud sees too little of the
room. Figure 6 makes the case visually and PTv3's 50.5 at 1 cm makes it
numerically. But the "average reduction of around 40%" appears in the abstract,
the introduction and one sentence of Section 4, and nowhere as a measurement.
The appendix says the inference subset sizes are "scaled relative to the input
scene size" without giving the ratio. Table 3's FLOPs (267 G for Invaria,
352 G for PTv3) don't state the input size either, so I can't tell whether they
already include the subsampling. I couldn't verify the 40%.

### What the 56% is

The abstract says Invaria "achieves a 56.0% higher mIoU at 3× lower
resolution". The column is $3\tau^*$, a 6 cm grid. The best baseline there is
Utonia at 47.3; Invaria scores 73.8. 73.8 / 47.3 is 1.560, so the 56.0% is a
**relative** gain over the strongest competitor, and the absolute gap is 26.5
mIoU points. Against PTv3 at 20.5 it would be a 3.6× ratio, so they chose the
conservative comparison, which I appreciate.

The scale claim, "a 20% improvement when the objects scale is reduced by a
factor of 3", is Table 5's ×1/3 column: Invaria 73.8 against Concerto-L 61.4,
a 1.20 ratio and 12.4 points. Given what calibration does to a pure rescale,
I'd have expected Invaria's scale curve to be flat by construction, and it is:
73.8 to 76.2 across a 6× range of scales. The flatness is the mechanism working
as designed.

### Who was trained on what

This is where you have to read the footnotes. Every baseline was trained at the
2 cm default and nothing else. Invaria was trained at three densities, a 16×
spread in point count. It would be strange if a model trained across densities
didn't beat models trained at one.

The authors know this and run the obvious control: PTv3 with resolution
augmentation. It loses 2 points at the training resolution (77.6 to 75.6), and
when tested at 1 cm, outside its augmented range, it drops to 56.0. The paper
sets that against Invaria's 76.2, which is its default-grid score; at 1 cm
Invaria scores 75.5, so the point stands. (That 56.0 is a coincidence with the
headline; it's a different number.) I'd have liked that augmented PTv3 as a row
in Table 1 at all four resolutions, because it is the baseline a practitioner
would actually reach for. As it is, it's one sentence of prose.

The three self-supervised models, Sonata, Concerto-L and Utonia, are scored by
**linear probing** on the authors' released weights: frozen encoder, one linear
layer trained on top. It's a legitimate way to measure the representation,
but Pointcept ships decoder-probe and full fine-tune configs for all three, and
a fine-tuned head might degrade differently. PTv3's 77.6 matches, to the
decimal, the ScanNet val number in Pointcept's model zoo, which suggests they
used the released checkpoint there too.

Then there is Figure 7, which I think is the most honest figure in the paper
and the one that most complicates the headline.

The red dashed line is PTv3 trained *at* each resolution and tested there. It
stays around 76 at 6 cm, which is the paper's evidence that the information is
still in the cloud and the baselines are taking a shortcut. It is also evidence
of something else: a PTv3 retrained at 6 cm scores higher at 6 cm than Invaria
does (I read about 76 off the plot; Table 1 has Invaria at 73.8). If you know
your deployment resolution and can afford a training run, retraining the
baseline wins. Invaria's case is the deployment where you can't: one checkpoint
for every sensor, every voxel filter, every latency budget.

Two small things I measured off that figure. The green and orange markers sit
at 71.5 and 62.3 at 4 cm, and at 47.1 and 34.2 at 6 cm, which are Table 1's
Utonia and Sonata values with the labels exchanged. And Invaria's purple marker
at 6 cm reads about 75.0, where Table 1 says 73.8. Neither changes a
conclusion. Table 4 has a similar slip: its Concerto-L row of "relative drops"
is printed positive (Toilet +96.2%) while every other row is negative.

### At the resolution you trained for

Read the second column of Table 1 on its own and Invaria is not the best model
in it. At the default 2 cm grid the order is Concerto-L 78.6, PTv3 77.6, Utonia
76.6, Invaria 76.2, the sparse-conv UNet 75.6, Invaria-s 73.3 and Sonata 72.6.
Fourth of seven, 1.4 points behind the model it is built from. On nuScenes at
the 5 cm default (Table 2) the gap is wider: 76.2 against PTv3's 80.4. Away
from the default Invaria wins everywhere, by a lot; at it, you pay a little.
The abstract's "maintaining high efficiency" is accurate; I wouldn't call the
default-resolution result competitive with the state of the art, and the paper
mostly doesn't either.

### The test where the margin disappears

Appendix Table 7 is the one I'd send to anyone who wants to use this. It changes
density without changing the grid: random subsampling to 10% and 5% of the
points, coordinates still on the 2 cm lattice. At 10%, Invaria scores 64.0.
Concerto-L scores 65.4, Utonia 63.9, PTv3 61.7. At 5% Invaria leads, 48.2
against Utonia's 41.8, but everyone is far below their 2 cm score.

Two things follow. First, Invaria's advantage under a pure density cut is much
smaller than under a grid change. Second, and more useful: PTv3 holds 61.7 at
10% random density but drops to 20.5 when the grid goes to 6 cm, a change that
thins the cloud by a similar order of magnitude. Most of the baseline collapse
in Table 1 is the grid-unit effect from the first section, the model seeing a
shrunken room, rather than missing points. Which suggests a cheap thing to try
on a baseline you already have: downsample however you need to, but compute
`grid_coord` at the grid size the model was trained on. I haven't tested that,
and on a sparse-convolution model the empty cells will hurt, but Table 7 says
it should recover a large share of what Table 1 shows being lost.

## What isn't out yet

The project page links "Code (coming soon)". There are no weights, so nothing
here can be run, and a few numbers I'd need to reproduce the method aren't in
the paper: the anchor count $N_a$, the scale constant $\alpha$, the alignment
weight $\beta$, the per-level weights $\lambda^{(m)}$, the number of predictor
blocks, the subset ratio used at inference, the input size behind Table 3's
FLOPs, and training time. Training used two A100 80GB cards; evaluation one.
The ScanNet results are on the 20-class val split; nuScenes is the only outdoor
set, and the self-supervised baselines appear there only at the default grid.

## Would I use it

For a fixed sensor and a fixed preprocessing chain, no: retrain PTv3 at your
resolution, or fine-tune Concerto, and you'll beat it at that one setting.
For a perception stack that runs one model across sensors with different
densities, or downsamples adaptively to hit a latency target, Invaria is the
first thing I've read that treats the grid as something to measure rather than
a constant baked into training. Calibration alone is worth stealing: it is a
few lines, it makes metric scale a non-issue by construction, and nothing about
it is specific to their training recipe.

The broader point I'll keep is the one the paper makes quietly. A grid-native
point-cloud network does not see centimetres. It sees cells, and the config
file decides how big a cell is. Every time you change that number, you have
shown the model a different world.

If you work on the LiDAR end of this, the odometry side has the same
sensitivity to point density in different clothes: [FAST-LIO2](/articles/fast-lio2-lidar-inertial-odometry)
and [FAR-LIO](/articles/far-lio) both voxel-downsample before registration and
tune that filter by hand. For a scene representation that is resolution-free by
design rather than by training, see [Surflo](/articles/surflo). And the
[first 3D reconstruction roundup](/articles/3d-reconstruction-roundup) is a
companion on reading benchmark tables for what they were measured on.

## How I checked

I read the arXiv HTML of 2605.15923v1 in full, including the appendix, and the
project page; the LinkedIn post linked from the X thread is where both links
came from. The code isn't released, so I shallow-cloned Pointcept at
`1342eda` and read the PTv3 model, the `GridSample` transform and the ScanNet
config for the grid-coordinate behaviour, the baseline depths and the
parameter count. The 56.0% and 20% are recomputed from Tables 1 and 5; the
model-size breakdown is my count of a PTv3 block ($40C^2$ plus norms and biases)
times the blocks Invaria removes. The Figure 7 readings come from locating the
coloured markers in the original 3000-pixel plot and mapping them to the axis,
good to about ±0.3 mIoU. The toy is my own 2D geometry, not the paper's data.
