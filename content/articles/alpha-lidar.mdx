A 16-line spinning LiDAR sees a band. On the Hesai XT16 and RoboSense RS16 that α-LiDAR
uses, the band is about 30 degrees tall, which means the ceiling, the floor near your feet
and anything on a staircase above or below you fall outside it. The usual fixes are a
denser sensor (more lines, more money) or a second sensor. α-LiDAR does something more
mechanical. It mounts the LiDAR on its side on a gimbal motor and turns it, so the 16 beams
sweep the whole sphere, and it lets the motor slow down over the part of the scene you care
about so the returns pile up there.

The paper is ["αLiDAR: An Adaptive High-Resolution Panoramic LiDAR System"](https://doi.org/10.1145/3636534.3690708)
by Jiahe Cui, Yuze He, Jianwei Niu, Zhenchao Ouyang and Guoliang Xing (Beihang University
and the Chinese University of Hong Kong), at ACM MobiCom 2024. CUHK's
[technology page](https://www.innovationhub.hk/article/an-intelligent-lidar-with-adaptive-focus)
lists two awards from that conference for it, Best Artifact and Best Runner-up Demo. Its
abstract makes these claims: centimetre-level pose accuracy at an average latency of 37 ms; 3D
mapping accuracy, coverage and density improved 8.5×, 2× and 1.6× over a conventional
LiDAR; and, from the adaptive rotation, 1.8× the effective sensing distance and 1.9× the
perceived objects.

The paper itself is behind ACM's paywall, and I could not get the full text for this piece,
so everything below about the method comes from the
[repository](https://github.com/Alpha-LiDAR/alpha_lidar) (hardware files, the estimator's
source, its configs and evaluation script, and the maintainers' answers in its issues), the
abstract, and the companion [demo abstract](https://doi.org/10.1145/3636534.3698851). The
figures are the README's, not the paper's.

## Why turn the sensor

A spinning LiDAR already rotates, about its own axis, ten times a second. Its 16 lasers are
fanned out vertically, so each revolution traces 16 cones around that axis. Everything
between the top and bottom cone is sampled; everything outside is never seen, however long
you wait. Mount the sensor upright and the blind zone is above and below you.

Now lay the sensor on its side and turn it slowly about a second axis at right angles to
its spin axis. Each revolution is now a vertical fan, and the motor carries that fan around.
Within a second or so of motor rotation the fan has passed through every direction. Nothing
about the laser changed: the same returns per second are spread over the whole sphere
instead of a band.

The repo's configs fix that geometry. `extrinsic_R_encoder_lidar` in
`rs16_rotation_v2.yaml` is a 90-degree turn to within a quarter of a degree: the LiDAR's spin
axis is perpendicular to the motor's.

<HeadCoverage />

Spreading the same returns over more sky has a cost, which the widget shows if you drag the
window down: in any short window, a given direction is hit less often than in the band of an
upright sensor. That is where the "adaptive" in the title comes in. If the motor swings back
and forth over a sector instead of turning steadily, the returns concentrate there. The
abstract's 1.8× sensing distance and 1.9× perceived objects come from that adaptive mode,
where the system optimises the rotation trajectory toward regions of interest. I come back to
what the repo does and does not contain for that part below.

## The rig

The bill of materials is short. A LiDAR that accepts PPS and GPRMC time sync (the README
lists the Hesai Pandar XT-16, with the RoboSense RS16, Livox Mid-360 and Livox Horizon as
alternatives), a Yesense YIS100 IMU, and a DJI RoboMaster GM6020 gimbal motor, which has its
encoder built in. The mechanical parts are 3D-printed in PLA+ (STEP files in
`hardware/Mechanical/`). Two small PCBs carry the electronics: a control board on the head
with an STM32F103 that gathers the sensors onto one gigabit Ethernet cable, and a host
interface that splits that cable back into 100 Mbit Ethernet for the LiDAR and USB for the
rest. Everything runs off 24 V.

Two details matter for the software. The IMU rides on the head with the LiDAR, not on the
base, so it measures the motor's rotation directly. And the encoder's angle reaches the host
through the same path as the IMU. A maintainer laid out that path in
[issue #13](https://github.com/Alpha-LiDAR/alpha_lidar/issues/13): motor, CAN, the STM32
(which "timestamps and packs the angle packets together with adjacent IMU packets"), USART,
the host, and a serial driver that publishes `/imu/data` and `/imu/encoder`.

The repo does not have everything a builder needs. The firmware is a `.hex` file with no
source ([issue #9](https://github.com/Alpha-LiDAR/alpha_lidar/issues/9)), and
[issue #16](https://github.com/Alpha-LiDAR/alpha_lidar/issues/16) lists other gaps: the
slip ring's part number, a component list for the PCBs, and the Livox mounts. One builder
found the motor's 18 mm hollow shaft too narrow for a 22 mm slip ring. The serial driver that
turns the STM32's stream into ROS topics is not in the repo either
([issue #17](https://github.com/Alpha-LiDAR/alpha_lidar/issues/17)).

## What turning does to a sweep

A spinning LiDAR takes 100 ms to complete one revolution, and it fires continuously during
it. If the head is also being turned by the motor, each return is measured from a slightly
different orientation. Stack a sweep as if the sensor had held still and every return is off
by the angle the head turned between its firing and the end of the sweep. At range $r$, a
return fired $\Delta t$ before the end, with the head turning at $\omega$, lands
$\omega \, \Delta t \, r$ from where it should be. At 3 rad/s that is 17 degrees per sweep,
and a wall 8 m away gets smeared by more than two metres.

<SpinDeskew />

This is the same problem a handheld LiDAR has when you swing it, only constant and larger,
and α-LiDAR solves it the same way [FAST-LIO2](/articles/fast-lio2-lidar-inertial-odometry)
does. Integrate the IMU through the sweep, keep the pose at every IMU sample, and move each
return to the end-of-sweep pose with the pose at its own firing time. Because the IMU is on
the head, the motor's turn is just more angular velocity; nothing in the filter needs to know
a motor exists. `IMU_Processing.hpp`'s `UndistortPcl` is FAST-LIO's backward propagation with
the same structure.

The one motor-shaped guard is in the IMU callback. A sample with any gyro axis above
10 rad/s is dropped as a glitch:

```cpp
if (std::abs(msg->angular_velocity.x) > 10
    || std::abs(msg->angular_velocity.y) > 10
    || std::abs(msg->angular_velocity.z) > 10) {
```

Ten radians a second is 573 degrees per second, so the head may turn fast, but not that
fast.

## The estimator is PV-LIO with two changes

I asked myself early on what the "uncertainty modelling of the rotation process" in the
abstract would turn out to be in code. A maintainer answered the question directly in
[issue #12](https://github.com/Alpha-LiDAR/alpha_lidar/issues/12): "PV-LIO is an
under-developing version of αLiDAR". The two updates over PV-LIO are "non-static
initialization for rapid rotation scenarios" and "uncoupled motor encoder fusion". The
`state_estimation` package's `voxelMapping.cpp` is that PV-LIO lineage: an iterated
error-state Kalman filter on a manifold (the IKFoM toolkit, as in FAST-LIO2), with the map
held in a VoxelMap-style octree of planes instead of FAST-LIO2's ikd-Tree.
[PV-LIO](/articles/pv-lio) has its own article on this site, measured on the same
sequences.

The state is position, rotation, the LiDAR-to-IMU rotation and translation, velocity, gyro
and accelerometer biases, and gravity on the 2-sphere: 23 degrees of freedom. The prediction
is the IMU, as in [the Kalman filter article](/articles/kalman-filter). The update is where it
differs from FAST-LIO2.

### Every return carries its own covariance

Each LiDAR return gets a 3×3 covariance in the sensor frame from two numbers in the config,
a range noise and an angular noise (`ranging_cov` 0.04 m and `angle_cov` 0.1 degrees for
the RS16 rig). Along the beam it is $\sigma_r^2$; across it, $r^2 \sigma_\theta^2$, so far
returns are less certain sideways. That covariance is then pushed through the
LiDAR-to-IMU extrinsic and the current pose estimate, picking up the filter's own rotation
uncertainty on the way:

$$
\Sigma_w = R\,\Sigma_b\,R^\top + R\,[\mathbf p_b]_\times\,\Sigma_\theta\,[\mathbf p_b]_\times^\top R^\top + \Sigma_t
$$

where $\Sigma_b$ is the return's covariance in the IMU body frame, $\mathbf p_b$ its
position there, $\Sigma_\theta$ and $\Sigma_t$ the rotation and translation blocks of the
filter's covariance, and $R$ the body's rotation. The middle term is what a spinning head
makes large: an attitude error of $\sigma_\theta$ moves a return by about
$r\,\sigma_\theta$, so a return 20 m away is twenty times less certain than one at 1 m.
In the code, `transformLiDARCovToWorld` does it in two stages (LiDAR to body with the
extrinsic's covariance, then body to world):

```cpp
M3D COV_world =
    state.rot * COV_body * state.rot.conjugate()
    + state.rot * (-point_crossmat) * rot_var * (-point_crossmat).transpose()  * state.rot.conjugate()
    + t_var;
```

The map side is VoxelMap's: space is cut into voxels (0.25 m, two extra octree levels for the
indoor configs; 2 m and four levels outdoors), each voxel fits a plane with its own 6×6
covariance on normal and centre, and splits into eight when its points are not planar
enough. A return is matched to a plane only if its distance is within 3σ of the combined
uncertainty, and its row in the update is weighted by

$$
w = \frac{1}{\mathbf J_{nq}\,\Sigma_{\text{plane}}\,\mathbf J_{nq}^\top + \mathbf n^\top R\,\Sigma_L\,R^\top \mathbf n}
$$

so a return near the sensor hitting a well-measured wall counts more than a far return
grazing a thin plane. One detail I did not expect: the matching gate uses the world-frame
covariance, with the pose uncertainty in it, while the weight in the observation model uses
only the LiDAR's own noise rotated into the world. FAST-LIO2, by contrast, gives every
point-to-plane residual the same fixed variance.

### Starting while the head is already spinning

FAST-LIO2 and most of its descendants initialise from a short static window: average the
accelerometer to find gravity, average the gyro to find its bias. On α-LiDAR the head may
be turning from the first sample, and averaging a gyro that reads the motor's rate gives
you the motor's rate as "bias". In [issue #1](https://github.com/Alpha-LiDAR/alpha_lidar/issues/1),
a user reported that FAST-LIO2, Point-LIO and iG-LIO all failed to run on the released
data, and the maintainer pointed at this: the head rotates "from the beginning", and
α-LiDAR has a non-static initialisation.

The non-static start is short. With `init_gravity_with_pose: true`, the filter takes its
initial attitude from the orientation the YIS100 reports in each IMU message (the IMU runs
its own attitude estimate), sets gravity to straight down in that frame, and starts the gyro
bias at zero:

```cpp
init_state.rot = SO3(
        meas.imu.back()->orientation.w,
        meas.imu.back()->orientation.x,
        meas.imu.back()->orientation.y,
        meas.imu.back()->orientation.z);
init_state.grav = S2(0, 0, -G_m_s2);
```

So the "non-static initialisation" leans on a sensor that already knows which way is down.
A plain 6-axis IMU without an orientation output cannot use this path.

### The encoder does not enter the filter

In October 2024 I replied in that same thread that other LIO systems would also need the
encoder data fused into their state estimation to run on these bags, and linked the encoder
block in `voxelMapping.cpp`. Reading the code properly
for this article, α-LiDAR itself does not do that. The encoder never reaches the Kalman
filter. `uncoupled_encoder_fusion` runs after the update: it slerps the two encoder samples
around the scan time, and composes the head's estimated pose with the encoder angle and the
head-to-motor extrinsic to give the pose of the motor's base, the thing the head is mounted
on:

```cpp
SO3 R_odom_o_base = R_odom_o_lidar * R_lidar_o_encoder * R_encoder_o_base;
V3D t_odom_o_base = R_odom_o_lidar * t_lidar_o_encoder + t_odom_o_lidar;
```

That pose is published on `/Encoder` for whoever carries the sensor (a robot that wants its
own pose, not the spinning head's). The odometry itself is computed from the IMU and LiDAR
alone. "Uncoupled" in the maintainer's phrase is accurate: the motor angle is a frame change,
not a measurement. The consequence for anyone porting this: you can run the estimator
without the encoder and lose nothing in the head's trajectory.

Two things are worth knowing about those numbers before quoting them. The ATE there is not
the usual one. `evaluate_segmented_local_ate` cuts the estimate into windows of 200 poses,
steps 100 poses at a time, aligns each window to the ground truth on its own, and reports the
mean of each window's mean error. That is a local-consistency number, much kinder than one
alignment over the whole run, and it is what "centimetre-level" refers to as far as the
released code can show. And the "vertical FoV" is computed by `logger.py` as the spread of
elevation angles in each scan after rotating it into the map frame, front and back halves
summed, so it can reach 360 degrees when the fan sweeps through the vertical.

## The data, and why I could not run it

The README links three recordings: `alpha_lidar_large_indoor.bag` (corridors and reflective

When I went to fetch them on 21 November 2026, the Mega folder was empty. Mega's API returns
one node for the link, the `datasets` folder itself, and nothing in it; the same request
against another public folder returns its files. The Baidu share still lists all five files,
2.21 GB, 3.35 GB and 3.56 GB of bags and two small reference files (26 KB and 14 KB, so a
sparse reference, a few hundred poses at most), but Baidu only serves downloads to a
logged-in client. So I could not run α-LiDAR on α-LiDAR's own data for this article. What I

## Building it without ROS

The estimator ships as a ROS 1 catkin package: `voxelMapping.cpp` subscribes to three topics,
publishes a dozen, and runs its loop on `ros::spinOnce()`. I wanted to run it the way the
site's other LiDAR-inertial articles now run everything, with no ROS installed at all, on the
same converted sequences and through the same evaluation as the
[FAST-LIO2](/articles/fast-lio2-lidar-inertial-odometry), [PV-LIO](/articles/pv-lio) and
[Voxel-SLAM](/articles/voxel-slam) runs.

Instead of editing the estimator, I wrote the slice of ROS it touches. A directory of headers
carries ROS's names (`ros/ros.h`, `sensor_msgs/Imu.h`, `nav_msgs/Odometry.h`,
`tf/transform_broadcaster.h`, `livox_ros_driver/CustomMsg.h` and so on) and defines them as
plain structs. A cloud travels as a `pcl::PCLPointCloud2`, so `pcl::fromROSMsg` becomes
`pcl::fromPCLPointCloud2`. `ros::NodeHandle::param` reads a flat file generated from the
shipped YAML. `subscribe` records the callback; `ros::spinOnce()` reads the next record from
the converted sequence, in the order a bag player would deliver it (IMU samples at their
stamps, a sweep once its last return has fired), and calls that callback. `Publisher::publish`
hands the message to a hook that writes the pose, the timing and the registered cloud. The
estimator's own `main()` runs unchanged on top of this.

The upstream sources compile as shipped with one edit: the latency log goes to the run's
output directory instead of a fixed `/tmp/latency.csv`, which parallel runs would share. The
`MP_PROC_NUM` thread count, a compile-time constant upstream, is read at run time. The same shim
builds FAST-LIO2's `laserMapping.cpp` unmodified, which gave me a baseline on the synthetic test
below through exactly the same pump.

## A spinning head with exact truth

Since α-LiDAR's own recordings were out of reach, I built a recording where the answer is
known. `synth.py` in the port puts a 16-beam LiDAR (rings every 2 degrees from −15 to +15, 10
sweeps a second) and a 200 Hz IMU on one head, inside a 12 × 9 × 3.5 m room with two boxes. The
head stands still for 2 s, ramps smoothly up to a 1.5 m circle at 0.6 rad/s with a slow yaw on
top, and, on top of that, spins about its own x axis at a fixed rate from the first sample:
the α-LiDAR situation, with the head already turning when the recording starts. The IMU readings
are exact derivatives of the trajectory and there is no noise, so whatever error comes out is
the estimator's. Each run is 30 s and 24.3 m of path, scored by one SE(3) alignment over the
whole run.

| Head spin | α-LiDAR, non-static init (shipped setting) | α-LiDAR, static init | FAST-LIO2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| none | 2.5 cm | 2.5 cm | 3.7 cm |
| 1.5 rad/s | 3.0 cm | aborted, 0 poses | diverged (1,418 m) |
| 3 rad/s | 5.6 cm | aborted, 0 poses | diverged (1,158 m) |
| 6 rad/s | 2.7 cm | aborted, 0 poses | diverged (1,184 m) |

With the non-static start, α-LiDAR tracks the spinning head to a few centimetres at every rate
I tried, up to 6 rad/s, which is 344 degrees a second and 34 degrees inside a single sweep. Both
alternatives fail, and for the same reason. A static initialisation averages the first IMU
samples and calls the mean gyro reading "bias". On a head that is already turning, that mean is
the motor's rate. The filter subtracts it from every later sample, believes the head is still,
and its gyro-bias random walk (`b_gyr_cov` of 2.66 × 10⁻⁶ in the shipped config) is far too
tight to walk 1.5 rad/s back out. FAST-LIO2 drifts off by kilometres. α-LiDAR with the static
path does not get that far: the process aborts within the first scans. Its CMake keeps
assertions on (`-O3 -g`, no `NDEBUG`), and I built it the same way, so a NaN reaching one of
IKFoM's checks stops it. I did not capture which assertion fired, and this is my ROS-free build,
not the authors' ROS one, but the code path is theirs and the failure mode is the one issue #1
describes.

The test is kind to the non-static path in one way: my orientation column is exact, where a real
YIS100 reports an attitude with its own error. The 5.6 cm at 3 rad/s is the worst of the four
and not monotonic with rate, so I read the four numbers as "a few centimetres", not as a trend.

## A real motorised recording, without a reference

I looked for any public recording of an actuated LiDAR with an IMU on the head and a ground
truth. I did not find one. The closest is the test bag that ships with
[LiMo-Calib](https://github.com/kafeiyin00/LiMo-Calib) (MIT), a LiDAR-motor calibration method
from Jianping Li's group: a Livox Mid-360 turned by a motor, with the Mid-360's own IMU on the
turning head, 120 s, 445.5 MB. There is no reference trajectory, so I can only check that a
system keeps the head in one place and builds a sharp map. The rig itself stays put; only the
motor moves, at about 3.6 rad/s once it starts, and the gyro averages 0.033 rad/s in the first
second, so this recording starts still.

| On LiMo-Calib's bag | α-LiDAR (its `mid360.yaml`) | FAST-LIO2 |
|---|---|---|
| poses | 1,196 | 1,196 |
| head position extent over 120 s | 3 × 6 × 5 cm | 5 × 9 × 6 cm |
| start-to-end gap | 2.8 cm | 5.6 cm |
| flat-patch RMS in the map (median, 1 m cells) | 17.4 mm | 18.5 mm |
| ms per scan (serial, 4 threads) | 30.8 | 2.5 |
| peak memory | 1.7 GB | 192 MB |

Both systems hold the spinning head within a few centimetres for two minutes and build maps of
the same sharpness. FAST-LIO2 does fine here because the recording starts still, which supports
the reading of the synthetic table: what breaks a standard LIO on a turning head is the start,
not the turning. α-LiDAR pays about twelve times FAST-LIO2's time per scan for its plane octree
and per-point covariances. For the extrinsic I used Livox's published IMU offset
(+0.04412 m in z); α-LiDAR's shipped `mid360.yaml` has −0.04412. I did not run both to see
whether the sign matters on this bag.

## The estimator with the motor taken away

The last test asks whether α-LiDAR's estimator is a good LIO on an ordinary fixed sensor. It
went through the same harness as every system in the [LIO shootout](/articles/lio-shootout):
the same converted Hilti 2022, Oxford Spires and Newer College sequences, frames, SE(3)
alignment without scale, and divergence rule, on the same 28-core machine. These rows are not
α-LiDAR: no motor, no encoder, no non-static start (the shared IMU stream has no orientation),
and α-LiDAR's shipped Hesai XT16 configs (indoor for the Hilti rooms, outdoor for Oxford and
Newer College) with only the line count, scan rate and extrinsic changed for each sensor.

| Sequence | α-LiDAR estimator | FAST-LIO2, same machine | PV-LIO, same machine | α ms/scan |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hilti exp14 basement, 38 m | 4.43 cm | 11.5 cm | 3.95 cm | 5.0 |
| Oxford Spires Keble, 283 m | 4.46 cm | 6.2 cm | 10.9 cm | 21.7 |
| Newer College park, 2.4 km | 27.1 cm | 28.7 cm | 30.0 cm | 61.2 (parallel pass) |
| Hilti exp16 stairwells, 117 m | aborted at 29.5 s (7.7 cm until then) | diverged | 1.74 m | n/a |

FAST-LIO2's row is its indoor variant from the shootout. The FAST-LIO2 article's earlier runs on
another machine (beast2) had 7.2 cm on Oxford and 50.1 cm on the Hilti basement with its
shipped 0.5 m map. Timings are from a serial pass pinned to four cores at four threads, except
Newer College, which comes from the parallel accuracy pass.

On a fixed sensor, the estimator is a strong LIO: best of the three on Oxford, within half a
centimetre of PV-LIO in the basement, and level with the others over the long park loop, where
all three drift by roughly 30 cm without loop closure. It is also lighter on memory than PV-LIO
where both map outdoors (1.0 GB against 8.7 GB on Oxford). On the stairwell sequence it aborted
29.5 s in, the same assertion stop as the static-init runs; PV-LIO, its parent, finishes that
sequence at 1.74 m.

## Is it worth building

I think the idea is right and the software is better than its framing. Turning a cheap 16-line
LiDAR is the cheapest way to see a ceiling, a staircase or the ground at your feet, and the code
that makes it work is a careful PV-LIO with one change that matters, a start that does not
assume the sensor is still. That change is small. It also leans on an IMU that reports its own
attitude, so if you build the head with a bare 6-axis IMU you need another way to start, or you
start the motor after the filter has initialised, as LiMo-Calib's recording happens to do.

What I would not do is quote the paper's "centimetre-level" next to other LIO numbers. The
repo's evaluation aligns 200-pose windows one at a time, which hides drift by construction. On
the runs I could score with one alignment, α-LiDAR's estimator lands between 2.5 and 5.6 cm on a
spinning synthetic head and about 4.4 cm on two real fixed-sensor walks. That is good, and close
to the claim, but it is my measurement, not the paper's, on my data, not theirs.

For someone building one: the mechanical and PCB files are enough to start, the firmware is not
open, and the released data has gone missing from Mega. If you have a motor and a LiDAR, the
estimator itself runs as is, and it builds without ROS.

## Notes on what I ran and read

Ran, on a 28-core Intel i7-14700 box with no GPU use: the ROS-free build of α-LiDAR's
`state_estimation` at commit 51374c4c and of FAST-LIO2 at 7cc4175d, the synthetic spinning-head
test, LiMo-Calib's bag, and the four shootout sequences through the shared harness. The port
(header shim, offline pump, configs, evaluation) is in the shootout's `systems/alpha-lidar`
directory. Read, not run: the paper (paywalled; abstract and demo abstract only), α-LiDAR's own
three recordings (Mega folder empty, Baidu needs a login), and the adaptive scan-trajectory
optimisation behind the 1.8× and 1.9× claims, which is not in the released code.
