Eight posts from 15 to 18 November sit in the part of the field I work in: a site gets captured with a phone, a 360 camera, a drone or a LiDAR, and something useful has to come out the other end. My own pipeline starts from Insta360 dual-fisheye walks and ends, when it works, in walls, slabs and openings that compile to IFC and Revit. So for each post I asked the same three questions. What does it hand you: a drawing, structured data, a mesh or splat you look at, a pose, or a massing model? How does it get there, read from code where there is code? And what would break on a real site?

Two of the eight have open code: rsasaki0109's LiDAR localizer and the Claude skill behind kotohibi's scene. One is an agent demo on a synthetic cloud. Three are products with pages I could read. Two are videos with nothing else behind them, and I keep those short.

## What a point cloud says about a wall

Every scan-to-drawing claim rests on one move: cut the cloud with a horizontal plane about a metre above the floor and read the cut as a plan. It works better than people expect for some things and not at all for others, so it is worth being exact about which.

The face of a wall is easy. A wall a few metres long collects hundreds of points in a 20 mm strip, and their mean lands within a millimetre or two of the true face even when each point carries 10 or 20 mm of noise, because averaging divides the noise by the square root of the count. Position and direction of a wall face are the most reliable numbers a scan gives you.

Thickness is not in the cloud unless both faces were scanned and the two scans were registered to each other. An office scanned from the inside has the inside face of every exterior wall and nothing else. The 250 mm you see on an as-built drawing came from a default, an old drawing, or someone measuring at a window reveal.

Openings are measured at whatever the scanner saw. Glass returns almost nothing, so a window shows up as a gap in the wall points, bounded by the frame. The gap is the glass between the frames, which is narrower than the rough opening the wall actually has. And a slice only finds an opening if it passes between the sill and the head.

Heights need a second cut, or a section. Above a certain height a plan slice through an office meets a downstand beam, and a beam and a short wall (a hanging wall, 垂れ壁 in Japanese drafting) look identical in a slice: a face some distance proud of the wall, from somewhere up to the ceiling. Furniture against a wall hides it at desk height.

## A scan of an office, drawn in Jw_cad by an agent

[akionionio](https://x.com/akionionio/status/2122097401846726893), who runs a construction-industry AI and outsourcing company, posted a 57-second video of an agent turning one office point cloud into a Jw_cad as-built plan: walls, columns and openings read from the cloud, beam soffit heights found ("2,350 only at the window side"), a 3D model built in Blender from those dimensions, and then Jw_cad driven to produce one A3 sheet at 1/50. It also noticed a part of the entrance that had not been scanned, asked for it to be scanned again, and could then read the door.

The thread reply matters more than the post. The cloud is **mock data made for the demo**: an iPhone LiDAR walk was simulated, with a few millimetres of noise, gaps behind furniture and missing window glass added, and neither the office nor the drawing exists. The reading was done by "a separate AI that did not know the answer", given only the point cloud file. Clear dimensions, columns, beam soffits and doors matched the answer; window widths came out about 90 mm small because it measured inside the frame. Places it could not read from the cloud (beam or hanging wall, the inside of a private room) were left on the drawing marked 要現地確認, "confirm on site". The Jw_cad on screen is version 8.01b running under Wine on a Mac, shown at 8x speed.

What it produces is a real drawing: a JWW file with walls, hatching, door symbols, dimension strings and notes, in the CAD package Japanese contractors actually use. It is not BIM. The Blender model is an intermediate, used to check the reading in 3D. Nothing here is an element with a type and a host; the next step for an IFC file would be another program entirely.

I would copy two habits from it. The agent flagged missing coverage while the scan could still be fixed, which is the most expensive failure of scan-to-BIM and usually discovered weeks later in the office. And unreadable items stayed on the drawing as explicit site checks, the same instinct as the evidence ledger in the [image-to-DWG skill](/articles/dwg-redraw-skill), where every value is tagged known, scaled, inferred or unreadable.

Where it would break is the cloud. The [floor-plan-to-3D roundup](/articles/floor-plan-to-3d-roundup) sorted four tools by whether they build elements or pictures; this one builds a drawing, and from the easiest input there is. A clean simulated cloud with "a few millimetres" of noise and a known answer is the best case. A real iPhone walk of a 93 m² office drifts, doubles walls where the loop closes badly, and smears glass and dark surfaces. The [Mid-360-on-an-iPhone rig](/articles/mobile-slam-iphone) exists because the phone's own LiDAR runs out at a few metres. No code is published. What it shows is a convincing demo of the reading and drafting steps on a cloud that cannot fail the way real ones do. The author calls it a starting draft for a site survey (現調のたたき台). That is the right claim.

## Inform: one phone video to a GLA report

[Sadao Tokuyama's post](https://x.com/tokufxug/status/2122871106457760018) summarised [Inform](https://inform.re), an American spatial-AI start-up for real estate: one smartphone video of a building, rooms, dimensions and fixtures recognised, "hundreds of items" structured to the UAD 3.6 appraisal data standard, every value tied to an evidence image, uncertain values checked by a person. The primary source he linked is a LinkedIn post by the founder, a residential appraiser in Pennsylvania.

Inform's own pages say less and more. The product that ships is Inform Scan, on iPhone and Android in the US: a few minutes of walking, then an ANSI Z765-2021 aligned floor plan and a GLA report (gross living area, the number an appraisal turns on) delivered within 24 hours, or 6 hours for \$5 more. A GLA report is \$10 per property and a marketing floor plan \$12. The site says "LiDAR is not required". The sample report is the part I trust most: the GP-18 example shows 1,681 sq ft above grade and 703 finished below grade on its own line, with every shape's area listed to one decimal so the total can be checked by hand.

The UAD 3.6 piece is a second product, Cortex, which is in early access. Its page says it pre-populates "structured, machine-readable property data" for the new standard, matches photos to the standard's photo requirements, and stamps each photo with its time and its location in the scan so you can "trace it back to the scan before you sign off". The home page does say a video becomes "hundreds of structured data points". What I could not find anywhere on the site is the post's line about uncertain values going to a person, or any accuracy figure. The phrasing of the delivery step ("We draw the plan", with a 24-hour turnaround) suggests drafting with people in the loop, but the site does not say how much of it is.

Inform also lists Video to BIM, "coming soon": walls, floors, openings and rooms exported as IFC from an iPhone, an Android phone, plain video or 360 video. That is the one I would want to test.

For a scan-to-BIM reader the interesting tension is in the standard itself. ANSI Z765 measures GLA to the exterior finished surface of the walls. A phone walked through the inside of a house sees the inside face (the widget above, with the outside scan off). So every GLA number from an interior walk adds an exterior wall thickness that was not measured. Take one storey of about 156 m² (1,681 sq ft) as a square: its perimeter is about 50 m, and 50 mm of error in the assumed thickness all the way round is 2.5 m², about 27 sq ft. That is my arithmetic on a made-up house, not Inform's figure. That is fine for an appraisal if the method is stated. It is the first thing I would ask Inform.

## Gauzilla Pro: 4D timelapses from drone and 360 video

[Gauzilla Pro](https://x.com/GauzillaPro/status/2123012340497162676) showed construction sites as Gaussian-splat scenes you scrub through in time: several drone or 360 videos, each turned into a splat, stacked as a 4D record so a team can see progress without a site visit. The product runs in a browser. A free renderer of the same name, [Gauzilla](https://github.com/BladeTransformerLLC/gauzilla), is open source (Rust compiled to WebAssembly, MIT); the Pro platform is commercial, and I could not confirm how much of the open renderer it contains.

What it hands you is a picture of the site at each date, navigable in 3D. There are no elements in it and no measurements were shown. The hard part of a 4D record is the part the post does not describe: putting every epoch in the same frame. Each splat comes out of its own structure-from-motion solve with its own scale and origin, and a site changes between flights, which is the point of flying it. Registration has to lean on what did not move (neighbouring buildings, roads, survey targets) and has to be checked, or the tower appears to sway between dates. My shell could not load Gauzilla's site; the product page, as a fetch tool summarised it for me, does not describe how epochs are aligned or how well. The [last reconstruction roundup](/articles/3d-reconstruction-roundup-5) comes at that problem from the other end: a bundle-adjustment engine is what puts many captures into one frame.

For progress tracking that ends in a schedule, a picture per date is the first half. The second half compares each epoch with the model of what should be there, element by element. That needs either BIM elements on one side or segmentation on the other, and neither is in this release.

What I could read is the method. The author's [ai-wide-area-modeling](https://github.com/Kotohibi/ai-wide-area-modeling), a Claude Code skill under MIT, went up on 14 November; I read it at commit 2773722, pushed this morning. It is the most careful description of agent modelling from capture I have read. The input is a COLMAP-format solve (poses, photos, points), from a 360 camera via Metashape and the author's own cubemap converter. The agent sets up a metric, Z-up frame; reads dimensions from plan and section plots of the cloud with a 1 m grid drawn on them; triangulates thin things from two or more photos; and keeps every dimension in a JSON file that generator scripts turn into Blender geometry through Blender MCP. Checking is built in: photo, splat render and Blender render side by side from the same camera, the 3D distance from cloud to model, and a scale check.

The skill is candid about the weak spot. Scale from SfM or a splat is arbitrary, so it comes from known dimensions: stair risers, camera height, lane widths. The temple recipe used parking-bay lines, 2.3 to 2.5 m apart, and notes that a 360 camera on a pole sits above 2 m, so the default 1.6 m camera height made everything about 25% too small. That one line is worth more than most papers on the subject.

What comes out is a model of appearance: roofs as height functions, tile rows as a sine added to the surface, brackets as stacked boxes. It is a good, light, browser-ready mesh. It is not a building model in the BIM sense, and it does not try to be. The earlier shrine from the same author, which I wrote about in the [agent 3D and BIM tools roundup](/articles/agent-3d-bim-tools), was scored against its own dense cloud; this skill makes that check routine. Its pipeline front half is the one in the [360-to-splat pipelines piece](/articles/3dgs-capture-pipelines).

## A LiDAR localizer that finds itself on a map

[Ryohei Sasaki](https://x.com/rsasaki0109/status/2122889467069698410) posted a video of his open-source [lidar_localization_ros2](https://github.com/rsasaki0109/lidar_localization_ros2) (BSD-2-Clause) localizing a Unitree Go2 on an existing map with no initial pose: "the robot searches the whole map and finds where it is", 4.5 s after the search started, within about 2 cm. The replies corrected the sensor (a Livox MID-360 mounted upside down, not the Go2's own LiDAR) and asked the right question.

### Why the search is the hard part

Tracking a pose on a map is local: start from where you were, predict the motion, match the new scan with NDT or ICP from that seed, and the optimiser only has to slide a few centimetres. Finding a pose with no seed is global. The unknowns are x, y and heading over the whole map, and a scan matcher started far from the answer converges to the nearest wrong one. Brute force over a 200 m map at 0.2 m cells and 5° steps is a million cells times 72 headings, each scored against hundreds of points.

The repo does it in two stages, and its author described them in a reply: "a coarse branch-and-bound search over a 2D grid made from the 3D map, then checks the top candidates with NDT against the 3D map. An answer is accepted only when two searches agree after the robot moves."

The first stage is in `include/lidar_localization/bbs_branch_and_bound.hpp`, a pure C++ port of the Python search that the header says is bit-exact against frozen fixtures. The map becomes an occupancy grid (`occupancy_map_generation.hpp`: 0.2 m cells, a cell occupied where its points rise at least 0.4 m above its lowest point, occupied cells inflated by 0.6 m). The scan is flattened to the points in a height band (0.5 to 5 m above the base by default), voxelised and capped at 512. A pose's score is the fraction of scan points that land on occupied cells.

The trick that makes it fast is a bound. Build a pyramid by max-pooling the grid 2x2 at a time, four levels deep. At level L one cell covers a 2^L by 2^L block of translations. Dilate each coarse level by one cell and the number of scan points that hit it can only be larger than for any finer pose inside the block, so it is an upper bound. Push every top-level block for every heading into a priority queue keyed by that bound, always expand the most promising block, and stop as soon as the best remaining bound cannot beat the k-th best full-resolution score. Whole regions of the map are discarded without ever being scored cell by cell. It is the same idea as the branch-and-bound scan matcher in Google's Cartographer, applied to the whole map.

The second stage scores the top candidates with NDT against the full 3D map, which is where most of the query time goes according to `docs/global_localization.md`. The third is the consensus: query, let the robot move, query again, and accept only if the first answer, moved by the odometry, lands on the second. Until then nothing is published. If tracking is lost later, the localizer stops publishing and raises a reinitialization request rather than holding an old pose; the author confirmed both in a reply.

<RelocSearch />

The widget runs the same search, ported line by line, on a floor of my own: two identical rooms over a corridor with one pillar at the east end. With identical rooms the two rooms score exactly the same, and the header's tie-break (score, then heading, then y, then x) puts the west room first, wrong. The second query from the corridor sees the pillar and lands somewhere the first answer cannot have moved to, so the pair is rejected. Put a cabinet in the map and one query is enough. Move within the room instead, and the second query sees only the room, ties the same way, and agrees with the first: a wrong pose that passes the check.

### Holding the post to the repository

The post's video says in its footer that the map was built from the same recording as the scans. A reply from JuiceEng pointed out that this flatters the result, and Sasaki agreed ("I overlooked that since an AI built the demo") and said he would redo it. He did, the same day: a commit about eight hours after that reply replaced the README's animation, and the README at commit 285070f opens with a cross-session run: the map from the JEPLO run Long_Stairs, the scans from Outdoor2, recorded 1.5 hours later along the same route. Each query returns 32 ranked places in 2.6 s, and the first pose came 13.3 s into the run, 6.4 s after the first search, 0.01 m from the motion-capture truth.

The benchmark in `docs/benchmark.md` is where the repo earns trust, because it prints its failures. Across 24 cross-session runs on JEPLO, 23 initialised and 5 started more than 1 m from the truth. On the EIL_Box map, one run of the robot's own recording asked for a manual pose after six queries whose top candidates were 1.5 to 9.3 m off, and four wrong first poses (1.05 to 5.50 m off) were published after their two queries had agreed. The document does not spell out why; my reading is that on a self-similar map the second query fits the same wrong hypothesis as the first. The widget's identical rooms do that when the robot moves within the room.

It also explains a failure I would have hit on a quadruped. An upside-down MID-360 0.45 m above the ground leaves about 100 points in the default height band. On a grid inflated by 0.6 m, 100 points fit perfectly at many places, and the search returns eight of them in search order. Quickstart now widens the band when it holds too few points.

On scale, the author replied that a 1 km map of the Boreas driving dataset loads, and that with the start offset by 10 m and a local search, 6 of 7 spots ranked a candidate near the truth first from all four headings. The seventh was at the edge of the map, with the LiDAR seeing beyond it. The guide's own Boreas section is blunter about a map-wide search on that route: tree-lined stretches scored 0.86 to 0.93 at wrong places.

For scan-to-BIM this is directly useful. Re-scanning a building on a later visit with a handheld LiDAR means putting the new walk into the frame of the old map, and this is that problem with the robot replaced by a person. Indoors, a floor of near-identical rooms or apartments is the worst case for a 2D search, and the consensus check will not save you there. I would give it the map, start the walk somewhere asymmetric (a stair core, a lobby), and check the first pose against a known point.

## Multi-session SLAM, built to learn

[Naoki Akai](https://x.com/naokiakai/status/2122535328221384901), a SLAM and localisation researcher at Nagoya University, posted a 15-second video of a multi-session SLAM he built himself "as study", after seeing several on X. He let Claude edit the video. In a reply he described the problem: building one map from a dataset of several sessions.

<Video
  src="/articles/reality-capture-roundup-nov-20/clip7"
  poster="/articles/reality-capture-roundup-nov-20/clip7-poster.jpg"
  alt="Two point-cloud maps of the same campus area, one blue and one orange, grow side by side as each session's trajectory is drawn, then rotate into each other and merge into one map."
  caption="Two sessions mapped separately (blue and orange), then aligned and merged into one map (video from @naokiakai's post on X)."

There is no code, so the mechanism is the general one. Each session's SLAM leaves a pose graph and a map in its own frame. To merge them you need constraints between sessions: place recognition finds scans in session B that look like places in session A, a registration turns each match into a relative pose, and a robust pose-graph optimisation solves all sessions together, with one session's frame anchored. The video shows the result as the two clouds rotating into one. The hard part is the false match between two similar places, the same aliasing as the localizer above, and the reason multi-session systems use robust kernels or a consistency check over several matches. His public repositories ([als_ros](https://github.com/NaokiAkai/als_ros), mcl3d_ros, plain_slam_ros2) are localisation and SLAM code, and none of their names or descriptions points at this one.

## MultiSet: locating a 360 camera from its frames

[Nikhil Sawlani](https://x.com/sawlaninik/status/2122643106155917402) of MultiSet posted "Real-time location tracking (RTLS) using only a 360 camera frames as input": a camera on a pole walked through a conference hall, its path drawn on a plan.

The overlay in the video is precise, and it reads differently from "real time". The query is a 360 equirectangular frame from an Insta360 X6. The counter reads fix 058 of 151 at 4:49 of a 12:29 walk, "one every 5 s · vision only", and the video is played at 5x. The rays drawn from the camera to surfaces are labelled "feature rays illustrative · end points on real surfaces". The plan inset shows a 205 m track.

MultiSet's own [360-to-VPS page](https://www.multiset.ai/360-to-vps) matches that: sub-5 cm median accuracy, a cloud fix in 1.5 to 2.5 s for one frame or 2.7 to 3.5 s for a multi-frame query, about 52 ms when localisation runs on the device, Insta360 X4 and X5 supported natively (the video's X6 is not on the list yet). Its RTLS page puts the update rate as "a fix every few seconds", with devices using their own motion tracking between fixes. So this is visual positioning sampled every five seconds, not a tracker. It needs no tags or anchors on site, which is the real selling point against UWB, and it inherits every visual-positioning weakness: a corridor of identical booths, a room rearranged since the map, glass. I went through how a VPS turns one photo into a pose, and what MultiSet's earlier benchmark video did and did not show, in [the RobotCar piece](/articles/multiset-vps-robotcar).

For capture work this is the cheap way to put a later 360 walk in the frame of the first: no LiDAR, a pole, a fix every few seconds, and the walk's own SfM between fixes.

It is closed, so I cannot say how its roofs are made. The standard way to get a roof from a footprint is the straight skeleton: move every edge of the polygon inward at the same speed, and the lines the corners trace are the hips and valleys. Height at any point is the roof slope times the distance its edge has travelled, so a rectangle becomes a hip roof and an L becomes two hips meeting in a valley. Flat roofs with parapets, which most of the video shows, are the easy case.

What it hands you is a massing model sized by hand. That is fine for showing a client where panels go and how shadows fall. The gap to a real site is the input: the footprint and the roof come from a person, not from a capture, so a water tank, a stair head or a parapet height that differs from the default is wrong until someone fixes it. A drone survey or even one 360 walk on the roof would give those heights.

## What each one hands you

Sorted by output, the eight split cleanly:

| post | what comes out | open | checkable |
|---|---|---|---|
| akionionio | a Jw_cad drawing with dimensions and site-check flags | no | the overlays and thread; cloud is synthetic |
| Inform | a GLA report and floor plan; UAD 3.6 data in early access | no | the sample report's arithmetic |
| Gauzilla Pro | splats per date, scrubbable in a browser | a free renderer only | no |
| kotohibi | a procedural mesh scene in three.js | the skill (MIT) | the method, not the 30 MB page |
| lidar_localization_ros2 | a pose on an existing map | yes (BSD-2-Clause) | code, benchmark, failures listed |
| Naoki Akai | one merged map from several sessions | no | the video |
| MultiSet | a pose every few seconds from 360 frames | no | the overlay and the product page |
| the solar editor | a massing model with panels | no | the video |

Only one of these gives a BIM element, and it is not a product yet (Inform's Video to BIM). When one arrives it should land in an element model like [Mutiny BIM](/articles/mutiny-bim), where a wall is a record with a type and a host, before anyone calls it IFC. The drawing is the closest thing to a deliverable, and it came from a synthetic cloud. The localizer is the one I would install today, because it publishes its benchmark with the failures in it and the README changed the same day a reply pointed out the weak demo. akionionio's habit of leaving unreadable items on the sheet as site checks is the one idea from this week I want in my own pipeline.
