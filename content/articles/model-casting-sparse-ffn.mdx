The feed-forward network is the part of a transformer that nobody talks about and
everybody pays for. Attention gets the papers; the FFN gets the FLOPs. In a modern
SwiGLU block the FFN holds roughly two-thirds of the non-embedding parameters, and
every one of them runs on every token. The uncomfortable part is that most of that
work is already wasted: for any given token, the large majority of the FFN's hidden
units contribute almost nothing. The activations are nearly sparse. We just can't
cash it in, because the activation function the whole industry switched to —
SiLU — produces *small* numbers, not *exact zeros*, and you can't skip a matmul
whose inputs are merely small.

[Model Casting and Low-Parameter Gating](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31975) (Lomeli
et al., Meta FAIR and ENS, September 2026) is about making that sparsity real and
then spending it. It is a mid-training recipe: take a pre-trained model, swap the
FFN activation for one that zeroes the negative half, add a penalty that drives the
sparsity to an explicit target, and keep training. At inference you then compute
the gate first and, because ~90% of its outputs are zero, skip the two other FFN
matrices for the dead units. That is up to a 3x cut in FFN FLOPs. A second idea,
LoPA Gating, makes the gate itself cheap and pushes past the 3x ceiling that every
activation-sparsity method hits. On a 5.8B model with custom kernels the authors
report a 3.31x wall-clock speedup on the FFN at 90% sparsity.

No code or weights have been released, so everything here is a read of the paper
and its figures, not a reproduction. Where I give a number I say whether it is
**reported** (the authors' measurement), **reasoned** (my arithmetic on their
formulas), or **measured** (something I computed from an artifact myself). There
are no measured numbers in this one — there is nothing to run.

## What an FFN actually computes

Strip a transformer block down and the FFN is two or three matrices with a
nonlinearity in the middle. Modern models use the *gated* form (SwiGLU and its
cousins). On a single token's hidden vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^{D}$, a gated FFN of
hidden width $H > D$ computes

$$
y = W_2\,\bigl(A(W_1 x) \odot (W_3 x)\bigr),\quad W_1, W_3 \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times D},\ W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times H}.
$$

Three matrices. $W_1$ is the **gate**: its output goes through the activation $A$.
$W_3$ is the **up** projection. The two are multiplied element-wise ($\odot$),
and $W_2$ projects the result back **down** to width $D$. Each matrix costs $HD$
multiply-adds per token, so a dense FFN costs $3HD$. With the usual ratio
$H = 8D/3$, that is $8D^2$ parameters in the FFN against roughly $4D^2$ in
attention — which is where the "about two-thirds" comes from (reasoned). Grouped-
query attention, which this paper's base architecture uses, makes the FFN's share
larger still.

Here is the lever. The vector $A(W_1 x) \in \mathbb{R}^{H}$ has one entry per
hidden unit. If entry $i$ is **zero**, then hidden unit $i$ is dead for this token:
its contribution to the element-wise product is zero, so you never needed row $i$
of $W_3$, and its zero contribution to the sum means you never needed column $i$ of
$W_2$ either. Skip both. The only catch is timing, and it is the whole story of
this paper.

## Sparsity you can't spend

"Activation sparsity" means exactly this: for a given token, most hidden units
output ~0. It is not a design goal anyone added — it falls out of training. When
the activation is ReLU, a trained transformer produces genuinely sparse
intermediate activations on its own, and the effect grows with training data
(Li et al., 2023; Luo et al., 2025, as cited). ReLU hands you exact zeros for
free: everything below the threshold is *identically* zero, and a zero is a thing
you can test for and skip.

The problem is that nobody trains with ReLU anymore. The field moved to gated
SiLU and GELU FFNs because they are better, and they are — but $\text{SiLU}(x) =
x\,\sigma(x)$ is smooth and never exactly zero for finite $x$. A SiLU gate
produces *small* numbers, not zeros. You cannot skip a matmul because its input is
0.001. So the sparsity is still there in spirit — most units are near-dead — but
there is no clean boundary to exploit, and pre-training from scratch with ReLU
instead risks neurons that die permanently and never come back.

This is why the interesting work happens at *mid-training*: start from a good
SiLU checkpoint and recover the exploitable sparsity without paying to pretrain
from scratch. Prior mid-training approaches swap the activation to ReLU when
fine-tuning (RELU-fication, Mirzadeh et al., 2024). The trouble is that the
attainable sparsity is then whatever the swap happens to give you — it is a side
effect, not a target you set. Training-free methods that threshold the SiLU gate
at inference (TEAL, top-$p$) are bounded by the sparsity the frozen model already
admits; one such method reports a *critical sparsity* — the level reachable at a
1% quality drop — of at most 40% for an 8B model (reported). Model casting's pitch
is that you should be able to *ask for* 90% and get it.

## The 3x ceiling, and why W1 is always on

Before the recipe, the arithmetic that bounds it. A zero is exploitable only if
you know it is zero **before** the matmul it feeds. The gate's zeros live in
$A(W_1 x)$, which you only have *after* computing $W_1 x$ in full. So $W_1$ always
runs. What the zeros buy you is $W_3$ (you skip the dead rows) and $W_2$ (you skip
the dead columns). Writing $s \in [0,1]$ for the sparsity fraction and
$\text{cost}(W)$ for the multiply-adds actually executed:

$$
\text{FLOP-ratio} = \frac{3HD}{\text{cost}(W_1) + \text{cost}(W_2) + \text{cost}(W_3)} = \frac{3HD}{HD + 2(1-s)HD} = \frac{3}{3 - 2s}.
$$

At $s = 0.9$ that is $3/1.2 = 2.5\times$; at $s = 0.95$ it is $2.73\times$; and
as $s \to 1$ it approaches $3\times$ and no further (reasoned, from the paper's
Eq. 2). That $3\times$ is a hard ceiling for any method that keeps a full gate
matrix, because one of the three matrices can never be skipped. The paper's name
for this is the always-on cost of $W_1$.

The widget below is that formula, made adjustable. Drag the sparsity up and watch
the two skippable matrices shrink while the gate holds firm; the speedup climbs
toward 3x and stalls. Then flip to the LoPA gate (the next section) and watch the
ceiling move.

## Model casting: make sparsity a target

The recipe has two independent ingredients, and you can use either alone.

**First, the activation.** Replace SiLU with **R-S+**, a hybrid that equals SiLU
on the positive side and zeroes the negative side:

$$
\text{R-S+}(x) = \begin{cases} x\,\sigma(x) & x \ge 0 \\ 0 & x < 0 \end{cases}
$$

The design is deliberately conservative. Because R-S+ coincides with SiLU for
$x \ge 0$, casting only perturbs the *negative half* of the activation — the part
that was already contributing least. RELU-fication, by contrast, changes the
function across the whole input range and forces a larger adaptation, which is why
it costs more quality for less sparsity. You start from a checkpoint while the
learning rate is still stable, swap every FFN's activation to R-S+, reset the
optimizer state, and resume with a short warm-up before decaying.

**Second, the penalty.** The activation swap alone does not get you to 90% —
applied mid-training it lands well short. So model casting adds an $\ell_1$ penalty
on the hidden activations and makes it *adaptive*. The loss is

$$
\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{CE}} + \frac{\lambda}{H}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L} \bigl|\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)}\bigr|_1,
$$

where $\mathbf{h}^{(\ell)} = A(W_1 x)$ is the FFN activation at layer $\ell$.
Bigger $\lambda$ means sparser activations but higher cross-entropy — a direct
quality-for-sparsity dial. Rather than guess $\lambda$, they target a sparsity
$s^{\star}$ and let $\lambda$ chase it: $\lambda_{i+1} = \lambda_i \cdot
\alpha^{\,\text{sign}(s^{\star} - s_i)}$, nudging $\lambda$ up when the measured
sparsity is below target and down when it overshoots ($\lambda_0 = 10^{-3}$,
$\alpha = 1.03$). That is the sense in which sparsity becomes an explicit training
target instead of an emergent accident. Set $s^{\star} = 90\%$ and the model
arrives at about 89% measured.

## LoPA Gating: moving the ceiling

The $3\times$ ceiling exists only because $W_1$ is a full $H \times D$ matrix that
always runs. So make it smaller. **LoPA Gating** (Low-Parameter Gating)
reparameterizes the gate as a low-rank term plus a fixed sparse one:

$$
W_1 = G + UV,\quad U \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times r},\ V \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times D},
$$

with rank $r = \alpha D$ (default $\alpha = 1/8$), and $G$ a non-trainable matrix
with a single 1 in each row. The low-rank product $UV$ is what makes the gate
cheap: it costs $r(D + H')$ instead of $HD$. The fixed $G$ is the clever bit —
without it, a pure low-rank $W_1 = UV$ is rank-deficient and the FFN loses
expressivity; adding $G$ makes $W_1$ full rank again (it generalizes the
diagonal-plus-low-rank trick of Chen et al., 2026 that restores the universal
approximation property) at no extra FLOPs or parameters.

Two practical fixes make it trainable. Factoring $W_1$ into two matrices deepens
the network and destabilizes large-scale training, so they insert an RMSNorm
between the low-rank terms: $W_1(x) = Gx + U\cdot\text{RMSNorm}(Vx)$. And because
shrinking the gate before the activation weakens the FFN's only nonlinearity, they
apply the activation to the outputs of *both* $W_1$ and $W_3$ (double activation),
which recovers — slightly exceeds — standard gating.

The payoff is that the parameters you save on the gate get redistributed: the
hidden width grows from $H$ to $H' = D(3H - r)/(r + 2D)$ to hold the total
parameter count fixed. Now the two *skippable* matrices carry a larger share of
the budget, and the always-on gate carries a tiny one. Plugging the LoPA costs
into the FLOP-ratio (the paper's Eq. 4) and using $H = 8D/3$, $\alpha = 1/8$:

- at $s = 0.9$, the FFN FLOPs drop by **6.02x** (reasoned; matches the paper's
  Figure 4);
- as $s \to 1$, the ceiling is no longer 3 but about **13.5x** (reasoned),
  because the gate is now ~7% of a dense FFN's cost instead of 33%.

That is the one-line reason LoPA matters: the cap on activation-sparsity speedups
was the gate, and LoPA makes the gate almost free.

The paper combines these into three recipes. **Activation Casting** keeps a full
gate and only swaps the activation (ceiling 3x). **LoPA Casting** pretrains with
the LoPA gate from scratch, then casts the activation — same parameter count,
faster inference, but you have to own the pretraining. **Architecture Casting**
converts an off-the-shelf model's full gate *into* a LoPA gate at casting time by
fitting $G + UV$ to the existing $W_1$ with alternating minimization, so you can
LoPA-ify a checkpoint you did not train (they do this to Qwen3-1.7B).

The headline, on a 0.81B model (Table 1, all reported): the dense SiLU baseline
scores NLL 2.145. LoPA Casting at an 89% measured sparsity scores 2.165 — a
**+0.93% NLL** increase — for a **5.83x** theoretical FFN FLOP speedup. Standard
Activation Casting at the same 89% gives +0.68% NLL but only 2.48x, because of the
3x wall. For comparison, at matched quality the best training-free methods (top-$p$
and TEAL) reach only 1.6x, and RELU-fication plateaus below 80% sparsity. Pushing
the critical-sparsity frontier from ~40% to ~90% is the real result; the speedup
is the consequence.

Theoretical FLOP ratios are one thing; wall-clock is another, and this is where
most sparsity papers quietly disappoint. This one does not, with custom kernels: a
sparsity-aware AVX-512 kernel on CPU, and Triton kernels on GPU that fuse the $W_2$
and $W_3$ projections and skip the inactive neurons.

On the 5.79B model, at 90% sparsity (all reported): CPU end-to-end goes 1.53x
(standard) to 1.88x (LoPA); the GPU FFN goes 2.06x (standard) to 3.31x (LoPA),
past the 3x ceiling that standard gating physically cannot cross. At 95% sparsity
LoPA reaches 3.90x on the GPU while standard Activation Casting is stuck at 3x.
The sparsity even stacks with quantization: int8 quantization-aware training on top
of a cast model costs 1.57% NLL, and the low-precision sparse kernels reach 8.13x
at 90% sparsity against the full-precision dense baseline (reported).

<Callout type="warning">
Read the GPU numbers carefully. They are **FFN-only**, not end-to-end — attention,
the KV cache, RoPE and Python overhead are excluded, and the authors say so. And
the whole thing works at *decode* time, one token at a time. Batch the requests and
the sparsity patterns stop lining up between tokens: you have to load the union of
every row and column some token in the batch needs, and the effective sparsity
collapses. This is a single-stream, latency-oriented win, not a throughput-at-scale
one.
</Callout>

## Checking the three claims

**The 3x ceiling (reasoned, holds).** It falls straight out of the FLOP-ratio
$3/(3-2s)$, which is monotone in $s$ and tends to 3. The only assumption is that
$W_1$ cannot be skipped, which is true by construction — you need its output to
know where the zeros are. LoPA's escape is equally mechanical: shrink
$\text{cost}(W_1)$ and the limit moves. I reproduced the 6.02x at 90% and the
~13.5x limit from the paper's Eq. 4 with $H = 8D/3$ and $\alpha = 1/8$, and both
match the paper's figures.

**The 3.31x / 3.90x GPU speedups (reported, plausible).** These are the paper's
kernel measurements on an H100, FFN-only, and I can't independently time them —
no kernels were released. What makes them credible rather than aspirational is
that they sit *below* the memory-bandwidth roofline the authors plot, and that the
standard-gating curve lands at 2.06x — just under the 2.5x its FLOP ratio allows at
90% sparsity, where kernel-launch and memory overhead should drag it. The method's
internal arithmetic is consistent with its measurements.

**The +0.93% NLL at 90% (reported).** This is a validation-set number on a 0.81B
model, and it is the LoPA Casting operating point. It is worth keeping the asterisk
the paper keeps: Architecture Casting — the recipe you would actually apply to a
model you did not pretrain — costs more, +2.20% NLL on the same 0.81B model,
because converting a full gate to low-rank sheds parameters it cannot grow back.
LoPA's clean 0.93% assumes you pretrained with LoPA in the first place. On
downstream tasks, Activation Casting costs 0.4 points of a 12-task average at the
90% target, and 1.6 points on Qwen3-1.7B (reported).

## Where it generalizes

The gate-is-a-router observation is the bridge to Mixture of Experts. A SiLU gate
over hidden units is, structurally, a soft router that decides which units fire —
so the same casting mechanism applies to an MoE's expert FFNs *and* to its router.
If you've built an MoE from scratch, the parallel is immediate; see
[Mixture of Experts, from scratch](/articles/mixture-of-experts-from-scratch) for
the router-and-dispatch machinery this reuses. On an 8-expert top-2 model, casting
the expert FFNs reaches 88.6% activation sparsity for +0.083 NLL, and — tellingly
— switching to top-1 routing, the usual architectural route to sparsity, lands at a
*worse* NLL with no activation sparsity at all (reported). Casting the FFN and
routing the experts are complementary, not competing.

It also composes with the other levers people pull on inference cost. The int8/int4
results put it alongside post-training quantization — the same territory as
[NVIDIA Model Optimizer](/articles/nvidia-model-optimizer), but attacking FLOPs
rather than bytes. And where
[runtime dynamic compression](/articles/runtime-dynamic-compression) exploits that
only a few *experts* are needed per token to keep memory down, model casting
exploits that only a few *hidden units* are needed per token to keep FLOPs down —
two readings of the same conditional-computation idea at different granularities. If
you want the ground floor on why the FFN and attention split the work the way they
do, [how self-attention works](/articles/how-transformers-attention-works) covers
the other half of the block this one leaves alone.

## The takeaway

Activation sparsity was always there; the gated-FFN era spent it without meaning
to and then couldn't get it back. Model casting's contribution is to make the
sparsity a number you set — ~90%, during a cheap mid-training stage — instead of a
number you measure and hope for. The inference trick that cashes it in is old and
simple: compute the gate, skip the dead units. The genuinely new piece is LoPA
Gating, which notices that the always-on gate was the whole ceiling and makes it
almost free, turning a 3x cap into a measured 3.31x and a theoretical 13.5x.

The honest limits are the ones to carry away. The big wall-clock numbers are
FFN-only and decode-only; batch the traffic and the win erodes. LoPA's best quality
assumes you pretrained with it, and the drop-in Architecture Casting path costs
more. And with no code or weights released yet, the 3.31x lives in the paper's
plots, not on anyone else's H100. But the arithmetic is clean, the measurements are
internally consistent, and the central claim — that you can *target* 90% activation
sparsity and keep the quality — is the kind of result that moves a frontier rather
than nudging a benchmark.
