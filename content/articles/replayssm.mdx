ReplaySSM has turned up on this site three times already, each time as a line item. It is in the
optimisation list of [GLM-5.3-Flash](/articles/glm-5-3-flash), between tensor parallelism and W8A8. It
is the change that made Z.ai's KDA decode kernel *slower*, 0.90x, in the version history of
[how GLM built its own inference stack](/articles/glm-built-its-own-inference-infra). And it is the
name of a maintainer note in the [Cinference](/articles/cinference) engine. Nobody, including me,
had explained what it does.

Yesterday Ze-Wei Liou [posted](https://x.com/zwljohnny/status/2109679465609470216) the paper, a joint
NVIDIA blog post and the code, with Tri Dao and five NVIDIA engineers. The post makes four claims: it runs
in production in GLM-5.3-Flash and Kimi-K3; it ships in TensorRT-LLM, vLLM, FlashInfer and SGLang; it
speeds up standard decoding by up to 1.47x and speculative decoding by up to 2.1x; and it makes a
low-precision SSM state possible "whose rounding error never propagates through the recurrence."

I expected a kernel paper. What I found is a two-line algebraic identity and a careful account of
what falls out of it. The identity is the kind of thing you can check on a napkin. The consequences
are where the interesting engineering is, and also where the post's wording runs ahead of the
evidence.

## Why a fixed-size state is not cheap to decode

A Mamba-2 layer keeps, per head, a state matrix $S_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_v \times d_k}$ and updates it
once per token:

$$
S_t = \alpha_t S_{t-1} + v_t k_t^{\top}, \qquad o_t = S_t q_t
$$

$\alpha_t \in (0,1)$ is a data-dependent decay, $v_t$, $k_t$, $q_t$ are this token's projections, and
$o_t$ is the output. (Mamba-2's own names are $x$, $B$, $C$ for $v$, $k$, $q$; the paper and the kernels
mix both.) Gated DeltaNet and Kimi Delta Attention add a delta-rule correction, which I come back to.

The selling point of this layer is that $S_t$ does not grow with context. The catch is in how a decoder
runs it. Every step loads $S_{t-1}$ from HBM, scales it, adds a rank-1 outer product, reads the output,
and stores $S_t$ back. For one head the step does about $5 d_v d_k$ FLOPs and moves $2 \cdot 4 d_v d_k$
bytes with an FP32 state, so the arithmetic intensity is about 0.63 FLOP per byte. An H100 wants around
20 FLOP per byte to keep its FP32 cores busy. Batching does not help, because every sequence brings its
own state and no element is reused. The step is a memory copy with a little arithmetic attached.

That copy is not small. I pulled the released `config.json` of
[Nemotron-3-Super-120B-A12B-NVFP4](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3-Super-120B-A12B-NVFP4)
and counted its `hybrid_override_pattern`: 40 Mamba-2 layers, 8 attention layers and 40 MoE layers,
with 128 Mamba heads of $d_v = 64$, $d_k = 128$, and `mamba_ssm_cache_dtype` set to `float32`. One
layer's state for one sequence is $128 \times 64 \times 128 \times 4$ bytes, exactly 4 MiB; across 40 layers
that is 160 MiB per sequence, and at batch 256 it is 40 GiB of state that has to be read and written on
every decode step.

Attention's KV cache in the same model is FP8 with 2 KV heads of dimension 128, so one token's entry
is 0.5 KiB per layer. One Mamba state is the KV cache of 8,192 tokens. Attention only reads its cache
while the SSM reads and writes, so the SSM layers move more bytes per step until $40 \times 8$ MiB equals
$8 \times 0.5 N$ KiB, which is $N = 81{,}920$ tokens. The paper does this same arithmetic and then
measures the crossover later, near 100K, which it puts down to attention kernels having had more
optimisation. Their profile, the left panel below, shows the SSM layers as the largest slice of a
decode step all the way out to 100K tokens of context. A hybrid model that was meant to be "mostly
cheap layers" is mostly paying for state traffic.

## The identity

Unroll the recurrence over the last $h$ tokens, starting from a checkpoint $S_{\text{ckpt}}$ taken
before them. Write $\gamma_{i:j} = \prod_{\ell=i}^{j} \alpha_\ell$ for the decay accumulated between two
positions, and $\gamma_h = \gamma_{1:h}$:

$$
S_t = \alpha_t S_{t-1} + v_t k_t^{\top} = \gamma_h S_{\text{ckpt}} + \sum_{j=1}^{h} \gamma_{j+1:h}\, v_j k_j^{\top}
$$

Both sides are the same matrix. The left side is how every SSM decoder runs: keep the summary, update
it, throw the inputs away. The right side says you could instead keep an old summary and the last $h$
inputs, $(\alpha_j, v_j, k_j)$, and rebuild the current state whenever you need it. Nothing about the
model changes; outputs agree up to floating-point order of operations.

ReplaySSM picks the right side. On a normal step (a *non-flush* step) it appends this token's inputs to
a small buffer, computes the output from the checkpoint plus the buffer, and stores nothing but the new
record. When the buffer holds $L$ records, a *flush* step folds them into the checkpoint, writes the
checkpoint once, and empties the buffer. The paper uses $L = 16$.

The saving falls straight out. Over a window of $L$ steps both schemes read the state $L$ times, because
every output needs it. Conventional decoding also writes it $L$ times; ReplaySSM writes it once. So the
state term goes from $8 L\, d_v d_k$ bytes to $4(L+1)\, d_v d_k$, a ratio of $(L+1)/2L$, just over a
half. The buffer adds a little back: step $i$ reads the $i-1$ records before it, so a window reads
$L(L-1)/2$ records and writes $L - 1$. The paper's Appendix C.3 prices a record per head at
$2 d_v + 2 d_k + 4$ bytes and gets 0.58 of conventional traffic for Nemotron-3-Super. I reran it and
get the same 0.581.

That count is deliberately conservative, and the released kernel shows by how much. In
`selective_state_update_replayssm_output_only.py` the buffer is three tensors: `x_cache` and `dt_cache`
per value head, and `B_cache` per *group*, so the 128-wide key is stored once for the 16 heads that
share it. For one Nemotron-3-Super layer a record is $128 \times (128 + 4) + 8 \times 256 = 18{,}944$
bytes. Stepping that layout through a window in the widget further down, with one token verified per
step, gives 4.40 MiB per token against 8 MiB for conventional decoding, about 0.55.

### Output without a state

The second trick is smaller and, I think, more consequential. On a non-flush step nobody will ever
read the state of that step: the next step rebuilds from the checkpoint again. So the step only needs
$o_t$, and a product of three things can be bracketed either way:

$$
o = (v k^{\top})\, q = v\, (k^{\top} q)
$$

The left side builds a $d_v \times d_k$ matrix and multiplies it by $q$. The right side computes one dot
product and scales a vector. Applied to the whole window:

$$
o_t = \gamma_{h}\, S_{\text{ckpt}}\, q_t + \sum_{j=1}^{h} \gamma_{j+1:h}\, v_j\, (k_j^{\top} q_t)
$$

The checkpoint is read once, as a matrix-vector product. Everything else is dot products against
cached keys, and because $k$ and $q$ are shared by every head in a group, the dot products are computed
once per group. The paper counts the state-building term shrinking by $d_v d_k / (d_v + d_k)$, about 43
for this model. That does not matter much for standard decoding, which is memory-bound either way. It
matters a great deal for speculative decoding, below.

Here is the non-flush branch of the released Mamba-2 kernel. It is the equation above, line for line:
`ck_acc` is $S_{\text{ckpt}} q$ streamed over tiles of the state, `bc_cache` holds the precomputed
$k_j^{\top} q$ (a small kernel computes them once per group), and the append that follows writes only
`x`, `dt` and `B`:

```python
# vllm/model_executor/layers/mamba/ops/selective_state_update_replayssm_output_only.py, lines 309-357 (trimmed)
ck_acc = tl.zeros([BLOCK_SIZE_M], dtype=tl.float32)
for i in tl.static_range(NF_NDS):
    ...
    ck_acc += tl.sum(st.to(tl.float32) * c_chunk[None, :], axis=1)
checkpoint_out = ck_acc * total_decay_cache
bc_cache = tl.load(bc_pre_ptr + offs_k_cache * stride_bc_pre_pos, ...)
cache_out = tl.sum(
    x_all_cache.to(tl.float32) * (scale_cache * bc_cache)[None, :], axis=1
)
out = checkpoint_out + cache_out

# Append the current token (x, dt, B) into the buffer at write_pos.
tl.store(x_cache_ptr + offs_m * stride_x_cache_dim + write_pos * stride_x_cache_pos, x_cur, ...)
```

`scale_cache` is $\Delta_j\, e^{A(\text{total} - \text{cumsum}_j)}$, the per-record decay rebuilt from the cached
`dt` values, so the kernel caches $\Delta_j$ rather than $\alpha_j$. The only full-state store in the
file is inside the `else` branch that starts at line 358. One difference from the paper's main text:
its Algorithm 1 materialises the state on every step, while the shipped kernel takes the output-only
route on every non-flush step (the appendix counts both routes).

### Why 16

A larger window amortises the one write over more tokens, but every step reads more records. The paper
writes traffic per committed token as a function of $L$ and minimises it:
$L^{*} = \sqrt{2\bar{a}\,(M_S/r - T)}$, where $M_S/r$ is the state-to-record size ratio (84 for this
model), $T$ the tokens verified per step and $\bar{a}$ the mean accepted. For standard decoding that
is about 13; the sweep over 8, 16 and 32 lands on 16 for all four models. The June blog post had picked
8 for Nemotron and 16 for Qwen; the October paper tunes vLLM's own launch configuration first and settles
on 16 everywhere.

### The delta-rule wrinkle

Gated DeltaNet does not fit the template at first glance:

$$
S_t = \alpha_t S_{t-1}(I - \beta_t k_t k_t^{\top}) + \beta_t v_t k_t^{\top}
$$

Expanding the product gives $S_t = \alpha_t S_{t-1} + u_t k_t^{\top}$ with
$u_t = \beta_t (v_t - \alpha_t S_{t-1} k_t)$, the same outer-product form with a corrected value. The
correction depends on the previous state, so you cannot cache $v$ and replay; you cache $u$, which is
computed once, on the step it belongs to, from the rebuilt state. That is why GDN takes the
state-and-output route even on non-flush steps (it needs the state read at both $k$ and $q$), and why
its speculative path needs a $T \times T$ triangular solve to get all draft corrections at once, the UT
transform that delta-rule training kernels already use. KDA, with a per-channel decay, unrolls the
same way; [the K3 architecture page](/architectures/kimi-k3) has its state at 414 MiB per sequence,
which is the number ReplaySSM is trying not to copy.

## Speculative decoding: rollback becomes a pointer

Speculative decoding is where an SSM's summary-and-discard habit really hurts. A drafter proposes a
few tokens, the target model verifies $T$ of them in one pass, and only afterwards do you learn how many
were accepted. Attention handles the rejects by moving the end-of-sequence pointer in its KV cache back;
the rejected keys are simply never read again. An SSM has already folded the drafts into its state, and
the update has no cheap inverse.

vLLM's answer is to write a snapshot of the state after each of the $T$ tokens and keep the one that
matches the accepted prefix. That is one state read and $T$ state writes per step, on a path that was
already memory-bound, plus $T$ state slots per request that cap how many requests fit. The paper's
kernel measurement on Nemotron-3-Super at batch 256 has vLLM's verification costing 4.66 times a plain
decoding step at $T = 8$.

ReplaySSM gets rollback almost for free because the drafts were never summarised. It appends all $T$
draft records behind the $h$ committed ones, computes all $T$ outputs with the output-only form (one
read of the checkpoint against $T$ queries, $K^{\top} Q$ as one small matrix product, a causal mask over
decays), and after sampling advances the committed pointer by $a$. Rejected records sit past the
pointer and get overwritten. A flush, when it happens, folds only the *committed* records, so it can run
before acceptance is known.

The one subtle part is when to flush. The buffer is a ring of capacity $L + T$, and the rule is to flush
when $h + T > L$: if this step did not flush and all $T$ were accepted, the next step's $T$ records
would overrun committed ones. The rule depends only on $h$ and $T$, so it is known before the step
runs and the decision can live inside a captured CUDA graph. In the vLLM fork the threshold is
`spec_flush_threshold = replayssm_buffer_len + max_spec_len` (`mamba_attn.py` line 123), and the
on-device commit kernel sets the next step's flag with
`next_is_flush = ((new_wp + 2 * MAX_SPEC_LEN) > MAX_CACHE_LEN)` (`selective_state_update_replayssm_spec.py`
line 810). Substitute the threshold and that is $h + T > L$. The June blog post states the rule as
$h + 2T > L$ for a buffer of capacity $L$; it is the same rule with $L$ counting the whole ring.

ReplaySSM is not the first scheme to cache inputs for SSM speculation, and the paper spends an appendix
being fair about it. The Mamba in the Llama, Snakes and Ladders and STree all avoid per-token snapshots
by replaying the accepted tokens onto a checkpoint. They still write at least one state per step, and
their replay is a serial recurrence that holds the state tile in registers for $T$ or $2T - 1$ steps.
The authors re-tuned each one's launch configuration (which made them 1.4 to 3.0x faster than as
released) and then measured all of them at batch 256. The result I did not expect: at $T \geq 4$
STree and The Mamba in the Llama move 1.6 to 2.9x *fewer* bytes than vLLM's snapshots and are still not
faster. Fewer bytes only help if the kernel can keep enough memory requests in flight, and a serial
recurrence holding a state tile in registers cannot.

## Checking the speed numbers

The post says "up to 1.47x" for standard decoding and "up to 2.1x" for speculative decoding. Both are
in the paper. Neither is the whole story, so here is where each one lives.

Standard decoding first. The paper's Table 6 gives end-to-end decode-step speedup over vLLM with its
best swept launch configuration. At batch 256 it is 1.47x for Nemotron-3-Nano-4B, 1.42x for
Nemotron-3-Super-120B, 1.27x for Qwen3.5-4B and 1.20x for Qwen3.5-122B, which is the 1.20-1.47x in the
abstract. The isolated SSM kernel is 1.46 to 1.80x faster at the same batch (Table 7). The gap between
the two is just Amdahl: attention, the MoE GEMMs and everything else are untouched.

The same tables show where it does not help. At batch 1 the end-to-end speedup is 0.96 to 0.99x, a
slight slowdown, and the kernel alone is 0.41 to 0.88x. At that size the conventional kernel reaches
only 21.6% of peak DRAM bandwidth on an H100 and 9.3% on a B300, so cutting its traffic saves nothing,

GPU, this is not for you. Two other footnotes are worth knowing. The batch-512 cells of 1.72x and

## The precision argument, derived

The third claim is the one I wanted to check by hand, because "rounding error never propagates" is a
strong sentence.

Start with a single low-precision state used for everything, the way you would naturally quantise an
SSM cache. Let $S_t$ be the exact state and $\hat S_t$ the stored one, rounded after every update:
$\hat S_t = \mathcal{Q}(\alpha_t \hat S_{t-1} + v_t k_t^{\top})$. Call the rounding error of step $t$
$e_t$ and the accumulated deviation $D_t = \hat S_t - S_t$. Subtract the two recurrences:

$$
D_t = \alpha_t D_{t-1} + e_t = \sum_{j \leq t} \gamma_{j+1:t}\, e_j
$$

Every rounding error ever made is still in the state, decayed but never removed, and the output error
is $D_t q_t$. With a constant decay $\alpha$ and independent per-step errors of bias $b$ and variance
$\sigma^2$, the mean deviation settles at $b/(1-\alpha)$ and the variance at $\sigma^2/(1-\alpha^2)$. A
head with long memory, $\alpha$ close to 1, amplifies its rounding error by a large factor, and it takes
on the order of $1/(1-\alpha)$ tokens to get there. Round-to-nearest contributes through the bias term,
which grows linearly; stochastic rounding is unbiased but its variance still accumulates. A KV cache
has no such loop: an FP8 entry is written once and read many times, never fed back into itself. That
is the reason, the post says, KV caches ship in FP8 while SSM states still ship in FP32.

ReplaySSM changes this in two steps. First, with one low-precision checkpoint, rounding now happens once
per window instead of once per step, so the same sum runs over flushes and the limits become
$b/(1-\alpha^L)$ and $\sigma^2/(1-\alpha^{2L})$. Smaller, but the loop is still there.

The second step is the real idea. Non-flush steps only *read* the checkpoint to make outputs; only
flushes *update* it. So keep two copies. The flush starts from a high-precision checkpoint
$S^{\text{HP}}$, folds in the buffer in FP32, and casts the result independently into both formats:
$S^{\text{HP}}_m = \mathcal{Q}_{\text{HP}}(\cdot)$, $S^{\text{LP}}_m = \mathcal{Q}_{\text{LP}}(\cdot)$ from the same
unrounded matrix. Non-flush steps read $S^{\text{LP}}$. The next flush reads $S^{\text{HP}}$, never
$S^{\text{LP}}$.

Take the recurrence copy as exact for a moment, so $S^{\text{HP}}_m = S_m$, and write
$S^{\text{LP}}_m = S_m + \varepsilon_m$, where $\varepsilon_m$ is a single rounding of a clean state. A
non-flush step $h$ positions into the next window outputs

$$
\hat o_t = \gamma_h (S_m + \varepsilon_m)\, q_t + \sum_{j=1}^{h} \gamma_{j+1:h}\, v_j (k_j^{\top} q_t) = o_t + \gamma_h\, \varepsilon_m\, q_t
$$

The error is $\gamma_h \varepsilon_m q_t$. It contains $\varepsilon_m$ and nothing else: no
$\varepsilon_{m-1}$, no sum over history, because the previous low-precision copy was thrown away and
never entered anything that survives. Since $\gamma_h \leq 1$, the error is bounded by one rounding of
the last checkpoint, whatever the sequence length. That is the sense in which the read error does not
propagate, and the derivation holds.

What it does not say is that a low-precision state has no accumulating error at all. Two terms remain,
and the paper names both. The recurrence copy is itself rounded at every flush unless you keep it in
FP32; the paper's preferred configuration keeps it in FP16 with stochastic rounding, which re-enters the
loop at $1/L$ of the per-step rate and at a precision where the loop is mild. And on GDN, the cached
corrections $u$ on non-flush steps are computed from the low-precision read copy, so they carry its
error into the rest of that window's outputs; the flush recomputes them from cached raw $v$ and the
high-precision state, so the error stops there. The emulation kernel shows the separation plainly: on a
flush it quantises `S_new` into `state_hi_ptr` with stochastic rounding and then, under the comment
"Quantize the unrounded solution independently for the read copy", into `state_lo_ptr` with
round-to-nearest (`emulation/mamba2_replay.py`, lines 150-174); a non-flush step loads `state_lo_ptr`
and nothing else (line 176).

I wanted to see the shape of this rather than take it from a plot, so I built a toy: one Mamba-2 head
with an 8 by 32 state, Gaussian inputs, a fresh decay every step, run for 4,096 steps four ways (exact,
rounded every step, one ReplaySSM checkpoint, and separate copies with an exact recurrence). The TypeScript
version runs in your browser below, from a fixed seed, so you see the numbers I quote.

With an FP8 state, stochastic rounding, a window of 16 and a long-memory head (decays between 0.9995 and
0.99999), the relative output error after 4,096 steps is 111% when the state is rounded every step,
37% with one checkpoint rounded once per window, and 2.4% with separate copies. The first two are still
climbing; the third went flat within the first few dozen steps and stays at one rounding's worth. Flip
the head to short memory (decays between 0.9 and 0.95) and the picture changes: everything plateaus
early, at 9.9%, 2.1% and 1.4%. For a head that forgets in a few dozen tokens, rounding once per window
already captures most of the benefit, and the second copy buys a factor of about 1.5. The separation
earns its keep on the slow heads, the ones carrying long-range information, which is exactly where the
recurrence amplifies error. Switching to round-to-nearest shows the other thing the paper reports: per
step, rounding to nearest is smaller than stochastic rounding early on (at around step 40 it is 10.7%
against 15.3% for the every-step FP8 state) and much worse later (506% against 111% at step 4,096),
because its bias adds linearly while stochastic rounding's noise adds in quadrature. The paper uses
stochastic rounding for anything that feeds the recurrence and round-to-nearest for the read copy,
which is rounded once and never fed back.

The toy is a toy. Its inputs are random, not a language model's, and its error is not KL divergence.
The paper measured the real thing: KL divergence of next-token distributions from FP32-state decoding,
under teacher forcing on PG-19 books, out to 128K generated tokens.

Then accuracy. Averaged over the four models, a shared NVFP4 checkpoint loses 13.05 points on
GPQA-Diamond, 5.88 on MMLU-Pro and 10.92 on Math-L5. NVFP4 reads with an FP16 recurrence cut those
losses to 2.36, 0.15 and 0.10 points. The average hides one model: Nemotron-3-Nano-4B with separate

state). Shared FP16 and FP8 checkpoints stay within benchmark noise of FP32, which means FP8 state
storage was already close to fine; it is 4-bit where the separation decides whether the model works.

reference answer, the shared NVFP4 checkpoint on Nemotron-3-Super matches FP32 in the two shorter

There is a speed side too. A smaller read copy moves fewer bytes on every non-flush step: NVFP4 reads
make the non-flush Mamba-2 kernel 3.83x faster than an FP32 state at batch 256, and 3.33x over a whole
window at batch 512 once the high-precision flushes are paid for. Those numbers come from separate
CuTe DSL kernels in the repository, not from the serving integration.

## What actually shipped

The post says ReplaySSM "now runs in production in GLM-5.3-Flash and Kimi-K3, and ships in TensorRT-LLM,
vLLM, FlashInfer, and SGLang." I checked each pull request through the GitHub API. The picture is real,
and narrower than that sentence.

| system | what is merged | what is not |
|---|---|---|
| vLLM | Mamba-2 standard decoding, Triton kernel ([#48018](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/48018), July 24); a FlashInfer ReplaySSM backend for Mamba-2 ([#52506](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/52506)) and its MTP path ([#52928](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/52928), September 30) | the authors' Gated DeltaNet standard and speculative PRs and their Triton Mamba-2 speculative PR were closed unmerged |
| FlashInfer | Mamba-2 checkpointing kernels ([#3975](https://github.com/flashinfer-ai/flashinfer/pull/3975)), GDN speculative kernels ([#4081](https://github.com/flashinfer-ai/flashinfer/pull/4081)), prefix-cache materialisation, autotuning, and SM100 CuTe DSL GDN MTP kernels | the GDN "v cache" variant ([#4140](https://github.com/flashinfer-ai/flashinfer/pull/4140)) is open |
| TensorRT-LLM | Mamba-2 MTP checkpointing replay ([#14203](https://github.com/NVIDIA/TensorRT-LLM/pull/14203), June 19) and GDN MTP replay ([#16464](https://github.com/NVIDIA/TensorRT-LLM/pull/16464)) | |
| SGLang | GDN and KDA buffered output-only decode ([#28451](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/pull/28451), June 26) and GDN ring spec-verify ([#28695](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/pull/28695)), both written by an SGLang contributor rather than the authors | the post lists no SGLang PR |

So for vLLM users the accurate statement is: Mamba-2 models such as Nemotron get it, including MTP
through the FlashInfer backend; GDN models such as Qwen3.5 do not, in upstream vLLM, as of today. The
repository's own README is more careful than the post. It says upstream vLLM has merged the Mamba-2
standard decoding kernel and that "the other three decoding paths, the emulated quantization and the
low-precision CuTe kernels are only available here." That last clause is the important one for the
third claim: the low-precision state is a research result with emulated quantisation and standalone
kernels. I found no two-precision ReplaySSM state cache in any of the four serving engines; a "quantized
ReplaySSM state cache" PR exists only on a fork. (A single low-precision state is a different thing and
does exist: vLLM's own `selective_state_update` kernel in `mamba_ssm.py` has an
`enable_stochastic_rounding` option for an FP16 state, and the ReplaySSM kernel keeps it for its one flush
write.)

The production claims need the same care. GLM-5.3-Flash is a straightforward yes: the paper's
appendix and Z.ai's own infrastructure report both say ReplaySSM was added to its KDA decode kernel. Kimi-K3 is
different. Its technical report (Section 5.4.2) describes caching only the projected inputs of draft
tokens, rebuilding the accepted states on-chip, and writing back "the states of the verified and bonus
tokens", "a design independently proposed in the concurrent work ReplaySSM." That is input caching for
rollback, but it writes a state every verification step, which puts it closer to the replay-then-verify
family above than to ReplaySSM's write-once-per-window design. The paper's own appendix describes Kimi-K3
accurately, as citing ReplaySSM as a concurrent approach. "Runs in production in Kimi-K3" compresses
that into something stronger.

And integration has a cost the paper does not dwell on: prefix caching. A cached prefix needs an exact
state at a block boundary, and a ReplaySSM layer only has an exact state at a flush. The vLLM fork
forces a flush on the step that completes a cache block (the `align_mode` branch next to
`is_flush_cpu` in `mamba_attn.py`, lines 673-682), and FlashInfer merged a separate "materialization for
prefix caching" kernel. In SGLang, an open issue from September 3 reports that `--enable-linear-replayssm`
requires the `no_buffer` radix-cache strategy, which on a RAG-style benchmark with shared 2,048-token
prefixes dropped the prefix hit rate from 88.2% to 65.9% and raised mean time-to-first-token from 226 ms
to 1,063 ms. That is one user's measurement on Qwen3.8-27B, which I could not rerun, but the mechanism
is real. One reply under the post says current implementations "seem to break/corrupt in many settings";
the long list of open ReplaySSM bug-fix PRs in SGLang's tracker (ring cursors not reset after HiCache
load-back, unflushed checkpoints, pending updates lost on CPU retraction) suggests the integration is
still settling, though none of them says the method is wrong.

## What I make of it

The clever part is not the caching. Mamba in the Llama and STree already cached inputs. It is the
observation that a decoder never needs the state of a step that nothing will resume from, so most steps
can produce an output and no state at all. Everything else follows: roughly half the state traffic, rollback by
pointer, verification as a small masked matrix product instead of a serial loop, and a read copy that
can be as coarse as you like because no future step depends on it. The post's own framing is accurate: "Caching SSM inputs instead of the state is what ReplaySSM does.
What it changes is that computing the output and updating the state can be separated during decoding."
The derivation above is all it takes to see why.

What I would use today: if you serve a Mamba-2 hybrid like Nemotron-3 at batches above roughly 32 in vLLM
or TensorRT-LLM, turn it on, and turn on MTP speculation with it, because that combination is the one where
the paper's numbers say speculation finally pays at serving batch. For GDN and KDA models the merged paths
are in SGLang, FlashInfer and TensorRT-LLM; upstream vLLM does not have them yet. Either way, check your
prefix-cache hit rate before and after. If you run one request at a time, leave it off; the paper's own
table says it is a few percent slower there.

What I would wait on: the 4-bit state. The derivation is sound and the accuracy table is convincing on
three of four models, but it is measured with emulated quantisation, the 4B Nemotron still loses 6.7
GPQA points, and no engine ships it. When one does, the thing to measure is long-generation accuracy by
length quartile, the way the paper did, because short benchmarks will tell you nothing.

[Mamba and state-space models](/architectures/mamba-ssm) has the layer itself, and
[liquid time constants and gated delta rules](/articles/ltc-gated-delta) the recurrence family ReplaySSM
unrolls. [KDA's half-life](/articles/kda-half-life) is the per-channel version of the "long memory" head

its code. I shallow-cloned [Johnny-Liou/ReplaySSM](https://github.com/Johnny-Liou/ReplaySSM) at commit
`5d3ea59` (September 30), a vLLM fork, and read the Mamba-2 standard and speculative Triton kernels, the
metadata builder that sets flush flags, and the emulation kernels; line numbers above are at that commit.
I did not run any of its code.

Recounted from primary files: Nemotron-3-Super's layer counts and shapes from its `config.json` on
Hugging Face; the 4 MiB state, 8,192-token equivalence and 81,920-token crossover; the 0.581 traffic ratio
and $L^{*} \approx 13$ from the paper's formulas; the 18,944-byte record from the kernel's buffer layout;
the flush rule from the fork's code. Every speedup and accuracy figure above is read from the paper's
Tables 2, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 12 and its text; 36x is my division of 0.136 by 0.0038. Merge status for each
pull request came from the GitHub REST API on October 13; Kimi-K3's design from its report's Section 5.4.2.

The error toy and the widget numbers are mine: a NumPy version first, then the same model in TypeScript
(`components/articles/replayssm/sim.ts`), which is what the widget runs. Its FP8 and FP4 are simplified (a
per-row or per-16 scale kept unrounded), it compares to an exact rather than an FP32 run, and it measures
relative output error rather than KL, so it shows the mechanism, not the paper's magnitudes. I could not
check the production claims beyond what Z.ai's and Moonshot's own documents say, and I could not rerun the
SGLang prefix-cache measurement.
