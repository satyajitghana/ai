Wenbo Hu [posted the thread](https://x.com/gordonhu608/status/2110400049515728911)
on Tev-Gym this week: "1,170 envs with dense progress rewards," a 27B model that
"matches GPT-6 Astra & Claude Opus 5" on a long-horizon exam at Pass@5, and a
320B model "only 0.02 behind GPT 5.6 sol" on Terminal-Bench 4.0. The paper is
[arXiv 2610.18868](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18868), from Adobe Research and
UCLA, with a [project page](https://tev-agent.github.io).

Two ideas carry it: write the correct answer first, as a program, and derive
the messy task from it; then, knowing the right answer at every stage, grade the
middle of a rollout and not only its end. The thread's arithmetic is right
everywhere I checked. What it leaves out is how small the denominators are.

## Why long-horizon RL starves

Group-relative policy optimisation (GRPO) samples $G$ rollouts of one task and
weights each by its group-normalised advantage, the paper's Eq. 2:

$$
\hat{A}_i = \frac{R_i - \operatorname{mean}(R_{1:G})}{\operatorname{std}(R_{1:G}) + \epsilon}
$$

With a terminal reward $R_i = \mathbb{1}[\tau_i \text{ passes}]$, a group where
every rollout fails has all $R_i = 0$. The mean is 0, the numerator is 0 for
every rollout, and the task contributes nothing to the gradient. Those $G$
rollouts were compute spent for nothing.

If the policy solves a task with probability $p$, and rollouts are independent,
the chance that all $G$ fail is $(1-p)^G$. The thread says "66% of groups at a 5%
solve rate, G=8". **Reasoned:** $0.95^8 = 0.663$. The paper's other figures
check too: $0.99^8 = 0.923$ (it says 92%) and $0.95^{16} = 0.440$ (it says 44%).

Two details the thread does not carry. First, the paper trains with $G = 24$,
not 8. At $p = 0.05$ that gives $0.95^{24} = 0.292$, so 29% of groups would be
dead. **Reasoned.** But on Tev-Gym itself the untrained Qwen3.8-27B fully solves
3.2% of environments in single-rollout probes (**reported**), and at $p = 0.03$
the figure is $0.97^{24} = 0.481$. **Reasoned.** About half the batch, at the
start of training, when signal matters most.

Second, a group with exactly one success is barely better. The one pass gets a
positive advantage and the other $G-1$ failures all get the *same* negative
one. A rollout that built nine-tenths of the answer and one that never opened
the input folder are indistinguishable to the gradient.

Mid-horizon pipelines discard all-fail groups and resample, and so does this
paper's training. On long tasks that means many draws per usable batch.

## Build the answer first

The usual way to make an agent task is prompt-first: ask a model for a task, an
input, a checker and a solution, and hope the four describe the same problem.
The paper calls the failure mode *artifact drift*: each piece looks valid alone,
but together they disagree.

Tev-Gym inverts the order. Every environment comes from a seeded generator
program, written once by a coding agent and reviewed by the authors, that runs
these stages with no model in the loop:

1. **World construction.** From a seed and a scale tier, generate the latent
   world $W$: the complete, correct state of the domain. For the paper's running
   example, a retail warehouse with 502 customers, 52 products and 6,210
   transactions over six months. $W$ is checked against invariants (unique ids,
   valid joins, finite measures). The same step draws a *defect plan*: exactly
   which records or fields will be corrupted, and by which declared operator.
2. **Workflow DAG.** Compile the workflow into a directed acyclic graph of
   stages, built by code from the size of $W$. For the warehouse:
   `profile-sources → reconcile-facts / normalize-dims → load-facts → aggregate-validate → package-sidecars`.
   The longest chain of dependent stages is the task's horizon.
3. **Rendering.** Project $W$ onto the files an agent would see: CSV, TSV, JSON,
   SQLite, safetensors shards, binaries, manifests. Rendering is a pure function
   of $W$, and the generator asserts it round-trips: parse the rendered files and
   you get $W$ back exactly.
4. **Controlled corruption.** Apply the defect plan. For the warehouse that is
   three operators: schema drift, duplicates and nulls, and category drift. The
   corrupted tree $s_0$ is what the agent receives. The untouched $W$ is the
   ground truth.
5. **Public contract and hidden suite.** Emit the prompt (inputs, required
   outputs and schemas, precedence rules, allowed tools) and seal, out of the
   agent's sight, metamorphic transforms that must not change the grade and
   plausible-but-wrong stale outputs that must fail.

Why does this give ground truth at every step, and not just at the end?
Because nothing in the environment is independent of $W$. The paper writes an
environment as

$$
E = \bigl(x,\; s_0,\; \operatorname{ver}_W,\; \Phi_W\bigr),
$$

the prompt, the input tree, the strict verifier and the progress potential, all
functions of the same $W$. Rendering round-trips and every corruption is
declared and invertible, so $s_0$ determines $W$: the task is solvable from what
the agent sees, and the generator already holds the answer. Since the DAG says
what the workspace should look like after each stage, every correct
intermediate state can be rendered from $W$ and compared against. That is the
"exact answer key for every step."

A concrete instance: a duplicate-records operator injects copies of rows that
exist in $W$, and the defect plan records which. Removing them restores $W$, and
the grader can check row by row whether the agent's fact table did exactly that.
A prompt-first pipeline has only the agent-visible files and whatever checker
the model wrote.

The caveat: "invertible" is something each generator *declares*; the paper does
not prove it per operator, and "missing records" is only recoverable if the
information survives elsewhere in the files. The check is empirical: a
reference solver and an independently written alternative must produce
identical output trees on every seed, or the environment is rejected. I cannot
check any of this in code. The generators are not released yet: the project page says "Code
soon / Data soon," and the thread says they are "in internal approval, out
within 7 days."

### The eight gates, and what a gaming probe is

An environment is admitted only if it passes eight automatic gates (Appendix
A.6, **reported**): reward causality (each reward component must drop when its
cause is mutated), exploit resistance, role independence (reference solver,
alternative solver, verifier and progress scorer share no code), solver
agreement, progress validity, metamorphic invariance, six-seed diversity, and
public-contract hygiene (no answer material in the prompt or input tree).

The 14 gaming probes are the exploit-resistance gate. Each is a canned cheat
applied to the public tree, and each must score exactly 0 under both the strict
verifier and the progress potential. The paper names six of the 14: empty
output, minimal schema-valid output, copied input, single-seed hard-coding,
path traversal and private lookup. The other eight are "and others." If you
have read [the MiMo audit](/articles/mimo-env-reward-hacking), where a fix
commit sat in `.git` in most of the coding tasks, "private lookup" is the one to
want a list of, and it is not in the paper.

### Is 12.4 minutes "6x the longest prior corpus"?

**Reported** (Table 1): a median rollout on Tev-Gym takes Gemini 3.6 Flash 12.4
minutes of wall-clock agent time; the longest prior corpus is SWE-Smith at 2.0
minutes. **Reasoned:** $12.4 / 2.0 = 6.2$. The project page names SWE-Smith
explicitly. The other corpora sit at 0.5 to 1.9 minutes.

The evaluator is shared; the scaffold is not. Prior corpora ran under Harbor's
`terminus-2` with 60 turns, Tev-Gym in the paper's single-bash harness with a
300-tool-call budget. The paper argues the prior medians would not move under a
larger budget, since 60 turns ends only 1 to 13% of their rollouts. That holds
for medians.

## The progress potential

Each environment ships a *progress contract*: a set of nodes $V$, each a
checkable state of the output workspace, with weights $w_v > 0$ summing to 1 and
prerequisite sets $\mathrm{pre}(v)$. A partial scorer $c_v(s) \in [0, 1]$ grades
the workspace on node $v$ directly against $W$, using set precision and recall
for record-valued outputs and declared tolerances for numbers.

Credit is prerequisite-gated. $\tilde{c}_v(s)$ is the lowest partial score
among $v$ and all of its direct and indirect prerequisites. The potential is
the weighted sum, the paper's Eq. 3:

$$
\Phi(s) = \sum_{v \in V} w_v \, \tilde{c}_v(s) \in [0, 1]
$$

The gating is what makes this hard to farm. A correct aggregate computed on a
wrong fact table earns nothing, because `aggregates` inherits the score of the
`facts` node beneath it. For the warehouse the nine nodes and their weights,
read off Figure 3, are facts 0.18, dimensions 0.15, schema 0.12, summary 0.12,
normalization, relations, aggregates and quality_report at 0.10 each, and
readable at 0.03. **Reasoned:** they sum to 1.00.

Three of the gates exist to make $\Phi$ safe to train on: it is *anchored* (the
untouched tree and every gaming probe score 0; $\Phi = 1$ exactly when the
strict verifier accepts), *monotone* (rises along the reference trajectory,
falls on regression), and *independent* (its scorer shares no code with the
verifier or the solvers). No step labels, no trained reward model, no LLM
judge. The paper's own AI-use statement is explicit: "No generative model
grades any task or produces any training reward."

## PPRS, and whether it is policy-invariant

Let $s_0, \dots, s_T$ be the workspace after each turn, with $\Phi(s_0) = 0$.
Following potential-based shaping, the per-step reward is the change in
potential, $r_t = \Phi(s_t) - \Phi(s_{t-1})$, and the rollout reward is the
paper's Eq. 4:

$$
R(\tau) = \mathbb{1}[\tau \text{ passes}] + \lambda \sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t = \mathbb{1}[\tau \text{ passes}] + \lambda \, \Phi(s_T)
$$

with $\lambda = 0.25$ in every run, and not tuned.

The reference this invites is Ng, Harada and Russell (1999). Their result: add
$F = \gamma \Phi(s') - \Phi(s)$ to the reward and the optimal policy is
unchanged, because along any trajectory the shaping terms telescope to a
constant. That constancy needs the terminal potential pinned (to 0, or to the
same value everywhere). PPRS keeps $\lambda \Phi(s_T)$, which differs between
trajectories. So **PPRS is not policy-invariant in Ng's sense**, and the paper
says so in as many words (Appendix B): "PPRS (Eq. (4)) is not policy-invariant
shaping in the sense of Ng et al. (1999)."

That is deliberate: zero the terminal potential and all-fail groups starve
again. What PPRS preserves is the ranking of individual trajectories. A pass earns
$1 + \lambda = 1.25$ and a failure at most $\lambda = 0.25$, so no single
rollout is rewarded for trading completion for partial progress. What it does
not preserve is the ranking of stochastic policies in expectation. The paper
bounds it: the shaped objective can prefer a policy with pass rate $p_1$ over
one with $p_2 > p_1$ only if

$$
p_2 - p_1 < \frac{\lambda (1 - p_1)}{1 + \lambda} \le \frac{\lambda}{1 + \lambda},
$$

which is 0.2 at $\lambda = 0.25$, and only when the worse policy's failures end
nearly complete while the better one's end near empty. **Reasoned:**
$0.25 / 1.25 = 0.2$, so the bound is right. Since every number reported is a
strict-verifier pass rate, a distortion would show up in the tables; the
sparse-reward ablation is the control.

Because $\Phi$ grades the workspace alone, two routes to the same files earn
the same reward, and a perfect workspace that runs out of context before
`submit` earns $\lambda \Phi(s_T) = 0.25$, not 1.25.

environment: 0.05, 0.26, 0.69, 0.78, 0.90 and 1. Which plateau a rollout reaches

$p = 5\%$ and $G = 8$, most draws produce an all-fail group: the terminal
column is flat zero while the PPRS column ranks the rollouts. Push $\lambda$ to
0 and PPRS collapses back to the terminal reward. Under PPRS a group goes
silent only when every rollout stops on the same plateau.

## What training actually used

The thread motivates PPRS with GRPO. The runs use DPPO, a group-relative
objective with centred advantages and a total-variation bound of 0.1 on the
policy ratio, no KL term (**reported**, Table 11). Per step: 8 tasks, $G = 24$
rollouts each, 192 rollouts, a 65,536-token response budget with 16,384 per
turn, at most 64 tool calls, one bash tool in a `python:3.13-slim` container.
Credit is per turn: turn $t$ gets $\lambda r_t$, the last turn also gets the
terminal term, and the advantage is the return-to-go with $\gamma = 1$ minus the
group mean at the same turn index.

Since all-fail groups are already refilled by active sampling, the honest
statement of what PPRS buys is *cost and feasibility*, and the ablations
(Table 5, on Qwen3.5-9B, **reported**) say so:

| Reward | GPU-h / step | ALE-CM S@1 | ALE-CM P@5 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sparse (terminal only) | 16.7 | 0.25 | 0.06 |
| PPRS, pooled normalisation | 10.9 | 0.28 | 0.06 |
| Direct additive $\Phi(s_t)$, $\gamma = 0$ | 9.7 | 0.29 | 0.06 |
| PPRS, per-turn normalisation (Tev-9B) | 9.0 | 0.29 | 0.11 |

**Reasoned:** sparse reward costs $16.7 / 9.0 = 1.86\times$ the GPU-hours per
step. At 27B the paper says the sparse run "fails to complete a single optimizer
step": active sampling never finds eight groups with any variance. That is the
strongest evidence in the paper for the reward, and it is a sentence, not a
table.

The data ablations make the case for the latent world. A *public-view verifier*
(an LLM writes the checker from the agent's view, everything else kept) discards
88% of sampled groups and lands at S@1 0.24; *prompt-first* generation lands at
0.23; the base model is 0.25 (**reported**). Neither beats doing nothing.

## The numbers, checked

The 27B base is Qwen3.8-27B, the 9B base is Qwen3.5-9B, and Tev-320B is
GLM-5.3-Flash (the model in [this site's GLM-5.3-Flash
walkthrough](/articles/glm-5-3-flash)). **Measured** from the Hugging Face
metadata: `gordonhu/Tev-27B` holds 26,895,998,464 parameters and declares
`base_model: Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B`; `gordonhu/Tev-320B` holds 321,323,031,390, with
288 routed experts and 8 per token in its `config.json`, and declares
`zai-org/GLM-5.3-Flash`. Both are `cc-by-nc-4.0`. The same base was post-trained the same week for knowledge work, with an LLM judge and rubrics instead of verifiable rewards: [Ultramarine 1 Flash](/articles/ultramarine-1-flash).

Now the denominators. ALE-CM, the computing-and-math split of Agents' Last Exam,
is **18 tasks** (8 Math & Science, 5 ML, 5 Coding & System), each run five
times. Pass@5 is the fraction of tasks solved at least once in five tries, so it
moves in steps of 1/18. **Reasoned:** $0.17 \times 18 = 3.06$ and
$0.28 \times 18 = 5.04$. The +65% is **three tasks becoming five**. "#1 on Math &
Science: Pass@5 0.50 vs 0.38" is **four of eight against three of eight**. Every
one of the ten frontier models scores exactly 0.38 there. One task is the whole
lead.

On the same table, Tev-27B's Pass@5 of 0.28 ties GPT-6 Astra, GPT-5.6 (sol) and
Claude Opus 5, and sits below Claude Fable 5 and Claude Sonnet 5 at 0.33. On
mean score S@1 it is 0.40 against 0.50 to 0.62 for the frontier rows; the
"0.40 vs 0.32" in the thread is against the open Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B, which runs
in the same harness. On ML, Pass@1 and Pass@5 are 0.00 before and after
training. The gains are concentrated in Math & Science.

The Terminal-Bench subsets are also small. TB 3.0-CM is 22 tasks and TB 4.0-CM is
18, each with five trials. They were chosen by asking Claude whether each TB 3.0
task belongs to the computing-and-math domain "even if only slightly," then
keeping those a CPU-only harness can run. The paper reports Pearson
$r = 0.96$ and $0.97$ between subset and full-benchmark Pass@1 across leaderboard
models, which is a reasonable defence of the subset as a ranking device. It does
not remove the sampling noise: **reasoned**, Tev-27B's TB 4.0 move from 0.04 to
0.10 is roughly 4 to 9 successful trials out of 90, and because both values are
rounded to two places, the true ratio could sit anywhere from about 2.1x to
3.0x.

Whose numbers are these? On TB 3.0 and 4.0, frontier rows are the official
leaderboard's; GLM 5.2 on TB 4.0 and both Gemini Flash models were run by the
authors (the asterisks). On ALE-CM the frontier models ran in their native
harnesses (Codex, Claude Code, Gemini CLI), every Qwen model in the training
harness, `Vanillux2`. On TB 2.1 (89 tasks, leaderboard frontier rows), Tev-27B's
0.794 against Claude Opus 4.8's 0.789 is a 0.030 gain over a base already at
0.764.

In-domain versus zero-shot matters here. ALE-CM is in-domain by design: the
generators "target ALE-CM," built around its seven computing-and-math
subdomains, and the ethics statement says "part of the training mix is built
against the interfaces of ALE-CM." Decontamination is lexical: no environment
shares an 8-gram or a 13-gram with any evaluation task, and nearest-neighbour
prompt Jaccard to ALE-CM is 0.22 median against 0.28 for ALE-CM tasks compared
with each other (**reported**). That rules out copying, not targeting. The
Terminal-Bench results are the zero-shot ones, and they are the more
interesting evidence that the training generalises.

Finally, the 320B result is mostly reliability. Tev-320B's Pass@5 on TB 4.0-CM
is 0.56 before and after; on TB 3.0-CM it moves 0.50 to 0.55. The paper says it
plainly: training "mainly makes the model solve the tasks it can already reach
more reliably rather than unlocking new ones."

## Where it fits

Most environment work on this site is about scale and containment: [Prime
Intellect's 365,000 environments behind one contract](/articles/scaling-agentic-rl),
[Karotte keeping the answer key in the sandbox](/articles/karotte-rl-environments),
[Skill2Env](/articles/skill2env), [PortSimEnv grading against a proven
optimum](/articles/portsimenv). Tev-Gym works upstream of that. Generate the
answer before the question, and the answer key stops being something you guard
at the end and becomes a grader you consult every turn.

The cost is authoring: one reviewed generator per workflow. Once it exists, an
environment costs a median 30 s of CPU and no model calls (**reported**).

The transferable lesson is narrower than the thread's headline. A terminal
reward on a task your policy solves 3% of the time leaves about half your groups
silent at $G = 24$. If you can say what a correct workspace looks like halfway
through, $\lambda \Phi(s_T)$ gets that half back, at the price of an objective
that is no longer exactly the one you care about. For why GRPO's estimator
behaves this way at the token level, see [Qwen's first-order RL
formulation](/articles/first-order-rl).
