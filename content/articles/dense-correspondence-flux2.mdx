The post that sent me here was a one-line summary of a new paper: FLUX2-4B plus DINOv3, supervised
learning on classical, tracked-video and synthetic datasets, plus teacher-guided iterative
refinement. I expected a DIFT-style recipe. Noise an image, run it through a diffusion model, pick
a layer and a timestep where the features happen to be good, and match those features with
nearest neighbours. Most "diffusion features for correspondence" work since 2023 has
gone like that.

FreeMatching does something else. It never adds noise to anything. It takes the whole FLUX.2
transformer, feeds it two clean images as one token sequence, and fine-tunes every weight until
the output tokens for the second image decode into a map that says, for each of its pixels, where
that pixel came from in the first. There is no layer to pick and the timestep is a constant knob.
The diffusion model is used as a very large, very well-initialised two-image transformer.

The paper is
[Beyond Spatio-Temporal Priors: A Generalizable Approach for Dense Correspondence Matching](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12421)
(arXiv 2610.12421, posted 8 October 2026, accepted at NeurIPS 2026), by Luping Liu, Bingyi Kang,
Yifan Wang and Dong Xu, of the University of Hong Kong and ByteDance Seed. The code is at
[luping-liu/FreeMatching](https://github.com/luping-liu/FreeMatching) under Apache-2.0, and both
checkpoints are on [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/luping-liu/FreeMatching). I read v1 of
the paper, every Python file in the repo, and the checkpoint headers.

<RepoCard repo="luping-liu/FreeMatching" />

The short version of what I found: the mechanism is clean and the gains on its own target task are
large and show up across several metrics. The "competitive on classical benchmarks" claim is
softer than it reads, because the classical tables compare against baselines that were
themselves fine-tuned on edited images, and on optical flow FreeMatching trails the flow
specialists by a wide margin. And the released code disagrees with the paper on three details
that matter if you try to reproduce it.

## The task is edits, not video

The title says "dense correspondence", and the summary mentions tracked video, so I went in
thinking of optical flow. The paper's real target is narrower and newer. It calls it IEG: image
editing and reference-guided generation. You give a model a photo of a horse on grass and ask for
the same horse in snow. You give it a photo of a guitar and a seal and ask for a picture of both on
a beach. Did the output keep the horse, or a horse? Which pixels of the output are the guitar from
the reference, and how well were they preserved?

Answering that needs a correspondence map between the reference and the generated image, and
none of the usual tools were built for it.

The output is defined precisely. Given a source image $I_a$ and a target $I_b$, the model predicts
a backward map $C$, where $C(x, y) = (u, v)$ says that pixel $(x, y)$ of the target came from
absolute position $(u, v)$ in the source, plus a covisibility mask $M$ in $[0, 1]$ that says
whether there is a counterpart at all. Generated content with no source, or background the edit
replaced, gets $M = 0$. The direction matters in practice: the released code documents it as
"target-to-source", and the warp you get out is the source resampled onto the target's grid.

It helps to put the neighbouring tasks side by side, because the brief I was handed for this
article lumped them together and the paper does not.

Optical flow (RAFT, SEA-RAFT, GMFlow) matches consecutive or near-consecutive frames, densely,
and is graded in pixels of end-point error. Point tracking (CoTracker, AllTracker) follows points
through many frames and has to handle occlusion over time. Wide-baseline matching (RoMa, and the
DUSt3R and MASt3R family on the 3D side) matches two views of a static scene where the camera moved
a lot. Semantic correspondence (DIFT, SD-DINO) matches *different instances* of a category: the
left ear of one cat to the left ear of another. FreeMatching's target sits between the last two.
It wants the same instance, like flow and wide-baseline matching, under the arbitrary
transformations of an image model, which is closer to what semantic matchers are used to.

## Why the priors break

"Spatio-temporal priors" is the paper's name for two assumptions classical matchers lean on:
things do not move far between the two images, and the motion field is smooth, or the scene is
rigid. Some of this is architecture. RAFT looks up its correlation volume in a small radius around
the current flow estimate and starts that estimate at zero, so it walks toward the answer from the
same coordinates. Much of it is data. Every flow dataset is two frames of one physical scene,
so a network trained on them learns what physically plausible motion looks like, and treats
anything else as noise.

An edited image breaks both at once. The horse can move across the frame. The background around
it is new, so nothing near the object's old position matches anything. Its pose changes in a way no
camera motion produces.

The explorer below is a toy version of that failure. I built two 32-by-20 "images" out of made-up
feature vectors: every cell of an object carries a feature that encodes which object it belongs
to and where it sits inside that object, so a point keeps its feature wherever the edit moves it.
Background cells carry a feature for "grass" in A and "snow" in B. Click a cell in A and the
heatmap over B is the cosine similarity to every cell. Then three readouts turn that heatmap into a
single match.

The amber box is the small-motion prior made explicit: search only near the same coordinates.
With the motion slider at zero it finds the match. At 30% of the full move, the upper tip of the
star has already travelled about seven cells, and a window of four cells either side has to settle
for the best of a bad neighbourhood. The global argmax, which searches all of B, is unbothered by
distance. So much for the first half of the paper's argument, which is not new: GMFlow made flow a
global matching problem in 2022, and RoMa matches globally from DINOv2 features.

The second half is that a global search is only as good as the features. In the toy the features
are perfect by construction, so the heatmap has one sharp peak until you push the appearance
slider. In real images it is the features that decide, which is why the paper reaches for two
foundation models instead of training a feature extractor from flow data.

the global search has two equally good answers, and the soft-argmax lands between them on empty
snow. Hold on to that.

## Two images, one token sequence

The figure hides the parts that make it work, so I traced the path with the numbers filled in from
the paper's appendix and the code.

Each image is resized to 288 by 512. The frozen FLUX.2 VAE encodes it to a 36 by 64 grid of
32-channel latents, and every 2 by 2 neighbourhood is packed into one 128-dimensional token. That
gives 18 by 32, or 576 tokens per image. A frozen DINOv3 ViT-L/16 runs on the same image and
produces 1024-dimensional patch features on exactly the same 18 by 32 grid, because 16-pixel patches
and an 8x VAE with 2x2 packing land on the same stride. The code throws away the first five DINOv3
tokens, the class token and four registers (`freematching/model.py:117`).

The fusion is the simplest thing that could work. A linear layer maps 1024 DINOv3 dimensions to the
transformer's 3,072, and its output is added to the image tokens right after FLUX's own input
projection:

```python
# freematching/vendor/model_flux2.py:847-859
hidden_states = self.x_embedder(hidden_states)
encoder_hidden_states = self.context_embedder(encoder_hidden_states)

if self.dino_proj is not None and dino_repre is not None:
    im_a_repre = self.dino_proj(dino_repre[0].to(hidden_states.dtype))
    im_b_repre = self.dino_proj(dino_repre[1].to(hidden_states.dtype))
    combined_dino_repre = torch.cat([im_a_repre, im_b_repre], dim=1)
    ...
    hidden_states = hidden_states + combined_dino_repre
```

That projection starts at exactly zero, weight and bias (`freematching/model.py:29-32`). On the
first training step the network is pure FLUX, and DINOv3 is let in only as fast as the gradient
finds it useful. This is the ControlNet trick, and it matters here because adding a random
3,072-wide vector to every token of a pretrained transformer would wreck its first few thousand
steps.

So to answer the question I came in with, which layers and which timesteps: DINOv3 contributes
only its last hidden state, once, at the input. FLUX contributes all of itself. Nothing is read out
of an intermediate layer.

The two 576-token sequences are concatenated into 1,152 tokens. FLUX.2's rotary position ids carry
both the spatial position and which image a token belongs to, so attention can tell "same place in
the other image" from "same place in this image". The transformer is FLUX.2-klein's, unchanged in
shape: five double-stream blocks and twenty single-stream blocks, hidden size 3,072, 24 heads of
128, which I read from the checkpoint and the base model's config.

The text path is still there, and still fed. FLUX expects a prompt, so the code encodes an empty
string with the base model's Qwen3 text encoder, keeps two tokens of it, and passes them on every
call. The timestep input gets a constant. Then, of the 1,152 output tokens, the code keeps the
target's 576 (`freematching/model.py:163-171`), undoes the VAE's latent normalisation, unpacks them
back to a 32-channel 36 by 64 grid, and hands that to a small convolutional decoder.

### The matching head is attention

There is no cost volume in FreeMatching, no argmax and no iteration. Every matcher I have worked
with ends in one of those. RAFT refines a flow field over 12 or more recurrent updates. RoMa
regresses a coarse warp from global matching and then refines it at several scales. FreeMatching
makes one forward pass and regresses coordinates.

The matching happens inside the transformer. When a target token attends to the 576 source tokens,
the attention weights are a softmax over similarities, which is the heatmap in the explorer above,
computed in 24 heads at every one of 25 blocks. Whatever the network does with them, it writes into
the target's tokens something that the decoder can read as two coordinates and a confidence. The
attention is global by construction: no window, no pyramid, no starting guess.

The decoder (`freematching/flow_decoder.py:73-161`) is about 2.4M parameters of convolutions. Three
stages each upsample 2x, which takes 36 by 64 back to 288 by 512, and each stage adds its own
prediction of the coordinates and the mask to the upsampled previous one, so gradients reach the
coarse levels directly. A last block adds a correction squashed by a tanh, where `flow_scale` is 4 pixels expressed in
normalised coordinates, so it can move a prediction by at most 4 pixels:

```python
# freematching/flow_decoder.py:149-159
flow_delta = self.flow_refine(torch.cat([x, flow], dim=1))
...
flow = flow + torch.tanh(flow_delta) * flow_scale
```

The output is three channels: normalised source coordinates for x and y, and a covisibility
logit. I counted the released checkpoint's tensors from its safetensors header: 257 tensors and
3,881,134,812 parameters in BF16, of which 2,433,651 are the decoder and 3,148,800 the DINOv3
projection. Everything else is the fine-tuned FLUX transformer. The VAE, DINOv3 and the text encoder
are fetched separately and stay frozen.

### What "timestep" means when there is no noise

Since nothing is noised, the timestep is just a conditioning value. It sets the adaptive layer-norm
modulation in every block, the way a noise level would during generation. The paper says it is a
fixed $t = 500$. The code says 620: it is the default in both training scripts, in the inference
CLI and in both checkpoints' `config.json`, and the README calls 620 the value "matching the
original checkpoint evaluation". I would trust the code. The paper's value looks like it came from
an earlier draft.

Why does a constant timestep matter at all? Because FLUX's weights learned different jobs at
different noise levels: near the clean end they sharpen detail, near the noisy end they lay out
structure. Picking a mid-to-late value starts the fine-tune from the behaviour that already
"knows" about object layout in a clean image. The paper does not ablate the choice, so that is my
reading, not theirs.

## Nine datasets, one Huber loss

Stage 1 is supervised. The mixture samples from nine sources, and the proportions in the paper's
Table 1 match `configs/datasets.json` exactly:

| Source | Share | What it teaches |
|---|---|---|
| ScanNet++ | 15% | indoor wide-baseline geometry |
| BlendedMVS | 10% | outdoor multi-view geometry |
| FlyingThings | 10% | synthetic flow, large motion |
| DynamicReplica | 10% | synthetic video, deforming people and animals |
| Spring | 5% | high-resolution flow |
| PointOdyssey | 5% | long synthetic tracks |
| FlyingChairs | 5% | the classic flow pretraining set |
| Video (theirs) | 20% | real non-rigid motion, pseudo-labelled |
| Blender (theirs) | 20% | clean multi-object rigid motion |

The two new sets carry 40% of the samples. The video set is 520k clips from ViPE, labelled by
running AllTracker with 16-frame windows and four refinement iterations, then sampling frame pairs
at varied gaps. This is how "tracked video" gets into a pairwise matcher: a dense point tracker
labels pairs far apart in time, so the pairs have the large, non-rigid motion that flow datasets
lack. The labels are a tracker's guesses, and they are not released. The README says the videos and
trajectories cannot be distributed for size and copyright reasons, so you get the conversion code
and have to source the clips yourself.

The Blender set is the clean counterweight: 360k pairs from Objaverse, two or three objects per
scene, each object rotated by 30 to 50 degrees and rescaled by a factor of 0.8 to 1.2 between the two
renders, with ground-truth flow from Cycles' motion vector pass and occlusion from alpha. Asset
filtering throws out flat and needle-like meshes. It is the dataset I would have built for this
task: independent motion of several objects, with exact labels.

Mixing pseudo-labels with exact labels is why the loss is Huber rather than L2. Its threshold
adapts: the code keeps an exponential moving average of the L1 error and sets the Huber delta to
twice that (`train_flow_flux.py:672-674`). Errors bigger than about twice the typical error are
penalised linearly, so a bad AllTracker label pulls on the gradient with a bounded force.

The paper's loss is a weighted sum of that Huber term on coordinates and an L2 term on the mask,
with weights 10 and 0.2. The Stage 1 script computes something a little different:

```python
# train_flow_flux.py:682
loss = loss_h * 10 + loss_mask * 0.33  + loss_grad * 1.0
```

The mask weight is 0.33, not 0.2, and there is a third term, a loss on the spatial gradient of the
flow field that the paper does not mention at all. That term penalises the difference between the
predicted and true flow gradients, so it pushes the predicted field to be as smooth, or as sharp,
as the label. The training guide acknowledges the gap in its own words: it "retains its actual loss
computation, including any gradient regularization". I do not think the gradient term changes the
story. It is the kind of detail you want to know before trying to reproduce Table 6.

## The teacher only has to fix small mistakes

Stage 1 sees no edited images. Stage 2 is where the model meets them, and the trick is how it gets
a training signal with no ground-truth correspondence.

<Figure

Below each round, an Initial Warp and a Refined Warp of the phone are shown; the refined warps are cleaner around the edges and the red call button."
  caption="Teacher-guided iterative refinement. An EMA copy of the student makes the big jump (Initial Warp). A frozen teacher only estimates the small correction between that warp and the target (Refined Warp). The composition becomes the student's target, and as the student improves, so does the EMA's starting point (FreeMatching paper, Figure 3)."
/>

Three networks are involved. The student is the model being trained. An EMA copy of it, updated
with decay 0.95 after every step, predicts an initial correspondence $C_0$. The source is warped
with $C_0$, giving an intermediate image that should already look like the target, roughly.

A frozen teacher then matches the target against that intermediate image and returns a residual
map $S$. The pseudo-label is the composition, $C_\text{new} = C_0 \circ S$: first follow the
teacher's small correction, then the EMA's big jump. The student is trained with the same adaptive
Huber loss toward $C_\text{new}$, but only where the teacher's confidence passes 0.2 and the EMA's
covisibility passes 0.7 (`train_flow_flux_roma.py:812-817`), and its mask is pulled toward the
EMA's.

I like this design, and the reason is the paper's own title. A classical matcher fails on an edit
because the two images are far apart. After the EMA's warp, they are not: the remaining error is
local, a few pixels of misalignment around the edges of an object that is already in the right
place. A matcher with spatio-temporal priors is good in exactly this regime. The paper
puts the prior where it holds and lets the foundation model do the part where it does not.

Two details from the code. The teacher in the paper is RoMa. The teacher in the code is
`romav2.RoMaV2` in its "fast" setting (`freematching/training/teacher.py:130-157`), and the
training guide says outright that this "backend name documents the implementation being
distributed; it is distinct from naming the RoMa baseline in the paper". And Stage 2 is not all
edited pairs: its loop runs three supervised updates for every weak one (`--sup_steps_per_cycle 3` and
`--weak_steps_per_cycle 1` in the guide's command), so about a quarter of what Stage 2 sees are IEG
pairs. The other three quarters keep the classical skills from eroding. The released Stage 2 model
is the EMA after 10,000 updates, starting from the Stage 1 checkpoint at step 275,000, with the
decoder frozen.

## What the tables show

### On edits

IEG-Bench is about 100 hard pairs drawn from the multi-reference subset of UNO's data, held out
from the single-reference subset used for refinement. There is no ground truth, so quality is
measured by reconstruction: warp the source with the predicted map and compare it with the target
inside the covisible region.

| Model | Ref-MSE ↓ | LPIPS ↓ | SigLIP 2 ↑ | DINOv3 ↑ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Identity (no warp) | 23.65 | 81.97 | 86.67 | 52.38 |
| SEA-RAFT | 18.49 | 72.45 | 85.18 | 58.14 |
| UniMatch | 11.07 | 58.42 | 87.86 | 66.35 |
| RoMa | 8.08 | 48.91 | 87.73 | 70.15 |
| UFM | 10.41 | 53.05 | 86.97 | 66.77 |
| FreeMatching | 6.22 | 45.41 | 90.77 | 80.01 |

FreeMatching wins every column, and by a lot on the two that move most: Ref-MSE is about 23% below
RoMa's and the DINOv3 score is nearly ten points higher. The qualitative figure is more convincing
than the numbers. In row after row, the flow models produce silhouettes filled with the wrong
content, RoMa and UFM tear the object into pieces, and FreeMatching gets the whole object.

Table 6 builds the system one component at a time, scored by the DINOv3 metric: a randomly
initialised DiT trained on classical data gets 67.31, FLUX initialisation brings it to 69.28,
DINOv3 inputs to 72.76, the video and Blender data to 76.52, and refinement to 80.01. Each step is
worth two to four points, and the two foundation models together are worth less than the new data
plus refinement.

The paper's human study points the same way: ten raters scored warps blind on a 0 to 3 scale, and
the mean opinion scores were 2.25 for FreeMatching, 1.39 for RoMa, 1.13 for UFM and 0.49 for
SEA-RAFT. Their Pearson correlation between the human ratings and the DINOv3 score is
$r = 0.738$, though the paper does not say whether that is computed per pair or per method.

### Things I would want a reviewer to ask

The DINOv3 score is computed with DINOv3, and FreeMatching is the only model that is given DINOv3
features as input. A model whose warps look good to DINOv3 has an obvious advantage on that column,
and Table 6, the ablation, is scored only with it. The paper does answer this in a second
comparison, Table 5, which uses DINOv2 and OpenCLIP instead. There, FreeMatching scores 54.31 and
70.56 against UFM's 39.65 and 56.48 and RoMa's 47.14 and 61.18. The lead survives a change of
judge, so I would not dismiss the main table on this ground. I would not use the ablation's
2-to-4-point steps to argue that one component matters more than another, either.

The reference masks were seeded by FreeMatching. The appendix says so: "FreeMatching predictions
provide initial candidates", which human annotators then screened, edited and froze before
applying them to every method. The finalisation by hand makes this less of a problem than it
sounds, and Full-MSE, which uses no mask at all, also favours FreeMatching (10.99 against 13.48 and
16.57). Still, a mask drawn around where your own model found a counterpart is a mask that will
never penalise you for the regions you missed.

The teacher is a matcher it is compared against. FreeMatching after Stage 1 scores a Ref-MSE of
8.08 in Table 10, the same 8.08 RoMa scores off the shelf in Table 4. All of the measured lead on
that metric arrives in Stage 2, the stage in which a RoMa-family model labels the pairs. It is a
legitimate method, distillation from a strong local matcher wrapped around a global one. It also
means a better teacher would probably lift the student, and the paper's "beyond priors" framing
undersells how much the prior-based model still contributes. One more number from Table 10 belongs
here: refining on unlabelled *flow* pairs instead of edits already brings Ref-MSE from 8.08 to
7.26. About 44% of the Stage 2 gain comes from running the self-training loop at all, on any data.

Table 5 also fine-tunes UFM and RoMa with the same 10k-step IEG adaptation, which is the fairest
comparison in the paper. The odd thing is that RoMa gets *worse* from it (8.08 to 9.70 Ref-MSE),
and UFM gets better (10.41 to 8.55). The paper does not comment. It suggests the adaptation recipe
was tuned for FreeMatching, which would be normal and is worth knowing.

### On classical benchmarks

This is where I would read the paper most carefully. The headline claim is that a single model
"achieves performance competitive with SOTA methods on classical optical flow and dense matching
benchmarks".

The first thing I noticed is that the UFM and RoMa rows in Tables 2 and 3 are not the published
models. Their numbers are identical, to the last digit, to the rows labelled "UFM (10k-IEG)" and
"RoMa (10k-IEG)" in Table 9: the versions fine-tuned on edited images. The text says it, "lower EPE
than the similarly adapted UFM and RoMa variants", but the table captions do not, and off-the-shelf
numbers for those two are not given anywhere. For a reader comparing against what they would
download, the right comparison is missing.

On dense matching it is a real win. ScanNet-1500 EPE is 8.56 against 12.03 for both adapted
baselines, and ETH3D is 10.23 against RoMa's 11.26. Pose AUC is a tie with RoMa, which comes out
slightly ahead at 20 degrees on both sets, 78.61 against 78.23 on ETH3D and 70.90 against 70.03 on
ScanNet.

On optical flow it is not competitive with the flow models, and the paper's own text half-admits
it ("UniMatch achieves the lowest EPE among the methods shown"):

| Method | Sintel clean EPE | Sintel final EPE | KITTI-2015 EPE |
|---|---|---|---|
| SEA-RAFT | 0.43 | 0.81 | 1.97 |
| UniMatch | 0.40 | 0.75 | 1.52 |
| UFM (10k-IEG) | 0.92 | 1.48 | 4.75 |
| FreeMatching | 1.05 | 1.36 | 4.95 |

FreeMatching's Sintel clean EPE is about 2.6 times UniMatch's, and its KITTI EPE about 3.3 times.
It is in the same band as the adapted generalist UFM, which is a fair peer, and far from the flow
specialists. I would call that "retains usable flow", not "competitive with SOTA".

Several things I could not check would matter for the flow numbers. The paper says the authors
"recompute the results ourselves" on covisible regions only, which is not the official protocol;
it does not say which checkpoint of each baseline was used; and EPEs as low as SEA-RAFT's 0.43 on
Sintel usually come from a checkpoint that saw Sintel in training, which would make those rows
in-domain while FreeMatching's are zero-shot. It also does not say at what resolution each
baseline ran or how many refinement iterations RAFT-family models were given at test time.
FreeMatching itself works at 288 by 512, which is less than a third of a Sintel frame's pixels
(1024 by 436) and less than a third of KITTI's. Sub-pixel EPE at the original resolution, from a
prediction made on a smaller grid, is fighting with one hand tied. No evaluation code is released,
so none of this can be read off the repo.

And the baselines the brief asked me about mostly are not there. RAFT appears only through
SEA-RAFT. CoTracker is cited as background for tracked video, not compared; AllTracker is a data
labeller, not a baseline. DUSt3R and VGGT appear in related work only, MASt3R not at all. DIFT is
not compared either. The one semantic matcher shown is SD-DINO, in a single qualitative example in
the appendix, where it is run with text prompts and loses. For an edit-matching paper the missing
semantic-correspondence comparison is the gap I would most like to see filled.

## What a pair costs

The paper is upfront that it is slow, and the appendix measures it: on one H20 at 288 by 512 with
batch 1, FreeMatching takes 121.9 ms a pair and peaks at 15.18 GiB of allocated memory. UFM takes
51.3 ms and 2.35 GiB, and RoMa 102.5 ms and 2.58 GiB. So it is about 2.4 times slower than UFM and
uses about 6.5 times its memory, at a resolution most flow benchmarks would call small.

I tried to account for the 15.18 GiB from the files. The checkpoint is 7,762,299,928 bytes. DINOv3
ViT-L/16 is 1.21 GB in FP32 on the hub, about 0.6 GB once the loader casts it to BF16. The VAE is
another 0.17 GB at most. Together, about 7.9 GiB of weights touched per forward pass. Attention over
1,152 tokens and a 96-channel decoder at 288 by 512 cost tens of megabytes, not gigabytes. I can't
say what the other half of the peak is; the profiling script is not in the repo.

One thing the released loader does that the paper does not mention: to get the empty-prompt
embedding, `build_model_stack` loads FLUX.2-klein's Qwen3 text encoder, 8,044,981,992 bytes of
BF16, onto the GPU alongside everything else (`freematching/model.py:41-80`), encodes the empty
string once, and deletes it. During startup that is roughly 15.4 GiB of weights resident at once.
The paper says the embeddings are cached. In the released code they are cached only after that
first load, so if you are trying to fit this on a 16 GB card, precompute the two-token embedding
once and skip the text encoder entirely.

Resolution is the other cost. Every image is resized to 288 by 512, and the code requires
multiples of 16. Attention cost grows with the square of the token count, so doubling each side to
576 by 1024 quadruples the tokens to 2,304 per image and multiplies the attention work by sixteen.
The model was trained only at 288 by 512, too, so going higher is an extrapolation in both cost and
accuracy.

Training is the cost most people will not pay. The paper reports 768 A100 GPU-hours for 150k Stage 1
updates and 80 for Stage 2, on 16 A100s. Then it adds that these figures describe "the original
training configuration reported in the rebuttal", not the released run. The released Stage 1
checkpoint is at step 275,000, nearly twice as many. If the rate held, that is around 1,400 A100
hours, a figure I computed and the paper does not give. And the video data has to be re-collected
and re-tracked by you.

## Where I would use it

As a metric, today. The paper's last application is the one I find most immediately useful: warp
the reference onto an edited image and look at the per-patch DINOv3 similarity inside the
covisible region. Global scores like a CLIP or DINO similarity on the whole image average away a
changed logo or a missing stripe. A spatial map does not.

For a team evaluating subject-driven generation or image editing models, that is a real tool: it
says not only that identity drifted, but where. At 121.9 ms a pair it is cheap enough for an eval
set of thousands.

As an optical flow estimator, no. The specialists are two to three times more accurate on Sintel
and KITTI, faster, and run at the resolution you have. As a wide-baseline matcher for pose, it
ties RoMa at about six times the memory. If you are already computing correspondences for
[dense 3D tracking](/articles/trackeverything) or [reconstruction](/articles/3d-reconstruction-roundup),
this is not the model to swap in.

Then there is the toy's second star. FreeMatching regresses one answer per pixel. When an edit
duplicates an object, which image generators do readily, a single-answer regressor has to pick one
copy or, like the soft-argmax in the explorer, blur toward the middle. The paper knows this:
Appendix F sketches a diffusion version that would sample one hypothesis per noise seed, then
notes that "all reported experiments use the efficient single-pass regression model". A model built
from a diffusion transformer, running with the diffusion switched off, would be the obvious place
to put it back.

The broader lesson is the one that keeps coming up on this site. [Marigold V2](/articles/marigold-v2)
turned a 20B image-editing transformer into a depth estimator. [RelateAnything](/articles/relate-anything)
built a scene-graph model on DINOv3. Here a 4B image generator, with DINOv3 bolted onto its input
and all of its weights fine-tuned, becomes a matcher. The generative pretraining is doing the work
that used to be done by hand-designed cost volumes. The bill comes in memory, and in the fact that
the result runs at a resolution its pretraining chose.

<Callout type="note">
The FreeMatching checkpoints are Apache-2.0, as is FLUX.2-klein-base-4B. DINOv3 is gated behind
Meta's own licence and access form, and you need it at inference: the projection was trained on its
features. The tracked-video training data is not distributed.
</Callout>

## How I checked

I read v1 of the paper in arXiv's HTML and its LaTeX source, and took Figures 1 to 5 from the
source PDFs. I shallow-cloned `luping-liu/FreeMatching` (commit `20add551`, 8 October 2026) and
read the model, decoder, teacher, both training scripts, the dataset config and the docs; I did not
run any of it. The parameter counts are from the safetensors header of the Stage 2 checkpoint, read
with an HTTP range request, and the base model's shape is from its `transformer/config.json` on
Hugging Face. The weight sizes for DINOv3, the VAE and the Qwen3 text encoder are the file sizes the
Hugging Face API lists. Everything in the "what the tables show" section is the paper's own numbers;
the relative figures (23%, 2.6 and 3.3 times, 44%, 2.4 and 6.5 times, about 1,400 GPU-hours) are my

baselines were evaluated, because no evaluation code is released.
