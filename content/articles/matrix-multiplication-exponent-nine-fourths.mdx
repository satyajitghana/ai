[Deedy Das posted](https://x.com/deedydas/status/2125445769629204816) on 25 November: "OpenAI just dropped a 111-page proof of a 57-year old math problem: the matrix multiplication exponent (ω) is exactly 9/4." The paper is [The matrix multiplication exponent is nine fourths](https://github.com/openai/math/tree/main/preprints/The-matrix-multiplication-exponent-is-nine-fourths-November-24-2026), added to [openai/math](https://github.com/openai/math) on 24 November with six companion papers. A few hours earlier Dan Roberts of OpenAI had [pointed at the repository's history file](https://x.com/danintheory/status/2125383890416337199), which lists two new result families: number 379, tensor geometry, which holds this paper, and number 380, linear programming.

The page count is right (I checked the PDF: 111 pages). The rest of the post needs one correction before anything else. The upper bound, $\omega \le 9/4$, is not new. OpenAI claimed it on 2 October, and by 6 November six groups of human authors had reproved or extended it on arXiv. What this paper adds is the other side, $\omega \ge 9/4$. That lower bound is the extraordinary claim, because nearly everyone who works on the problem expected $\omega = 2$.

I read the paper's LaTeX source, the Comparator challenge file, the Lean scope documents and the solution tree, and I searched for reactions. I did not referee 111 pages of new mathematics, and nobody else has had time to either.

## The question

Two $n\times n$ matrices have $n^2$ entries in their product, and each entry is a dot product of length $n$, so the schoolbook method costs about $n^3$ multiplications. The exponent $\omega$ is the smallest number such that the product can be computed in $O(n^{\omega+\varepsilon})$ arithmetic operations for every $\varepsilon > 0$. Since you must at least write the answer down, $\omega \ge 2$. Since the schoolbook method works, $\omega \le 3$. The paper's definition (its §1) counts additions, subtractions, multiplications and divisions in a formal circuit, where every output has to equal its product entry as an identity of polynomials, even over a finite field. That is the standard algebraic model. It says nothing about bits, caches or memory traffic.

For scale, $9/4 = 2.25$. The conjecture people wrote down for decades was $\omega = 2$: that multiplying matrices is, up to $n^{\varepsilon}$, no harder than reading them. The paper's first paragraph says it shows instead that "the arithmetic exponent exceeds the exponent of the output size by exactly one quarter."

## Fifty-seven years of upper bounds

Strassen started it in 1969. He found seven products of sums that give a $2\times2$ product instead of eight, and since the identity never uses commutativity, the blocks can themselves be matrices. Recursing gives $7^k$ multiplications at size $2^k$, so $\omega \le \log_2 7 \approx 2.807$. The [openai-math roundup](/articles/openai-math#what-ω-measures-and-why-it-moved-so-slowly) has a widget that runs his identity on numbers you type; there is a smaller one at $n = 2$ in the size widget further down.

The decade after Strassen found savings by multiplying several matrix products at once. The paper's history section names the steps: Pan's aggregation of trilinear forms, Bini's approximate algorithms (border rank, turned into exact ones by interpolation), Schönhage's asymptotic sum inequality, which converts a direct sum of small products into a bound for one big one. By 1981 the bound was 2.522. Then Strassen's laser method (1986) and the Coppersmith–Winograd tensor (1990) brought it to 2.375477, and the next thirty-six years went into analysing higher and higher powers of that one tensor: Stothers, Vassilevska Williams, Le Gall, Alman, Duan, Wu, Zhou and others, down to 2.371177 by Dupont et al. in August 2026. The whole post-1990 stretch moved the bound by 0.0043 (my subtraction).

That route had known limits. Ambainis, Filmus and Le Gall showed that a broad class of laser-method analyses of Coppersmith–Winograd powers cannot go below 2.3078, and Alman's barrier puts any method that goes through a CW tensor above 2.168. Getting to 2.25 needed a different road.

## October: 9/4 from above, and the humans who checked it

OpenAI's [first 9/4 paper](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/Matrix-Multiplication-Nine-Fourths-October-2-2026/paper.pdf) (2 October, 13 pages, family 107 in the release) took that road. It never builds an algorithm. It uses Strassen's duality theorem: the asymptotic rank of a tensor equals the largest value any point of the asymptotic spectrum gives it, where those points are maps that add on direct sums, multiply on tensor products and never increase under restriction. So $\omega \le 9/4$ follows if every such point gives the $n\times n$ product at most $n^{9/4}$, and the paper shows that by watching how these points value polynomial multiplication. I went through that proof in detail in the [roundup's matrix-multiplication section](/articles/openai-math#what-ω-measures-and-why-it-moved-so-slowly), including its last page, which is a squeeze between $a^{4/3}$ and $(2a-1)^{1/t}$.

What has happened to it since is the most reassuring part of this story. Within five weeks, six papers by outside authors reproved or extended the upper bound:

- Przemysław Uznański, [arXiv:2610.10494](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10494) (7 October): rectangular products, $\omega(1,k,1) \le 1 + k + \frac{1}{4k}$ for $k \ge \frac12$, so the dual exponent $\alpha \ge 1/2$. He uses it to run Zwick's all-pairs shortest paths in $O(n^{2.5})$.
- Houxi Lü, [arXiv:2610.18914](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18914) (12 October): the shared-leg separation step without roots of unity, so $\omega \le 9/4$ in every characteristic.
- Rafael Pass, [arXiv:2610.28627](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28627) (20 October): "a self-contained constructive proof", with an explicit Strassen-style identity and ordinary block recursion, so an actual $O(n^{9/4+\gamma})$ algorithm for integer matrices.
- Alimzhan Amanov, Maxim van den Berg and Jeroen Zuiddam, [arXiv:2610.30054](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30054) (21 October): $\omega_{\mathbb F} \le 9/4$ over every field, the full rectangular picture, and a barrier showing "the current analysis of all these upper bounds" is optimal.
- Baitian Li, [arXiv:2611.04175](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.04175) (3 November): the heart of it as an "untwisting lemma for semidirect sums of tensors".
- Mahmut Levent Doğan, Vladimir Lysikov, Keiya Sakabe and Michael Walter, [arXiv:2611.09716](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09716) (6 November): a proof through a fractional, symmetrized version of Coppersmith and Winograd's tensor value instead of spectral points.

Six write-ups by outside authors, one of them constructive and one by a group that studies the asymptotic spectrum for a living, is about as much checking as an unrefereed result gets in five weeks. I treat $\omega \le 9/4$ as established. The new paper says the same: "The upper bound was known."

## What the new paper claims

Theorem 1.1, verbatim: "Over every field $F$, the exponent of square matrix multiplication is $\omega_F=9/4$." The new content is a matching lower bound, and the paper states it at every finite size too (Corollary 1.2):

- every formal arithmetic circuit for the $n\times n$ product, for every $n \ge 2$, has at least $\lceil n^{9/4}/2\rceil$ multiplication or division gates, even if additions are free;
- every exact bilinear algorithm uses at least $\lceil n^{9/4}\rceil$ products;
- the geometric border rank of the matrix multiplication tensor $T_n$ is at least $n^{9/4}$, and its asymptotic rank over the base field is exactly $n^{9/4}$.

The factor of two between circuits and bilinear algorithms comes from a short lemma (Lemma 2.4): evaluate the circuit at $A + uX$ and $B + vY$ with $u^2 = v^2 = 0$, and each multiplication or division gate contributes at most two new products of a form in $X$ with a form in $Y$.

These are strong statements at small sizes, so I checked them against what was known. The previous lower bounds the paper itself cites are Bläser's rank bound $\frac52 n^2 - 3n$ over every field and Landsberg and Michałek's border-rank bound $2n^2 - \lceil\log_2 n\rceil - 1$ over $\mathbb C$. Constant factors above $n^2$ vanish when you take $j$-th roots along $T_n^{\otimes j} \cong T_{n^j}$, which is why nobody could get a lower bound on $\omega$ itself above 2. The widget computes the claimed floor exactly (as the least $r$ with $r^4 \ge n^9$, so no floating-point rounding) and compares.

<RankFloor />

At $n = 2$ the claimed bilinear floor is 5, and Strassen's algorithm uses 7, so no contradiction. At $n = 3$ it is 12, at $n = 4$ it is 23. The claimed floor first exceeds the Landsberg–Michałek border-rank bound at $n = 16$, where it is $16^{9/4} = 512$ against 507, and from there it pulls away: at $n = 64$ it is 11,586 against 8,185 (all four numbers my arithmetic from the formulas). So for border rank, at every side from 16 up, this is a bound nobody had. For ordinary rank the old bounds hold out longer: Bläser's $\frac52 n^2 - 3n$ stays at or above the claimed floor until $n = 33$, and the claim passes it for good from $n = 34$ (my arithmetic again). Either way these are statements at a fixed size, where a single counterexample would be a finite object. The paper makes one such comparison itself: the first version of a preprint by Sun proposed a dyadic border-rank estimate of $2^{17}$ at $n = 2^8$, and Corollary 1.2 gives $2^{18}$ there.

The paper draws consequences that show how far this reaches:

1. **Strassen's conjecture for tight tensors is false.** The paper assigns integer weights to the three factors of $T_n$ that make its support tight, notes that each flattening has rank $n^2$, and points out that Strassen's 1994 conjecture would then give asymptotic rank $n^2$. Corollary 1.2 gives $n^{9/4}$. That conjecture was one of the standard reasons to believe $\omega = 2$.
2. **Rectangular products have an exact formula.** The paper itself proves $\frac12 \le \alpha_F \le \frac23$ for the dual exponent, the largest $\beta$ for which an $n\times n^{\beta}$ by $n^{\beta}\times n$ product costs $n^{2+o(1)}$. The Lean scope document for family 379 goes further and states $\alpha(F) = 1/2$ and, for weights sorted as $x \ge y \ge z > 0$,

$$
\omega(F;a,b,c) = x + y + \frac{\bigl[\max\bigl(0,(x+y)z - xy\bigr)\bigr]^2}{4xyz}.
$$

I expanded that by hand. When the bracket is positive it equals $\frac{x+y+z}{2} + \frac14\bigl(\frac{xz}{y} + \frac{yz}{x} + \frac{xy}{z}\bigr)$, which is exactly the upper bound Amanov, van den Berg and Zuiddam state in their abstract, and at weights $(1,1,k)$ it reduces to Uznański's $1 + k + \frac{1}{4k}$. So the claim is that the human upper bounds of October are tight everywhere, not just at the square. (Lilting.ch, below, checked the same identity in SymPy.)

## How the lower bound goes

The proof is a contradiction, and its shape is short even though its parts are long. Section 2 of the paper sets it up in two theorems that pull in opposite directions.

The first is a lower bound with no matrices in it. Take $2^w$ words of $2w$ bits each and ask whether they are all distinct. Write the test as a tensor network: vertices carry tables over bits with at most three bits each, wires join pairs of positions, and the network sums over internal bits. Theorem 2.2 says any network that tests $t$ independent lists at once, even one allowed to share wiring across lists, has volume above $t\,n_w\,w^{\sigma}$ for a fixed $\sigma > 0$, uniformly for $1 \le t \le 2^w$. Sorting gives a network of size about $n_w w^2$ (Batcher's sorting network, then compare neighbours), so the truth sits somewhere between $w^{\sigma}$ and $w^2$ per input bit.

The second says what a fast matrix product would buy. Theorem 2.1: if $\omega < 9/4$ over a finite field, then any trivalent network of size $m$ on $e$ exterior bits can be rewritten, for some tensor power, as a network whose size per copy is $m h^{-\gamma} + e h^{\eta}$, with $h \approx \log_2 n$ and $\eta$ as small as you like. That saves a power of the logarithm on the old size and charges an arbitrarily small power per bit. Apply it a fixed number of times to the sorting network and the cost per input bit falls to $O(w^{\eta})$ with $\eta < \sigma$, which contradicts Theorem 2.2. Proposition 2.3 is that half-page calculation.

The passage from finite fields to every field is classical: a circuit with a strict saving gives a bilinear identity with a strict rank saving at one fixed size, its coefficients generate a finitely generated ring, and reducing modulo a maximal ideal lands in a finite field where the identity still holds (Lemma 2.5, via Zariski's lemma).

Everything difficult is in Theorem 2.1, and that is what Parts I to IV of the paper prove. The organising picture is the paper's Figure 1.

<Figure src="/articles/matrix-multiplication-exponent-nine-fourths/fig1.png" alt="Two diagrams. Assign each tensor a price: the logarithm of a point of Strassen's asymptotic spectrum. Put the three factors $A, B, C$ at sites in some space. A wire, an index shared by two factors, costs per bit the distance between its sites. $T_n$ has three wires, so its price is the perimeter of the triangle times $\log_2 n$. A rank decomposition with $r$ terms builds the same tensor from a single label shared by all three sites; if that label costs $D$ per bit, monotonicity gives

$$
(\text{perimeter}) \cdot \log_2 n \le D \cdot \log_2 r, \qquad\text{so}\qquad \omega \ge \frac{\text{perimeter}}{D}.
$$

In Euclidean space this only ever gives 2. The companion paper, [Euclidean geometry in the asymptotic spectrum of tensors](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/Euclidean-geometry-in-the-asymptotic-spectrum-of-tensors-November-24-2026/paper.pdf) (86 pages), proves that a bit shared by several sites costs the normalised mean width of their convex hull, which for a triangle is half its perimeter. The critical case for 9/4 is the three tips of a tripod with unit arms: each pair of tips is 2 apart, the perimeter is 6, and the paper says "If the shared-label rate is $8/3$, the ratio is $6/(8/3)=9/4$." Under the hypothesis $\omega < 9/4$, such a tripod cannot occur, and Part I turns that exclusion into a way to price a whole family of "sphere tensors" at once.

Parts II to IV then build the compression: sorting records and batched table lookup costed with incidence inequalities from sphere tensors (the key estimate is a stable sort at $O(u^p)$ per record with $p < 2$, which matters because the controlled quantity is the square of a leg width), concentrating the incident bits of the network at fewer stations, and finally rounds of rewriting that add up to Theorem 2.1.

A strict saving below 9/4 forces sphere prices; sorting and concentration give two estimates on the same network; together they compress tensor powers far enough to break the list-distinctness bound (The matrix multiplication exponent is nine fourths, Figure 2)." />

This structure is where I would look for trouble. It leans on the release's own companion papers: Theorem 2.2's proof cites the tensor-geometry paper and [Circuit lower bounds for Hamiltonian cycle and the permanent](https://github.com/openai/math/blob/main/preprints/Circuit-lower-bounds-for-Hamiltonian-cycle-and-the-permanent-November-24-2026/paper.pdf), both posted the same day, both unrefereed. The seven papers of family 379 together run to 418 pages, and they also claim VP ≠ VNP over every field and the Erdős–Rado sunflower conjecture. And the release has withdrawn papers before: on 7 October it pulled three Hodge-conjecture manuscripts after a sign error in one of them invalidated two that depended on it. On paper alone, a 418-page web of mutually citing preprints claiming three famous results in one day is exactly where I would expect a gap.

On paper alone. The Lean changes that.

## What Lean checks

The release verifies its formal claims with [Comparator](https://github.com/leanprover/comparator): a challenge file states the theorem against Mathlib with `sorry`, a solution module proves it, and Comparator checks that the two statements match and that the proof uses only permitted axioms. The challenge for this result is `lean/ComparatorChallenges/MatrixExponent.lean`, and it is 136 lines I could read in one sitting. It defines a straight-line program with five gate types (a constant from $F$, an input, add, subtract, multiply), charges 1 for each add, subtract or multiply and 0 for constants and inputs, and calls a program correct when every output register's formal polynomial equals $\sum_j X_{ij} X_{jk}$ in `MvPolynomial`. Then:

```lean
def formalOmega (F : Type*) [Field F] (w12 w23 w31 : ℝ) : ℝ :=
  sInf {τ : ℝ | FormalAdmissibleExponent F w12 w23 w31 τ}

theorem square_exponent (F : Type*) [Field F] : formalOmega F 1 1 1 = (9 : ℝ) / 4 := by
  sorry
```

with `dual_exponent` ($\alpha = 1/2$) and `rectangular_exponent` (the formula above) beside it. The configuration `MatrixExponent.json` permits only `propext`, `Quot.sound` and `Classical.choice`.

I looked for the usual ways a statement like this can be weaker than it reads. The `sInf` is the junk-value trap I described for the October challenge: the infimum of an empty set of reals is 0 in Lean. Here that works in the theorem's favour, because the claim is an equality with $9/4$, so the set has to be nonempty and its infimum has to be exactly $9/4$. The cost model counts every arithmetic operation, which is the paper's $\omega$. Correctness is a formal polynomial identity, which over a finite field is stronger than agreeing at every point, and is the paper's convention. I found nothing in the 136 lines that makes the statement easier than "the exponent of matrix multiplication over $F$ is $9/4$".

The solution is `OAI.LinearAlgebra.SpectralBooks.ExactExponent`, and its proof of `square_exponent` is one line that chains two theorems from the library. The two directories it draws on, `SpectralBooks/` and `MatrixExponent/`, hold 1,395 `.lean` files and 347,203 lines (counted with `find` and `wc` at commit 3344da5). `grep` finds no `sorry`, no `axiom` declaration and no `native_decide` in either; "admit" appears only in English docstrings. I did not referee 347,000 lines, and that is the point of Comparator: nobody has to.

Three limits on what that buys:

- **The model is division-free.** The Comparator statement has no division gate. The paper's theorem allows division and counts only nonscalar multiplications and divisions. The library does contain `divisionOmega_square_eq_formalOmega` (`SpectralBooks/DivisionExponent.lean`, line 82), which says division does not change the square exponent, and I read it. It is not a Comparator target, so its own definitions were not matched against a challenge file. Some press write-ups say the division case is unchecked; it is more accurate to say it is proved in the library but not certified the same way.
- **The finite-size claim is not in Lean.** The scope document says plainly: "The paper's lower bound of $\lceil n^{9/4}/2\rceil$ on the number of nonscalar multiplication or division gates at each side length $n\ge2$ remains unformalized." The border-rank corollary is not there either. The rank-floor widget above is the paper's claim, not a checked theorem.
- **Lean states more than the paper proves.** The paper proves $\frac12 \le \alpha_F \le \frac23$; Lean states $\alpha = 1/2$ and the full rectangular formula. That is not an error (the scope document lists the companion papers the Lean draws on), but it means the formal development is the more complete source for those two results, and the manuscript lags it.

### Has anyone built it?

Yes, one person so far. [Lilting.ch published a build log](https://lilting.ch/en/articles/matrix-multiplication-exponent-nine-fourths-openai) on 25 November: Lean 4.34.1 and Mathlib at `d13f23b` on an M1 Max, 592 modules (the import closure of the solution plus the challenge), 942 seconds wall clock, about 6.5 GB peak memory. Its `#print axioms` on all three theorems shows only `propext`, `Classical.choice` and `Quot.sound`. It checked that the 108 constants in the challenge match between the two environments, that changing `9 / 4` to `9 / 5` in the challenge is caught as a mismatch, and that `leanchecker --fresh` re-checks the environment in about 22 minutes. It could not run Comparator itself (its sandbox is Linux-only) or the independent `nanoda` kernel, which the release's configuration disables. Note also what that build needed: Mathlib alone. None of the release's patched third-party dependencies are in the import closure.

I have a second build running on a 28-core Linux box, from a clean clone at 3344da5, which also prints the axioms of the division theorem. Until it reports, lilting.ch's is the only independent build, and if mine fails, this section changes.

## So what is proved?

Here is how I would sort it today.

**Established.** $\omega \le 9/4$ over every field. Six human papers, one constructive, plus Lean.

**Machine-checked, not yet understood by anyone outside OpenAI.** $\omega = 9/4$ exactly, in the division-free algebraic model, over every field; $\alpha = 1/2$; the rectangular formula. The statements are short and faithful, the proofs use only standard axioms, and an outside builder has reproduced the check. If Lean's kernel and Mathlib are sound, these are theorems. That is a different epistemic position from a 111-page manuscript, and it is the reason I am not writing "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence" here: a formal proof is the extraordinary evidence. What it does not give is understanding. Nobody outside OpenAI has explained why the lower bound is true, and a theorem that overturns the field's central conjecture deserves a human-readable proof that someone has checked.

**Claimed in the manuscript only.** $\lceil n^{9/4}/2\rceil$ gates at every $n \ge 2$; the border-rank bound $n^{9/4}$; the division case as a certified statement; and everything in the companion papers that the Lean scope document leaves out.

What would change my mind fastest is a flaw in the definitions that I and the outside builder both missed, or a small explicit bilinear algorithm, at any side $n$, with fewer than $\lceil n^{9/4}\rceil$ products. The second is a finite search problem, and people with AlphaTensor-style searches running will look.

## What experts are saying so far

Not much yet, and nothing technical about the lower bound. As of 25 November I found no blog post, MathOverflow thread or arXiv note by a researcher in algebraic complexity that engages with Theorem 2.1 or 2.2, and no published comment from the authors of the October reproofs. - Clément Canonne [posted on Bluesky](https://bsky.app/profile/ccanonne.github.io/post/3n33wkxaygs26) a little after midnight UTC: "Common sense is dead, ω=2.25." - [Lilting.ch's build log](https://lilting.ch/en/articles/matrix-multiplication-exponent-nine-fourths-openai), above, is the only independent technical check I found, and it checks the Lean, not the mathematics.
- [ProofAtlas](https://www.proofatlas.ai/open-problems/famous/) still lists "Matrix-Multiplication Exponent Equals Two" at rank 031 as Open, with a 7 October note that the October release "reports partial progress". ## What it means in practice

For anyone who writes GEMM kernels: nothing. Every bound since 1990 is galactic, and a lower bound changes no running code. The algorithms on real hardware are the blocked cubic schedule in every BLAS and, rarely, a level or two of Strassen–Winograd.

On paper it changes a lot. Every algorithm stated as $O(n^\omega)$, from linear systems and determinants to triangle detection and the graph algorithms built on rectangular products, now has a known exponent instead of a moving target: $n^{9/4}$ and no lower, if the result holds. Research programmes aimed at $\omega = 2$, including the conjectures in the group-theoretic approach that would imply it, are aimed at something false. And the techniques, prices as distances and lower bounds from network compression, are the same ones family 379 uses for VP ≠ VNP and the permanent, so their fate is tied together.

My position: the October upper bound is settled. The exact value $9/4$ is formally proved in a model I accept, and I would bet on it. I would not yet cite the finite-size gate counts, and I would wait for a human reading of Theorem 2.1 before teaching the proof. If you work on matrix multiplication, the 136-line challenge file is the best hour you can spend this week.
