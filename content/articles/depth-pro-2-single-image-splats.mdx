Four posts went past on 19 and 20 November, and they read like one story.
Apple released Depth Pro 2, a family of metric depth models for photos and
video. A second account announced, as if separately, a 23.5-million-parameter
network that predicts a camera's focal length from one photo. A third said
Apple had open-sourced a project that turns a single photo into a 3D Gaussian
splat in under a second on an ordinary GPU, with a video of someone pulling a
photo off an iPhone and stepping into it. A fourth showed SplatLab, which
picks objects out of a splat, moves them, and fills in what was behind.

The story the four posts imply is: a better depth model, a focal length
estimator, and from those two a 3D scene from any photo. Two of the pieces
are what they say. The other two are older, or not what the post suggests:

- **Depth Pro 2** is new: the technical report is
  [arXiv 2611.26603](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26603), submitted 19 November,
  with code at
  [apple-aiml-research/ml-depth-pro-2](https://github.com/apple-aiml-research/ml-depth-pro-2)
  and model cards at [apple/DepthPro2](https://huggingface.co/apple/DepthPro2).
- **The focal length estimator** is part of that same release, not a separate
  project. It is [apple/DepthPro2-focal](https://huggingface.co/apple/DepthPro2-focal),
  it loads with every size of Depth Pro 2, and both posts carry the same
  12-second hero clip from the model card.
- **The single-photo splat project** is SHARP, Apple's
  [ml-sharp](https://github.com/apple-aiml-research/ml-sharp), from the paper
  "Sharp Monocular View Synthesis in Less Than a Second"
  ([arXiv 2512.10685](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.10685)), published in
  December 2025. The post that went round this week opens with "才发现", "just
  found out". Its video is Bihe Xi's Apple Vision Pro app, which uses SHARP for
  the scene. SHARP is built on the first Depth Pro, not on Depth Pro 2, and
  its "under a second" is measured on an A100.
- **SplatLab** is Eros Carrasco's research engine for editing splats of
  multi-view scans. There is no paper and no public code, and it does not
  start from one photo.

So this is not a pipeline Apple shipped. It is two Apple models, one from
this week and one eleven months old, and someone else's editor. The pairing
is still worth writing up, because the reason the two models fit together is
the most useful idea in either paper. A single
photo does not contain distance. It contains how big things look. Distance
comes from the focal length, and a wrong focal length does not shrink or
inflate the 3D scene uniformly: it stretches the scene along the camera's
axis and leaves its width alone. Depth Pro 2's small estimator exists to fill
exactly that gap, and SHARP, when a photo has no focal length in its EXIF,
logs a warning and assumes 30 mm.

I ran both on an RTX 3090: the focal estimator on 51 photos with EXIF focal
lengths and on 24 frames from a calibrated RGB-D camera, all four Depth Pro 2
sizes against that camera's depth, the speed and memory of each, and SHARP on
the photos the viewer below shows.

## What one photo can and cannot tell a network

Start with a pinhole camera. A point at $(X, Y, Z)$ in the camera's frame, $Z$
metres in front of it, lands on pixel

$$
u = c_x + f\,\frac{X}{Z}, \qquad v = c_y + f\,\frac{Y}{Z},
$$

where $f$ is the focal length in pixels and $(c_x, c_y)$ the principal point,
usually the image centre. Everything a network sees is on the left of those
equations. A doorway 0.9 m wide, 4 m away, shot with $f = 1500$ px, spans
$1500 \times 0.9 / 4 \approx 338$ pixels. The same doorway at 8 m with
$f = 3000$ px also spans 338 pixels. The pixels are identical. Nothing in the
image separates the two.

What the network can learn is how large known things look: a door, a chair,
a person, a floor tile, the texture of carpet. That is a statement about
$f/Z$, not about $Z$. Depth Pro and Depth Pro 2 are built around this. Their
networks do not output metres. They output inverse depth normalised by the
focal length, and the code turns that into depth with one line, which the
README spells out:

```python
depth = f_px / (width * inverse_depth)   # depth_pro_2/model.py, DepthPro2.infer
```

So metric depth is linear in the focal length you supply. The README says it
plainly: "a focal length 10% too long places everything 10% too far."

Now put the pixel back into 3D. Inverting the projection,

$$
Z = \frac{f}{W\,\hat{\rho}}, \qquad X = \frac{(u - c_x)\,Z}{f} = \frac{u - c_x}{W\,\hat{\rho}},
$$

with $\hat\rho$ the network's normalised inverse depth and $W$ the image
width. The focal length cancels out of $X$, and out of $Y$ the same way. The
sideways and vertical positions of every point are fixed by what the network
saw. Only the distance along the optical axis depends on $f$.

That is a stronger statement than "the scale is unknown", and it is the one
that matters for anyone using these depths. A wrong focal length does not
scale your room. It stretches it along the view. Give Depth Pro 2 a focal
length 30% too long and the room comes out 30% deeper and exactly as wide:
corners that were square are now obtuse or acute, a circular table is an
ellipse, a corridor is longer and no wider. Scale errors are easy to fix with
one known length. A stretch along one axis is not, because a known width tells
you nothing about it; you need a known depth.

### Where the focal length comes from

Most photos carry it. EXIF has a `FocalLength` tag in millimetres of the real
lens, which is useless without the sensor size, and a
`FocalLengthIn35mmFilm` tag: the focal length that would give the same field
of view on a 36 by 24 mm frame. Both Depth Pro 2's `load_rgb` and SHARP's
`io.load_rgb` read the second one and convert it to pixels through the
diagonal:

```python
f_px = f_mm * np.sqrt(width**2 + height**2) / np.sqrt(36**2 + 24**2)
```

That is exact only if the camera computed its 35 mm equivalent from the
diagonal and nobody cropped the image afterwards. A screenshot, a video
frame, a photo through a messaging app or off the web usually has no tag at
all.

What each model does then is where they differ. Depth Pro 2 runs its focal
estimator. SHARP, whose loader is otherwise identical, does this:

```python
f_35mm = img_exif.get("FocalLengthIn35mmFilm", img_exif.get("FocalLenIn35mmFilm", None))
if f_35mm is None or f_35mm < 1:
    f_35mm = img_exif.get("FocalLength", None)
    if f_35mm is None:
        LOGGER.warn(f"Did not find focallength in exif data of {path} - Setting to 30mm.")
        f_35mm = 30.0
    if f_35mm < 10.0:
        LOGGER.info("Found focal length below 10mm, assuming it's not for 35mm.")
        # This is a very crude approximation.
        f_35mm *= 8.4
```

That is `sharp/utils/io.py`, verbatim. With no 35 mm tag it falls back to the
real lens focal length and, if that is under 10 mm (a phone), multiplies by
8.4 as a guess at the crop factor. With no focal length at all, it warns and
assumes 30 mm, a wide-ish normal lens. On a phone's main camera
(about 24 to 28 mm equivalent) that is 7 to 25% too long, and the scene comes
out that much too deep. On an ultra-wide (13 mm) it is more than twice too
long. On a 70 mm portrait shot it is 57% too short. The splat still renders
correctly from the photo's own viewpoint, because a stretch along the view is
invisible from the camera that made it. It only shows when you move, or when
you measure.

## Depth Pro, the first one

[Depth Pro](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02073) (Bochkovskii et al., October
2024) gave metric depth from one image, sharp at object edges, in about 0.3
seconds on a V100. It ran at a fixed 1536 by 1536: the image was cut into
384-pixel patches at several scales, each through the same DINOv2-L "patch
encoder", with a second ViT seeing the whole image shrunk to 384 for context,
and a DPT-style decoder fusing the lot. Its focal length came from a small
head on the depth network's frozen features plus its own ViT, trained
afterwards on photos with EXIF but no depth. It traced hair, fur and
branches better than anything before it. It also squashed every image into a
square, cost two ViT-L passes over many patches, and predicted each video
frame alone, so its depth flickered.

The architecture that gets there is simpler than Depth Pro's, not more
complicated.

**One ViT, at the input's own resolution.** The tiling is gone. Depth Pro 2
starts from DINOv3 ViT-S/16, B/16, L/16 or H+/16, which were trained to give
dense features at high resolution, cuts the image into 16-pixel patches at
whatever size it is, and runs one transformer over all of them. The rotary
position code divides both axes by the longer side,

$$
y' = \frac{2\,(i + \tfrac12)}{\max(h, w)} - 1, \qquad x' = \frac{2\,(j + \tfrac12)}{\max(h, w)} - 1,
$$

so a 16:9 frame is not squeezed into a square of positions and the network
knows its aspect ratio. Why native resolution matters is in the paper's own
ablation (its Table 21): Depth Pro 2-H+ fed frames with a 360-pixel short
side scores a boundary F1 of 24.1 on DynamicReplica; at 720 pixels or native
it scores 57.7. Metric accuracy barely moves (95.0 against 95.4 δ1 on Waymo).
Resolution buys edges, not metres.

**Frames attend to each other.** The same transformer takes one image or a
clip of up to 32 frames. The first third of its blocks attend only within a
frame. After that, blocks alternate between attention within a frame and
attention across all the frames' tokens; Table 1 of the paper lists which
blocks those are (5, 7, 9 and 11 of 12 for S and B; every other block from 9
or 11 onward for L and H+). There is no frame-order encoding, so the network
is equivariant to shuffling the frames and the cross-frame blocks reuse the
pretrained single-image attention weights with no new parameters. A single
photo is a clip of one, and the cross-frame blocks then attend over one frame.
Table 20 measures what the cross-frame attention buys: dropping it entirely
raises jitter on DynamicReplica from 0.24 to 0.84 and lowers boundary F1 from
57.7 to 53.4, while metric δ1 falls only from 98.9 to 98.1.

**A DPT decoder per frame**, reading tokens from four blocks, reassembled at
1/32 to 1/4 of the input resolution, with transposed convolutions instead of
bilinear upsampling. The decoder is small: 2.7M parameters for S, 31.6M for
H+.

| model | total params | backbone | blocks / width | decoder params |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Depth Pro 2-S | 24.3M | DINOv3 ViT-S/16 | 12 / 384 | 2.7M |
| Depth Pro 2-B | 96.5M | DINOv3 ViT-B/16 | 12 / 768 | 10.9M |
| Depth Pro 2-L | 334.0M | DINOv3 ViT-L/16 | 24 / 1024 | 30.9M |
| Depth Pro 2-H+ | 872.1M | DINOv3 ViT-H+/16 | 32 / 1280 | 31.6M |

The sizes are from the paper's Table 1, which excludes the focal estimator.
The released checkpoints on Apple's CDN are 97 MB, 386 MB, 1.34 GB and
3.49 GB.

### Training: synthetic depth, real scale, a consistency loss

The paper trains on 19 datasets "with a strong focus on synthetic ground
truth". The largest is internal: 24,150,620 rendered frames of people, pets
and objects with dense metric depth and poses. The second is also internal
and real: 3,046,117 frames of handheld phone video with time-of-flight depth.
Public sets (TartanAir, Hypersim, BEDLAM, Dynamic Replica, Waymo and others)
fill the rest. Ground truth is never interpolated, because averaging depth
across an edge makes exactly the flying pixels a sharp model should avoid.
Batches are packed by tokens (73,728 per GPU), so a clip takes as many frames
as fit, up to 32. Four losses are summed:

- a trimmed L1 on the focal-normalised inverse depth, only where the ground
  truth is metric: the term that teaches scale;
- the same after normalising each map to zero median and unit deviation, on
  everything: the MiDaS-style term that teaches shape;
- a multi-scale gradient match, only on synthetic data, where edges are exact;
- the new one, multi-view consistency: lift a pixel of frame $i$ with its
  predicted depth, move it into frame $j$ with the true poses, and penalise
  the difference from what the network predicted there, skipping pixels the
  ground truth says are occluded or moving.

The ablations in Table 19 say which choices carry the result. Square crops
at native scale instead of any aspect ratio cost 46.6 points of metric δ1 on
Waymo. A frozen DINOv3 backbone costs 13.1. Public data only costs 45.2
points on ARKitScenes, so the internal phone data is where the indoor scale
comes from. Switching the consistency loss off only moves jitter from 0.36
to 0.41; the cross-frame attention does most of that work.

### Video, two ways

At inference a video longer than a clip is processed in one of two modes.
Buffered streaming cuts it into clips of $N$ frames overlapping by $K$ and
blends the overlaps linearly; the default is $N = 16$, $K = 4$. Unbuffered
streaming predicts each frame on arrival, attending to itself and the
$C - 1$ frames before it through a first-in, first-out cache of keys and
values in the cross-frame layers, default $C = 16$. Table 22 shows the two
nearly equal: for H+ on 300-frame DynamicReplica sequences, jitter 0.291
buffered and 0.300 unbuffered, boundary F1 57.4 and 57.1. A video gets one
focal length, the median of the estimator's per-frame answers over the first
window.

## A 23.5M-parameter network that guesses the lens

The second post of the four is about this part, and it is the part I would
reach for first. Depth Pro's focal head read the depth network's frozen
features. Depth Pro 2's estimator is its own network, small enough to run on
every frame of a stream.

It is a DINOv3 ViT-S/16 (21.6M parameters), fine-tuned end to end, with a
1.9M-parameter head on top: two transformer blocks of width 248 that read the
tokens of blocks 2, 5, 8 and 11, pooled, and regress the horizontal and
vertical fields of view, each squashed into 1° to 179° by a sigmoid. The
input is resized so its short side is 512 pixels, never upsampled, and
frames wider than 16:9 are centre-cropped to 16:9, which changes neither the
short-side field of view nor the focal length in pixels. Only the short-side
field of view is used, converted with

$$
f_\text{px} = \frac{s/2}{\tan(\theta_s/2)},
$$

where $s$ is the length of the short side in pixels and $\theta_s$ the field
of view across it. For a clip, the estimator answers per frame and the median
is the clip's focal length.

It was trained on about 92 million Flickr photos with valid EXIF, an L1
loss on both angles, 120,000 steps on 32 B200s, with photometric
augmentations and flips but no crops, since a crop changes the field of
view. On the
six photo benchmarks Depth Pro used, the paper's Table 6 reports the share
of photos whose focal length comes out within 25% of the truth:

| estimator | mean δ25 | rank |
|---|---|---|
| GeoCalib | 45.5% | 6.17 |
| UniDepthV2-L | 48.8% | 5.83 |
| DA3-NGL | 53.2% | 4.67 |
| AnyCalib | 54.2% | 4.17 |
| UniK3D-L | 57.8% | 3.83 |
| Depth Pro | 71.7% | 2.33 |
| **Depth Pro 2 estimator** | **88.6%** | **1.00** |

It ranks first on all six datasets. Within 50%, it is above 97% on four of
them and at least 94% on all six.

Table 23 explains why it is a separate network. Apple first tried the cheap
option, a head on each depth model's frozen encoder, which would add almost
nothing to the cost. On H+ that works: a 1.9M head on frozen H+ features
reaches 86.5%. On S it reaches 74.5%, and an eight-times-larger head only
78.8%. A small network fine-tuned for the one job beats them all at 88.6%,
gives every size of Depth Pro 2 the same focal length for the same photo,
and does not need retraining when the depth models change. For a 1080p frame
it adds about 8% to the compute of Depth Pro 2-S and 0.5% to H+, and the
paper times it at 118 frames per second at 1080p on a B200.

It has limits it cannot train away. It predicts the field of view a photographer
would have had for a picture that looks like this one, which is a prior over
cameras, not a measurement. A deliberate crop of a wide shot looks like a
telephoto shot, and the estimator will say so. And it assumes a pinhole: a
fisheye or an equirectangular panorama has no single focal length to find.

### The estimator on 51 photos and 24 calibrated frames

I ran the estimator on two kinds of input with a known answer. The first is
51 CC0 photos from Wikimedia Commons with a `FocalLengthIn35mmFilm` tag,
chosen to spread from 13 mm (phone ultra-wides) to 255 mm (a telephoto
detail of a rose window), from dozens of camera models, at their native aspect ratio
and without "crop" in the title, downloaded as 1920-pixel thumbnails. The
truth is the EXIF tag turned into pixels by the same diagonal formula the
models use, which is itself only as good as the camera's rounding. The
second is 24 frames from the TUM RGB-D benchmark, whose Kinect cameras were
calibrated by the dataset's authors: ten from a freiburg3 sequence, whose
images are already undistorted, and fourteen from freiburg1 and freiburg2
sequences, whose images carry the lens's radial distortion.

| set | photos or frames | median error | within 10% | within 25% |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| all 51 photos | 51 | 7.2% | 59% | 80% |
| photos at 20 to 35 mm | 14 | 1.4% | | |
| photos at 60 mm or under | 30 | 3.9% | | 87% |
| SHARP's 30 mm guess on the same 51 | 51 | 60.5% | | 27% |

The estimator is very good where most photos are taken. On the fourteen
photos between 20 and 35 mm equivalent, the ordinary phone and kit-lens
range, its median error is 1.4%, and all four of the viewer's photos are
within about 1%. At the ends it regresses towards the middle. On the eight
ultra-wide photos (13 and 16 mm) its median error is +19%: it thinks they
are narrower than they are, and one Samsung ultra-wide comes out 79% too
long. On the 19 long-lens photos (70 mm and up) it is 7% short on the median,
with a 180 mm shot at −59%. That is what a regression trained on Flickr
should do: the further a photo is from the bulk of photos, the more the
answer is pulled towards the bulk. The paper's 88.6% within 25% is on its
benchmark mix; on my deliberately wide spread it is 80%, and 87% if I stop
at 60 mm.

SHARP's fallback is the comparison that matters for anyone feeding it photos
without metadata. A fixed 30 mm is within 25% on only 27% of these photos,
with a median error of 60.5%. It is right for a phone's main camera and
wrong for everything else.

The calibrated frames are less flattering. On the ten undistorted
freiburg3 frames the median of the per-frame estimates is 536.3 pixels
against a calibrated $f_y$ of 539.2, 0.5% off, and running the ten frames as
one clip gives 538.8, 0.1% off. But the per-frame answers jump between two
values: six frames say 516 to 540 and four say 619 to 652, up to 21% long,
on frames from the same camera in the same office. The median hides that,
which is exactly why the paper takes a median per clip. On the distorted
freiburg1 and freiburg2 frames the estimator is consistently about a quarter
too long (+23.9% to +26.1% per sequence), all but one frame landing between 637
and 662 pixels against a calibrated 516 to 521. I can only guess why. Those images
have uncorrected radial distortion, which a model trained on corrected phone
photos has rarely seen, and 640 by 480 VGA frames are smaller than the 512-pixel
short side it was trained at. Either way, it is a reminder that "metric
depth without EXIF" means "metric if your camera looks like the cameras on
Flickr".

## How good the metres are, in the paper

The paper evaluates zero-shot, on datasets kept out of training for most
methods, and reports every metric twice: with the true focal length, and with
the focal length each method estimates for itself. That second column is the
one that matches using a model on a photo off the internet, and it is where
the estimator shows up.

On single images (Table 2, δ1: the share of pixels within a factor of 1.25 of
the truth), Depth Pro 2-H+ has the best average rank of the eleven methods
both ways, 2.4 with true focal lengths and 2.7 with its own. A few columns
are worth reading closely:

| dataset | what it is | Depth Pro 2-H+, true f | its own f | Depth Pro, own f |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| iBims | indoor rooms, laser scan | 94.4 | 86.6 | 82.9 |
| ETH3D | indoor and outdoor, laser scan | 97.8 | 91.8 | 41.0 |
| KITTI | driving, LiDAR | 98.2 | 87.9 | 82.7 |
| HAMMER | indoor objects, includes specular | 89.4 | 86.3 | 55.2 |
| Booster | transparent and specular surfaces | 65.7 | 43.1 | 59.0 |
| Sintel | synthetic film | 60.2 | 43.4 | 30.1 |

With the true focal length the model is good almost
everywhere; swapping in its own estimate costs up to 23 points (and on
Middlebury and Spring it does better than the true focal length), which is
the focal estimator's error propagating through the depth exactly as the
equation says it must. And Booster, which is a benchmark built of
glass, mirrors and shiny objects, is where it is weakest: 65.7 with the true
focal length, 43.1 with its own, and the older Depth Pro is ahead there with
its own estimate. Glass is the hardest case for any monocular depth model,
because the network has to decide whether to report the glass or what is
behind it, and the ground truth decides one way.

On video clips (Table 3, the four datasets that have video), H+ ranks first
on average with an average rank of 1.0 using true focal lengths and 1.2 with
its own. On DyDToF it scores 94.8 δ1 with its own focal length against 86.2
with the true one. The paper does not comment on it. A median over 50
frames is at least a steadier estimate than any single frame's.

The sharpness numbers (Table 4, boundary F1) put H+ first on average among
22 methods on single images, rank 1.6, with InfiniDepth next at 3.0 and Depth
Pro at 5.4. The jitter numbers (Table 5) put it first among 25, 1.39 and 0.86
on Sintel and Spring (scaled by 100) against Depth Pro's 11.26 and 8.99, an
eight-to-tenfold steadier depth.

On DIODE, the paper notes, most of Depth Pro 2's absolute error comes from
sky seen through bare branches: the laser marked the branch, the model
predicted sky, which it returns as infinite depth. Anything downstream has
to clip it.

### Against Depth Anything, MoGe and VGGT

**Depth Anything.** Version 2 predicts relative depth: good shape, no
metres. Version 3 (the tables' DA3-NGL) is a multi-view geometry model that
also gives metric depth; the site has
[traced where its metres come from in a ROS 2 wrapper](/articles/depth-anything-3-ros2),
a fixed 300-pixel focal normalisation, the same idea as Depth Pro's with the
focal length handled outside the model. In Depth Pro 2's tables DA3-NGL is
strong with true intrinsics (96.1 on iBims) and weak with its own (50.6 on
KITTI), near the bottom on boundary sharpness (average rank 18.2 of 22), and at 1.0 frame per second at
1080p, out of memory at 4K, on a B200. [InfiniSplat 2](/articles/infinisplat-2)
builds splats on a frozen DA3 and shows what those depths do in a splat.

**MoGe.** MoGe-2 and MoGe-3 predict an affine-invariant point map plus a
metric scale and recover the camera from the point map. They are sharp
(MoGe-3-G is fourth on boundary F1, just ahead of Depth Pro), but their
own-focal numbers fall away on unusual cameras: MoGe-2-L from 91.5 to 42.7
δ1 on KITTI. [MetricTrail](/articles/metrictrail) uses MoGe-2's metric depth
inside visual odometry, exactly where that matters.

**VGGT.** VGGT, π³, MapAnything and VGGT-Ω solve for cameras and geometry of
an image set in one pass. They are steady on video (VGGT-Ω-1B jitter 2.36
and 0.99) and blurry at edges because they run at reduced resolution: a
boundary F1 of 3.7 on iBims against Depth Pro 2-H+'s 20.5, and 68.0 GiB for
a 32-frame 1080p clip on VGGT-1B. For a multi-image capture they are still
the right tool, since they give you the cameras. For one photo, or depth on
frames whose poses you already have, Depth Pro 2 is sharper, steadier and
much cheaper.

All of these numbers are the paper's, run by Apple with each method's
official code. That is the usual arrangement, and the usual reason to check
a few yourself, which is the next section.

## How good the metres are, on my frames

The paper deliberately leaves out datasets captured with a Kinect, "due to
noisy, biased, and incomplete ground truth". That is fair, and it is also
what I could download in a few megabytes, so read what follows with that
in mind: the ground truth is a structured-light sensor good to a few
centimetres at one or two metres and worse beyond, with holes at edges and
on dark or shiny surfaces. I scored only pixels where the sensor reported
between 0.1 and 10 m. The 24 TUM frames above are offices and desks between
about half a metre and six metres away.

Each size of Depth Pro 2 ran on each frame alone, at its native 640 by 480,
first with the calibrated focal length and then with the estimator's (the
second needs no second pass, since depth is linear in $f$). I report the
median ratio of predicted to true depth (the scale), AbsRel and δ1 as the
paper does, and AbsRel after dividing out each frame's median ratio (the
shape, with scale removed).

| model | median ratio | AbsRel | δ1 | AbsRel, scale removed | with its own focal: ratio, AbsRel |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Depth Pro 2-S | 0.922 | 0.141 | 80.5 | 0.091 | 1.128, 0.202 |
| Depth Pro 2-B | 0.960 | 0.112 | 91.3 | 0.066 | 1.126, 0.176 |
| Depth Pro 2-L | 0.979 | 0.082 | 96.0 | 0.062 | 1.178, 0.188 |
| Depth Pro 2-H+ | 0.945 | 0.081 | 97.6 | 0.054 | 1.158, 0.169 |

With the right focal length, H+ gets 97.6% of pixels within 25% of the
sensor and an average relative error of 8.1%, of which 5.4 points are
shape and the rest a scale that reads 5.5% short on the median frame. The
sizes line up the way the paper says: S is clearly worse (14.1%), L and H+
are close. With the estimator's focal length every size reads 13 to 18%
long, and that is the estimator's quarter-too-long answer on the distorted
freiburg1 and freiburg2 frames passing straight through into depth. On the
ten undistorted freiburg3 frames alone, H+ with its own focal length has a
median ratio of 0.981.

The scale is not constant with distance. Binning every scored pixel of H+
by its true depth:

| true depth | pixels | median ratio, true focal | AbsRel | median ratio, own focal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| under 1 m | 1,180,154 | 0.936 | 0.088 | 1.149 |
| 1 to 2 m | 3,291,275 | 0.949 | 0.076 | 1.157 |
| 2 to 3 m | 836,796 | 0.991 | 0.077 | 1.218 |
| 3 to 4 m | 147,497 | 1.030 | 0.095 | 1.260 |
| 4 to 10 m | 251,676 | 1.072 | 0.122 | 1.293 |

Near things come out about 6% too close, far things 7% too far: the
predicted depth range is stretched compared with the sensor's, and the
relative error grows from 7.6% at one to two metres to 12.2% beyond four.
In absolute terms that is the difference between centimetres and decimetres:
6% of 80 cm is 5 cm, 7% of 6 m is 42 cm. Some of the far error is surely the
Kinect's, whose depth noise grows with the square of distance. Some of it is
not, because the trend is smooth and monotone and present in every size
(L goes from 0.956 to 1.168). If you need a monocular depth to be metric at
room scale, align it to something you trust, and expect the alignment to
depend on distance.

| Depth Pro 2-L | 50.2 ms | 123.7 ms | 301.5 ms | 7.44 GiB | 5.0 fps |

keeps up with 25 fps footage at 720p in 16-frame clips on this card; H+ is
an offline tool at 2.8 fps.

## From one photo to Gaussians: SHARP

A depth map is already most of a 3D scene: put every pixel at its depth along
its ray and you have a point cloud that looks exactly like the photo from
where it was taken. Move, and points break up into dots, and black slits open
behind every edge, because nothing exists behind anything.

A Gaussian splat fixes the first problem and gives you a place to fix the
second. Each point becomes a 3D Gaussian with a position, a scale along
three axes, an orientation, an opacity and a colour, and a renderer sorts
them by depth and blends them, each drawn as the ellipse it projects to.
Sized right, neighbours overlap and the surface stays closed when you move.
The site's earlier pieces cover the representation and its formats in detail:
[how Blender 5.3 imports and renders splats](/articles/blender-5-3-gaussian-splats),
[a three.js renderer and its compressed format](/articles/gaussian-splat-lite),
[PlayCanvas's SOG format](/articles/sog-splat-format), and
[how people capture scenes for them from a 360 camera](/articles/3dgs-capture-pipelines).
All of those start from many photos and an optimiser that fits the Gaussians
over thousands of steps. SHARP starts from one photo and predicts the
Gaussians in a single forward pass.

<Figure
  src="/articles/depth-pro-2-single-image-splats/fig-sharp-overview.png"
  alt="SHARP architecture. An input image of a turquoise truck goes into an image encoder; features go to a depth decoder that outputs two layered depth maps, then a depth adjustment module, then a Gaussian initializer that makes base Gaussians. A Gaussian decoder reads the features, the image and the adjusted depth and outputs Gaussian refinements; a Gaussian composer combines base Gaussians and refinements into output Gaussians, which a renderer turns into novel views. Green boxes are fine-tuned, orange from scratch, blue differentiable and not learned."
  caption="SHARP: a Depth Pro encoder and a two-layer depth decoder, base Gaussians placed from that depth, and a decoder that predicts corrections to every attribute. Depth adjustment is used in training only (SHARP paper, Figure 3)."
/>

The pieces, as the paper and the code describe them:

1. **A Depth Pro encoder and decoder.** SHARP resizes every input to 1536 by
   1536, exactly as Depth Pro does, and runs Depth Pro's two ViT-L encoders
   (326M parameters each). The decoder's last convolution is duplicated, so
   it predicts two depth maps instead of one: the first for the visible
   surface, the second for whatever is behind it at depth edges. Like Depth
   Pro 2, the network's depth output is turned into metres by the focal
   length: the code passes `disparity_factor = f_px / width` and divides by
   the predicted disparity.
2. **Base Gaussians from that depth.** Both depth maps are min-pooled by 2,
   to 768 by 768, and a Gaussian is placed at every cell of each, at its
   depth, coloured with the average of its 2 by 2 pixels, scaled in
   proportion to its depth (so it is about a pixel wide), with opacity 0.5
   and no rotation. That is $2 \times 768 \times 768 = 1{,}179{,}648$
   Gaussians for every photo, whatever its size.
3. **A Gaussian decoder**, 7.8M parameters trained from scratch, reads the
   features, the image and the depth and predicts a small correction to
   every attribute of every Gaussian, about 16.5 million numbers per image.
4. **Unprojection.** The Gaussians are predicted in a normalised space that
   ignores the camera and mapped to metres with the intrinsics at the end.

The whole network has 702M parameters, 340M of them trained. The
depth-adjustment module in the figure rescales the predicted depth towards
the ground truth during training only, so a depth guess of the right shape
and wrong scale does not poison the view-synthesis loss; at inference it is
the identity, and the scale comes from the focal length. Training renders the
Gaussians into the input view and a second view and compares them with
photos (L1, a perceptual loss with Gram matrices that does most of the work
on what gets painted into holes, an alpha loss, a depth loss), first on
synthetic scenes, then on real single photos using SHARP's own renders as
pseudo second views.

The results on the paper's Table 1 are strong for what it measures: views
near the input, scored with DISTS and LPIPS on six datasets none of which
were used in training. SHARP is best on every column. On ScanNet++ it scores
0.071 DISTS and 0.154 LPIPS against Gen3C's 0.090 and 0.227, and Gen3C, a
video diffusion model, takes about 830 seconds per scene on an A100 against
SHARP's 0.91 (Table 6). The paper's own claim is a 25 to 34% LPIPS reduction
and 21 to 43% DISTS reduction over the best prior model.

### Why it only holds near the photo

The paper is direct about its scope. Its goal is "natural posture shifts in
AR/VR headsets", not "walking around" in the photograph, and Appendix D.6
measures quality against how far the camera moves: SHARP is best below half a
metre of baseline, and still best or second best up to 3 metres on most
datasets, while Gen3C, a diffusion method that invents content for far-away
views, overtakes it only on ETH3D beyond 3 metres and on ScanNet++ beyond
half a metre. Its failure cases
(Appendix D.7) are all depth failures: a macro photo of a bee whose shallow
depth of field puts the bee behind the flowers, a starry night sky
reconstructed as a curved surface, and a reflection in water read as a
mountain. "These failures are root caused by the depth model," the authors
write.

The reason it degrades with distance is visible in its structure. There are
exactly two Gaussians per 2 by 2 pixel cell, and every one was placed on a
ray of the input camera. The first layer is the surface the photo shows. The
second layer is the only thing that can be behind it, and only where the
network chose to put it there. Move the camera a little and the first layer
covers almost everything, the second can fill the thin slits that open at
edges, and the perceptual loss has trained both to look plausible. Move it a
lot and you see past both, to where nothing was ever placed, and you see the
first layer's Gaussians edge-on, as streaks, because each was shaped to look
right from one direction.

### SHARP on a 3090, and what its two layers actually do

I ran SHARP's released checkpoint (702.3M parameters, as the paper says) on
six CC0 photos, the four in the viewer plus a hospital corridor and a
garden, and on the 24 TUM frames.

**Speed.** One forward pass at 1536 by 1536 took a median 1,248 ms in
float32 on the 3090, with 10.99 GiB reserved at peak. Under float16 or
bfloat16 autocast it took 493 ms, which beats the paper's 0.91 s on an A100;
I did not check whether the half-precision splats differ from the
full-precision ones, so take that as a speed, not a recommendation. Turning
the network's output into metric Gaussians took another 3.7 s, almost all of
it the CPU decomposing 1,179,648 covariance matrices with a float64 SVD in
the released code. "Less than a second" is the network.

**The focal sweep.** For each photo I ran SHARP three times: with the EXIF
focal length, with Depth Pro 2's estimate, and with its own 30 mm fallback.
The result is the derivation from earlier, to three decimals. On every
photo the median depth of the splat's front layer scaled exactly with the
focal length (the hall with 30 mm: focal ratio 1.111, depth ratio 1.111),
and the 5th-to-95th-percentile width of the splat did not change at all
(ratio 1.000 on all six). A SHARP splat of a photo with no EXIF is 7 to 15%
too deep on these six photos, and correctly wide.

**How metric the splat is.** On the TUM frames, given the calibrated focal
length, the depth of SHARP's front-layer Gaussians lands with a median ratio
of 1.002 to the Kinect and an AbsRel of 0.076, 0.051 with the scale
removed. That is as good as Depth Pro 2-H+ on the same frames (0.081 and
0.054), which surprised me. SHARP's depth is Depth Pro's, fine-tuned
end-to-end for view synthesis, and on indoor scenes at these distances that
fine-tuning has not hurt it. With the 30 mm default the ratio is 1.071 (a
Kinect is close to a 30 mm equivalent lens, by luck); with Depth Pro 2's
estimate it is 1.210, the estimator's error on the distorted frames again.

**The second layer.** I expected the second layer to sit behind the first
at depth edges and nowhere else. It does not look like that in the output.
Across the six photos, the second layer's Gaussians are as opaque as the
first's (mean opacity 0.88 to 0.97 for both), and for nine in ten of the
visible ones the gap between the two layers' depths is under 1% (the 90th
percentile is 0.3 to 0.8% depending on the photo); between 48% and 81% of
them sit a hair behind the first layer rather than in front. In these
photos the second layer is mostly a second coat on the same surface, with
the real separation, the part the step-through draws, confined to a small
share of pixels along depth edges. The paper says the second layer "may represent
occluded regions and view-dependent effects"; on these photos its depths
say it mostly stays on the visible surface, which fits the second reading
better than the first.

The splats in the viewer are not SHARP's full output, so here is what was
cut. To fit each scene in about 8 MB, every one of SHARP's 589,824 cells (768
by 768) keeps its more opaque Gaussian, and the rest of the budget goes to
the other layer in order of opacity. That keeps 589,824 to 668,218 of the
1,179,648 Gaussians per photo: between 23,207 and 78,394 second Gaussians in
the hall, cabin and facade, and none in the courtyard, whose one-per-cell
file alone came to 8.0 MB. Positions are 24-bit, good to a tenth of a
millimetre. Because every cell keeps a Gaussian, the photo's own viewpoint is
closed, and the black at the edges of the swing is SHARP's rather than mine:
nothing exists past the photo's frame, and where the second layer stays on
the first surface there is nothing behind the pillar either. (My first
export kept Gaussians by opacity alone and left 37 to 44% of cells empty;
its holes were bigger.)

## SplatLab: what people want to do with a splat next

The fourth post answers the question a splat raises next: now what? A
splat is a cloud of coloured blobs with no objects in it. You cannot select
the chair or move the table, and if you could, there would be nothing behind
it.

SplatLab is Eros Carrasco's attempt at that. What is public is a LinkedIn
post, a project page with two live scenes, and a 40-second video. The post
lists four things the engine does: it "finds the objects in a scan and
segments them", lets you "move them, take them out, or reuse them in another
scan", "fills in what the scanner never saw, like the gaps left after moving
objects", and lets you "relight the full environment and even add new
lights". He showed it at Adobe MAX in Miami on the ASUS ProArt stand, trained
on a pair of ASUS Ascent GX10 machines. The Japanese post that spread it
adds measuring walls and objects; that is not in his text, and I did not see
it in the video, so I would treat it as unverified.

the floor behind a table was at least partly seen from other angles. On a
single-photo splat the same editing hits the wall the step-through shows:
take the table out of a SHARP scene and behind it is the second layer, a
thin guess, and behind that nothing.

## What this means for reality capture

My day job includes turning 360° and fisheye site walks into metric,
gravity-aligned splats, meshes and point clouds, and from those into BIM. So
the question I care about is narrow: where could a metric depth from one
frame, or a splat from one photo, sit in a pipeline whose output someone
will measure?

**Not as the geometry.** A capture pipeline gets its metres from many views:
structure from motion over hundreds of frames, scale from an IMU or a known
baseline, then dense matching or splat optimisation that every frame
constrains. Monocular depth gets its metres from a learned sense of how big
things look. On my frames that sense was 6% short at a metre and 7% long
beyond four, which at five metres is about 35 cm. A wall in a BIM model has
to be within a centimetre or two. The
[capture pipelines piece](/articles/3dgs-capture-pipelines) and the
[latest reconstruction roundup](/articles/3d-reconstruction-roundup-5) are
about the tools that get there; one photo replaces none of them.

**As a prior, yes.** Construction sites are made of the surfaces multi-view
matching cannot match: bare plaster, painted drywall, concrete floors. A
monocular depth aligned per frame to the sparse structure-from-motion points
fills those walls with a plausible surface instead of noise, and Depth Pro
2's sharp edges keep a door frame from smearing into the wall. Its depth
edges and its infinite sky are also a cheap second opinion for masking out
the operator, the tripod and the sky, and a per-frame depth at walking pace
is enough to flag a room that was never seen from close enough, before the
expensive reconstruction runs.

**In a 360 pipeline the focal length comes for free.** I did not expect
this when I started reading. Depth Pro 2 is a pinhole model, so a panorama
goes in as six cube faces, and a cube face has a 90° field of view by
construction:

$$
f = \frac{W/2}{\tan(45^\circ)} = \frac{W}{2}.
$$

No EXIF, no estimator, no 30 mm guess: the reprojection gives the focal
length exactly, and with it the depth axis. What it does not give is
agreement between faces, each predicted alone. The multi-frame mode could
let the six faces attend to each other in one pass, but six views from one
centre are not what it was trained on, so whether that helps the seams is
something to measure, not assume.

**Where it fails.** Absolute error grows with distance, so a facade is worse
than a room. Glass is the weakest case in the paper's own tables (Booster,
43.1 δ1 with its own focal length), and the courtyard in the viewer shows a
glass wall whose reflection became a picture painted on it. Sky is infinite
in Depth Pro 2 and has to be clipped; in SHARP it is a far-away sheet of
Gaussians. A white wall's distance comes only from context, the floor line
and the doors around it, which is right in an ordinary room and fragile in a
bare site. And the estimator is a prior learned from Flickr, so
machine-vision cameras, crops and distorted lenses are outside it, as the
freiburg1 frames showed.

**The licence decides the rest.** Depth Pro 2's weights are under Apple's
Machine Learning Research Model licence, "for research use only:
non-commercial scientific research and academic development", and SHARP's
under the same family of terms. Neither can go into a product that measures
buildings for paying customers. The ideas transfer (focal-normalised output,
a separate focal estimator, native resolution, frames attending to each
other); the weights do not.

## What I think

Depth Pro 2 is the best monocular depth model I can name for anyone who
needs metres, sharp edges and steady video from one camera, and it is a
cleaner design than its predecessor: one ViT at the input's own resolution,
a few blocks that let frames see each other, and a loss that asks the
predictions to agree after warping. None of those ideas is new on its own.
Putting them together and measuring the trade-off they break is the
contribution, and the paper's ablations are unusually frank about which
parts carry it (native resolution and aspect ratio for edges, internal phone
data for indoor scale, cross-frame attention for steadiness).

The focal estimator is the part I would take even if I took nothing else. It
is small, it is honest about what it is (a prior over cameras), and it closes
the one gap every focal-normalised depth model has. It should have been its
own release, and in effect it is.

SHARP is a lovely piece of engineering for exactly what it says, a photo you
can lean into, and nothing more. A splat from one photo is a relief, not a
scan: two layers on the input camera's rays, the second one a learned guess,
metric only as far as the focal length it was handed and the Depth Pro guess
it was built on. Use it for what the Vision Pro clip shows. Do not measure
anything in it.

For reality capture, Depth Pro 2 is a better prior than the ones I have
used, and its research-only licence keeps it out of anything that ships. The
idea I am keeping from this week is the arithmetic in the second section:
monocular depth gets the sideways geometry right without any focal length,
and the depth axis right only with one. Any pipeline that uses these depths
should carry the focal length as a first-class input, with its source
(EXIF, estimator, cube-face geometry) recorded next to it, because that one
number decides whether the room is the right depth.

## What I ran and what I read

Ran, on one RTX 3090 (PyTorch 2.8, CUDA 12.8 wheels), from
`ml-depth-pro-2` at commit `aa389cb` and `ml-sharp` at `aed6527`, with the
weights from Apple's CDN: the focal estimator on 51 Commons photos and 24 TUM
RGB-D frames; all four Depth Pro 2 sizes on the TUM frames against Kinect
depth; the timings; Depth Pro 2-H+ on the six scene photos for the depth maps
and the widget's grids; SHARP on the same photos and frames, with the focal
