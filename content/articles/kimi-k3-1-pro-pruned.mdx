On 5 November, three days after Moonshot put the [Kimi K3.1 Pro](/articles/kimi-k3-1-pro) weights on Hugging Face, a
repository called `vllm-project/Kimi-K3.1-Pro-pruned75` appeared beside it. A post that went round the same evening
summed it up: "Original: 896 experts per MoE layer. Removed: 672 experts (75%). Remaining: 224 experts (25%)",
2.8 trillion parameters down to "~700–750B", "476 GB". Then the question everyone asks of a pruned model: "Anyone want to
try it out?"

I did not run it. It still needs four datacenter GPUs. What I could do from a sandbox with no GPU was read both
checkpoints' safetensors headers by HTTP range, stream every router matrix from both repos (92 layers, about 1.5 GB in all,
never stored), and check three things the card claims: what was cut, how the survivors were chosen, and what it costs
to serve. Then I built a small synthetic MoE in numpy to see when the card's choice of ranking can work at all.

Every claim below is labelled **measured** (I computed it from the files), **reported** (a card or post says it) or
**reasoned** (my arithmetic or inference on top of the measured numbers).

## What the card says it is

The model card is short and unusually candid. In its own words (reported):

- "A calibration-free, expert-pruned checkpoint" that "removes 75% of the routed experts in every MoE layer".
- "Intended for vLLM serving and evaluation smoke tests on fewer GPUs, not for quality-sensitive use. Expect a large
  quality regression relative to the source model."
- Ranking: the "signed sum of each expert's router row, `sum(router.weight[i])`, computed independently per layer".
- Removed: the expert tensors, the matching rows of `gate.weight` and entries of `gate.e_score_correction_bias`.
  Experts are renumbered and `num_experts` set to 224.
- Unchanged: attention, the shared experts, the dense first layer, the vision tower, and the original MXFP4 quantization
  of the routed experts. "Calibration data: none."
- Produced with the `MagnitudeExpertPruner` converter in
  [compressed-tensors](https://github.com/vllm-project/compressed-tensors), "with its default L1 scoring
  (`weight.abs().sum(-1)`) replaced by a signed sum. On this model the signed ranking scored much higher (GSM8K 43.7%
  vs. 14.4% for L1)."

So this is not a REAP checkpoint and does not pretend to be. The sibling it was modelled on,
`vllm-project/Kimi-K3-pruned75`, says why it exists: vLLM's CI needs a K3-shaped checkpoint that fits on fewer GPUs
"while accurate calibration-based pruning, such as REAP support in llm-compressor, is under development" (reported).
That framing matters for everything below. It is a test fixture with real weights, published in the open.

I checked the converter's default from the published wheel rather than the card: `compressed-tensors` 0.20.1 on PyPI,
`entrypoints/convert/converters/magnitude_expert_pruner.py`, computes `scores = weight.abs().sum(dim=-1)` over each
router row and keeps the `max(1, num_experts - round(sparsity * num_experts))` largest (measured, read from the
source). At 896 experts and a sparsity of 0.75, that is 224.

## The diff, from the headers

Both repositories ship 96 shards. I read the first eight bytes of each shard for the header length, then the JSON
header itself, 192 small range requests in all (measured):

| | Kimi K3.1 Pro | pruned75 |
|---|---:|---:|
| tensors in the headers | 497,340 | 126,396 |
| MoE layers | 92 | 92 |
| routed experts per MoE layer | 896 | 224 |
| `config.json` fields that differ | | 1 (`num_experts`) |
| parameters (Hugging Face count) | 2,780,110,363,392 | 737,611,524,480 |
| routed-expert parameters | 2,722,740,830,208 | 680,685,207,552 |
| everything else | 57,369,533,184 | 56,926,316,928 |
| tensor payload | 1,561,217,377,280 B | 475,488,771,584 B |

Each expert is three matrices, `w1` and `w3` of 3,072 by 3,584 and `w2` of 3,584 by 3,072, so 33,030,144 parameters.
K3's experts work in a 3,584-wide latent space (`routed_expert_hidden_size`) behind a shared up and down projection
from the 7,168-wide residual, the "Stable LatentMoE" of the [K3 article](/articles/kimi-k3). They are stored as
MXFP4: a `weight_packed` U8 tensor holding two 4-bit values per byte and a `weight_scale` U8 tensor with one exponent
per 32 values, 17,547,264 bytes an expert (measured). Multiply out: 33,030,144 times 896 times 92 is exactly the
2,722,740,830,208 routed parameters the Hugging Face API reports for the source, and the same times 224 is exactly
the pruned figure. The API counts the packed tensors in logical 4-bit values, not bytes, which is why its "U8" line is
almost twice the bytes on disk (measured).

The rest of the diff is the router. The 672 dropped rows of `gate.weight` per layer, 7,168 BF16 values each, are
443,154,432 parameters across 92 layers, and the 672 dropped bias entries are the 61,824 missing F32 values. Nothing
else changed: the BF16 attention, shared experts, latent projections, norms, embedding, head and vision tower are
the same tensors at the same shapes (measured, by name and shape; I did not hash their bytes).

Both of the post's numbers hold. 737.6B is inside "~700–750B", and 475.5 GB is its "476 GB". The card's "~443 GB" is
the same payload in binary units: 475,488,771,584 bytes is 442.83 GiB. The source's "~1.5 TB" is 1,561.2 GB, or
1,454.0 GiB (measured, reasoned on the unit).

One more ratio, because it decides what the next compression step should be. In the source, routed experts are 92.6%
of the bytes. In the pruned checkpoint they are 76.1%. The non-expert part, 114.8 GB of BF16 and F32 in the source, was 7.4% of
the bytes; at 113.9 GB it is now 23.9% (reasoned from the measured bytes). Cutting experts further now gains less than quantizing what was left alone.

## Checking which experts survived

A config saying 224 does not tell you which 224. The router does. Each row of `gate.weight` is one expert's
fingerprint, a 7,168-vector the token's hidden state is dotted with. If the pruned router's rows are copies of
source rows, matching them recovers the selection exactly. This is the method from the
[Comet 60B teardown](/articles/comet-60b), where it took cosine matching because the rows had been refit. Here a byte
hash is enough.

For each of the 92 MoE layers I range-requested the source `gate.weight` (896 by 7,168 BF16, 12.8 MB) and
`e_score_correction_bias`, the same two tensors from the pruned repo, hashed every row, matched them, and recomputed
both rankings in float64 (measured):

- **All 20,608 pruned rows match a source row byte for byte.** No row was modified or refit.
- **In every layer, the kept set is exactly the top 224 by signed row sum.** Not most layers: all 92.
- **The order is preserved.** Kept experts are renumbered in ascending source order, so expert 0 of the pruned model is
  the lowest-numbered survivor.
- **The balance bias is carried with its expert.** The pruned bias vector equals the source bias at the kept indices.

The margin at the cut is thin. Signed sums have a standard deviation of about 3.85 within a layer, and the median gap
between the 224th and 225th expert is 0.0086 (measured). That matters for reproduction: a different summation order
in BF16 could, in principle, swap a boundary pair. In float64 my selection and the shipped one agree in every layer.

<RouterGrid />

The interesting number is the comparison. Had the converter run with its default L1 score, it would have kept a
mostly different set: the two scores agree on a mean of 68.6 experts out of 224 per layer, with a range of 51 to 90
(measured). Two random picks of 224 from 896 share 56 on average. The two calibration-free rankings are only slightly
better than independent of each other, and the card reports 43.7% against 14.4% on GSM8K between them. Two
cheap proxies that disagree this much cannot both be good estimates of the same thing.

I also checked the one usage-derived signal the checkpoint carries. K3 routes with `topk_method: noaux_tc`, the
auxiliary-loss-free scheme in which each expert has a bias added to its score for selection only. During training the
bias is nudged down when an expert is overloaded and up when it is idle. If the signed row sum tracked how often an
expert is picked, it should correlate with that bias. It does not: the Spearman correlation between the two averages
0.018 across the 92 layers, and stays within ±0.2 in every layer. The 224 lowest-bias experts share 52.6 of 224 with
the signed-sum set on average, which is chance (measured). The biases are not all zero; they have a standard deviation
of 0.054 and run from −0.76 to 0.15. They are simply unrelated to the ranking.

What the checkpoint cannot tell me is how often each expert is used on real text. That needs forward passes through
a 2.8T model, which is the calibration step the card deliberately skipped.

## What did not get smaller

Pruning removes experts from the pool, not from the token's path. The config still says `num_experts_per_token: 16`,
so each token in each MoE layer still runs 16 routed experts plus the two shared ones. Counting what one text token
touches, 16 times 92 experts at 33,030,144 parameters each is 48.62B, plus every non-routed tensor except the
embedding table (one row is read) and the vision tower. That gives 104.37B active for the source, which matches
Moonshot's "104B", and 103.92B for the pruned model (reasoned from measured counts). The only active parameters that
went away are the 672 router rows per layer the scores are no longer computed against.

So per-token compute is unchanged, and so is the per-token weight traffic of a single-stream decode. What changes is
how many distinct experts a batch can touch. With 64 tokens each picking 16 of 896, a uniform router would hit about
613 experts per layer per step; picking from 224, every one of them, 224 (reasoned, from 1 − (1 − 16/E)^64). A
batched decode reads fewer distinct expert weights per step, which helps, but the model is not "four times faster".
It is four times smaller in the one place that was large.

## Memory and GPU count

Weight bytes set the floor for how many GPUs you need. The source, as shipped, is 1,561.2 GB, which is more than one
8-GPU node of anything but B300. The pruned checkpoint is 475.5 GB. The planner below takes the measured parameter
counts and re-prices them at other precisions, then finds the smallest power of two GPUs whose share fits in 90% of
each GPU's memory. Powers of two, because tensor parallelism has to divide both 224 experts and 96 attention heads.

As shipped, that gives (reasoned, weights only):

| GPU | Kimi K3.1 Pro | pruned75 |
|---|---:|---:|
| H100 80 GB | 32 (4 nodes) | 8 |
| H200 141 GB | 16 (2 nodes) | 4 |
| B200 180 GB | 16 (2 nodes) | 4 |
| B300 288 GB | 8 | 2 |

Four times fewer GPUs at every size, which is the point of a CI fixture. The card's own serving line is
`--tensor-parallel-size 4` on B300, which leaves about 169 GB a GPU free after weights. That is generous on purpose:
K3's million-token window needs it. The Kimi K3 sibling's card measured what vLLM actually allocated on four GB300s:
"Model memory: 119.06 GiB per GPU" and 7,615,283 tokens of FP8 KV cache (reported). Four times 119.06 GiB is 476.24
GiB, 7.6% above its 442.50 GiB payload (reasoned), so real per-GPU shares run a little over the even split.

Two re-pricings are worth reading off the planner. Upcasting the pruned model to BF16 would make it 1,475.2 GB, about
the size of the source as shipped: MXFP4 and 75% pruning each buy roughly a factor of three. And storing the
non-expert part in FP8 instead of BF16 takes the pruned model from 475.5 GB to 418.5 GB without touching an expert
(reasoned). Whether K3's attention and latent projections survive FP8 is a question nobody has published an answer
to; the quantization config deliberately leaves `self_attn`, `shared_experts` and the latent projections out of MXFP4.

## How expert pruning chooses

There are three families of answers to "which experts can go?", and they differ in what they need to see.

**Router frequency.** Run a calibration set through the model, count how often each expert lands in a token's top-k,
drop the least used. It is the obvious baseline. It needs real forward passes, and it says nothing about how much an
expert contributes when it is picked.

**REAP.** Cerebras' Router-weighted Expert Activation Pruning ([arXiv 2510.13999](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.13999))
scores each expert by the average, over the calibration tokens routed to it, of its gate value times the norm of its
output (reported, from the paper; the [Comet 60B article](/articles/comet-60b) covers a model pruned with it). It
also needs forward passes, and adds the output norm, so a rarely picked expert that matters a great deal when it is
picked can survive.
[Runtime dynamic compression](/articles/runtime-dynamic-compression) goes a step further and swaps the resident set
every 256 tokens.

**Weight-only scores.** Look at the router rows alone. L1 magnitude is the converter's default; the signed sum is what
shipped. They need no data at all, which is the only reason to use them.

Why would the signed sum of a router row mean anything? The router's logit for expert i on hidden state x is
$w_i \cdot x$. Write x as a shared mean $\mu$ plus a token-specific part. Averaged over tokens, the logit is
$w_i \cdot \mu$. The signed sum is $w_i \cdot \mathbf{1}$, the same dot product against the all-ones vector. So the
signed sum ranks experts by their expected logit exactly when the mean hidden state points along all-ones, and in
proportion to how much of $\mu$ does (reasoned). L1 magnitude, by contrast, ranks rows by how large they are in any
direction, which says little about whether the hidden states ever point their way. I cannot measure $\mu$ for K3
without running it, so I cannot say why the signed sum did better here. The card does not say either.

## A toy: when can a weight-only score stand in for frequency?

To see the mechanism, I built one MoE layer in numpy routed the way K3's is: sigmoid scores, the top 4 of 64
experts chosen on score plus a balance bias, gates renormalized without the bias, each expert a small ReLU MLP. Hidden
states are a shared offset plus Gaussian noise. I pruned to 8, 16, 32 or 48 experts by four scores, measured on 6,000
calibration tokens where a score needs them, and compared the pruned layer's output with the full layer's on 6,000
held-out tokens. The error is squared error over the squared size of the full output, so 1.0 is as bad as deleting
the routed output entirely. Eight seeds, numpy 2.5.3, two threads, 45 seconds (measured). The core:

```python
# toy3.py (core). W: router (E, d); b: balance bias; ids: experts kept
def route(X, ids):
    s = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(X @ W[ids].T)))                # sigmoid scores
    top = np.argsort(-(s + b[ids]), 1)[:, :k]             # bias picks, does not weigh
    g = np.take_along_axis(s, top, 1)
    return np.asarray(ids)[top], g / g.sum(1, keepdims=True)

if balanced:                                              # aux-loss-free balancing
    for _ in range(300):
        e, _ = route(Xc, np.arange(E))
        load = np.bincount(e.ravel(), minlength=E)
        b -= 0.002 * np.sign(load - load.mean())

scores = {"frequency": np.bincount(ec.ravel(), minlength=E),
          "signed": W.sum(1), "l1": np.abs(W).sum(1), "random": rng.random(E)}
```

<PruneToy />

Keeping 16 of 64, the same 25% as the real checkpoint (measured, toy):

| hidden-state offset | load | frequency | signed sum | L1 | random |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| along all-ones | skewed | 0.737 | 0.753 | 1.306 | 1.435 |
| random direction | skewed | 0.711 | 1.445 | 1.350 | 1.437 |
| along all-ones | balanced | 1.454 | 1.443 | 1.460 | 1.473 |
| random direction | balanced | 1.456 | 1.461 | 1.469 | 1.472 |

Three things fall out of it.

1. **The signed sum is a frequency estimate in disguise, when the geometry lines up.** With the offset along all-ones,
   it keeps 14.5 of the 16 experts frequency would keep, and its error is within 0.02 of frequency's. Move the offset
   to a random direction and it keeps 4.1 of 16, which is what random keeps, with the same error as random.
2. **L1 does not track usage in either case.** It is a little better than random on average, nowhere near frequency.
3. **Balancing removes most of what any ranking can find.** Without the bias, the 16 most-used experts handle 60.6% of
   routing decisions; with it, 25.7%, barely above an even quarter. Then frequency, signed sum, L1 and random all land
   between 1.443 and 1.473. When every expert does a share of the work, dropping three quarters of them drops three
   quarters of the work, whichever three quarters you pick.

Above 1.0 is not a bug. When a token's chosen experts are gone, the renormalized gates put full weight on whichever
survivors it scores highest, which can be further from the right answer than nothing.

The toy's experts are independent random networks, so none is redundant with another. Real experts are not like that,
and REAP's argument is that redundancy is what makes pruning work; I left REAP out of the toy because a model with no
redundancy cannot test it fairly. What the toy does say about K3 is narrower (reasoned). K3 was trained with
`noaux_tc` balancing, so its natural load should be close to the balanced rows. On such a model a 75% cut costs a lot
whatever the score, and the gap between two weight-only scores comes from how they interact with K3's own geometry,
not from either of them finding the important experts.

## The quality numbers

There is one, and it needs care:

| checkpoint | GSM8K 5-shot | harness | source |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Kimi K3 | 95.53% | vLLM `gsm8k_eval.py`, 256 tokens | K3 sibling card (reported) |
| Kimi K3, pruned 50% | 68.23% | same | K3 sibling card (reported) |
| Kimi K3, pruned 75% | 34.42% | same | K3 sibling card (reported) |
| Kimi K3.1 Pro, pruned 75%, signed sum | 43.67% ± 1.37 | `lm_eval` flexible-extract | this card (reported) |
| Kimi K3.1 Pro, pruned 75%, L1 | 14.4% | not stated | this card (reported) |

The K3 rows are one controlled comparison: same 1,319 questions, same prompts, same hardware, same vLLM checkout,
and they show a 61.11-point drop at 75%. The K3.1 Pro rows use a different harness and give no unpruned K3.1 Pro
baseline at all, so 43.67% cannot be read as "K3.1 Pro loses about 50 points" except by borrowing a baseline from
another model and another harness. Strict-match is 43.06%, so answer extraction is not the gap (reported). There are no
coding, agentic, long-context or vision numbers, and a model that gets fewer than half of grade-school maths word
problems right is unlikely to be strong on any of them (reasoned).

The card says what to expect: "Expect a large quality regression". It is right.

## What I ran, and what I did not

Ran (measured):

- 192 header reads by HTTP range across both repos' 96 shards, and a full tensor-by-tensor tally.
- 368 range requests for every `gate.weight` and `e_score_correction_bias` in 92 layers of both repos, about 1.5 GB
  streamed into memory and reduced to per-row statistics and hashes; nothing was stored.
- The signed, L1 and bias rankings in float64, and the row-hash match, for every layer.
- The toy, `toy3.py`, at 64 experts, four keep counts, four conditions and eight seeds.

Read but did not run: the card's conversion script and `MagnitudeExpertPruner` itself. The sandbox has no PyTorch,
and the conversion reads 1.5 TB; my numpy ranking reproduces its output in every layer, which is the part that can be
checked. Did not run: the model. No GSM8K of my own, no tokens per second, no check of how much of a hidden state
lies along all-ones. Those need the GPUs the model is for.

## The take

`Kimi-K3.1-Pro-pruned75` is exactly what its card says. The 224 survivors in every layer are the top 224 by signed
router-row sum, copied byte for byte with their balance biases, and nothing else in the 2.8T model was touched. The
parameter count falls to 737.6B and the payload to 475.5 GB, which takes the weights from eight B300s to two, while
each token still runs the same 104B parameters.

The choice of score is the part to take away. Two data-free rankings of the same router rows agree on about 69 of
224 experts, barely above chance, and one does three times as well as the other on the only benchmark measured. The
toy shows why that is possible: a signed row sum is the expected router logit when the hidden states lean along
all-ones, and it is noise otherwise. On a load-balanced MoE neither score can find much. That is why the vLLM team
calls this a smoke test and is building calibration-based REAP for the real thing. Use it to test your serving stack
on four GPUs. Do not use it to judge Kimi K3.1 Pro.
