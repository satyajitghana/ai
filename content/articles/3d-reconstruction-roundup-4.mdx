The [last roundup](/articles/3d-reconstruction-roundup-3) asked how much of
each release a reader could check from outside. This one starts somewhere
else: what is the mechanism, in enough detail that you could implement it
from the page. Four of the five papers are about Gaussian splatting. One
changes what a primitive is (Spaghetti Splatting), one changes what
supervises it (AffineGS), one moves the Gaussian into an image codec
(Gaussian Grids), and one predicts splats from a stereo rig in a single pass
(StereoSplat). The fifth, Click3R, is about the feed-forward point-map models
that now sit in front of many splatting pipelines, and what to do when they
get a scene wrong.

## Spaghetti Splatting: a Gaussian whose point became a line

Rasterisers draw points, lines and triangles. A learned scene would be most
useful as those primitives, but each of them is a Dirac mass: no extent, so a
pixel beside it gets no gradient telling it to move closer. The paper, from
Andrea Tagliasacchi's group at Simon Fraser with Wayve and Toronto, starts
from an idea old enough to sit inside EWA splatting and Poisson
reconstruction. Convolve the Dirac with a smooth kernel and you get a density
that volume rendering can differentiate. A 3D Gaussian is exactly that, a
Dirac at $\boldsymbol\mu$ convolved with $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf 0, \boldsymbol\Sigma)$:

$$
\mathcal G(\mathbf x) = \big[\delta(\cdot - \boldsymbol\mu) \circledast \mathcal N_{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\big](\mathbf x).
$$

Written that way, 3DGS has two knobs. The kernel is one, and Deformable Beta
Splatting already swapped the Gaussian for a compact Beta. The thing being
convolved is the other. Points and triangles have been tried; this paper
tries a segment.

### From a segment to a density

A segment from $\mathbf a$ to $\mathbf b$ is the measure
$m(\mathbf x) = \int_0^1 \delta(\mathbf x - \mathbf a - u\mathbf v)\,du$ with
$\mathbf v = \mathbf b - \mathbf a$: unit mass spread evenly along the line.
Convolve it with the same Gaussian and scale by a density $\rho$:

$$
\sigma(\mathbf x) = \rho \int_0^1 \mathcal N_{\boldsymbol\Sigma}\big(\mathbf x - \mathbf a - u\mathbf v\big)\,du .
$$

Every point of the segment contributes a Gaussian and the density is their
sum. Set $\mathbf a = \mathbf b$ and the integral collapses to one Gaussian,
so a 3DGS scene is a Spaghetti scene whose segments all have zero length.
The "strict superset" claim is true by construction.

### The ray integral is an error function

Volume rendering needs the optical depth $\tau$ along each ray
$\mathbf r(t) = \mathbf o + t\mathbf d$. The density is already an integral
over $u$, so $\tau$ is a double integral, and because the Gaussian is
non-negative the order can be swapped. A 3D Gaussian integrated along a line
is a 1D Gaussian in $u$, with a centre $\bar u$ (the point of the segment
closest to the ray, in the kernel's metric) and a precision $\kappa^2$ that
says how quickly the segment leaves the ray. A Gaussian integrated over
$u \in [0, 1]$ is a difference of error functions:

$$
\tau(\mathbf r) = \frac{\rho\, f(\bar u)\sqrt\pi}{2\kappa}\Big[\operatorname{erf}(\kappa\bar u) + \operatorname{erf}\big(\kappa(1 - \bar u)\big)\Big],
\qquad \alpha = 1 - e^{-\tau}.
$$

$f(\bar u)$ is the kernel's line integral at the closest point. A ray far
from the segment makes it tiny. A ray grazing the middle puts both error
functions near one. A ray passing beyond an end drives one of them negative
and the contribution fades. A triangle convolved with a Gaussian would need
Owen's T function at this step; a segment needs only erf, which the authors
give as the practical reason to stop at curves.

Evaluating that per primitive and per pixel is too slow for training, so the
paper does what EWA splatting does for points. It linearises the camera
projection once per frame at the segment's midpoint and scales the projected
kernel along the segment by inverse depth, so the near end of a receding
segment is drawn wider than the far end. Per-pixel work drops to a few
screen-space quantities, and the Beta kernel fits too, since nothing now
depends on the kernel being Gaussian in 3D. Mip-Splatting's anti-aliasing
filters add to the projected covariance along the whole segment and come
along for free.

### Why a thin strand wants a segment

A strand of width $\sigma$ drawn with points needs its points close together
or it beads. The widget draws a line ten units long both ways in the image
plane, using the paper's formula: one segment, or the same total mass as $K$
Gaussian points.

<SegmentKernel />

At $\sigma = 1$ the centre line of five points ripples by 17% of its mean,
six points (spacing $2\sigma$) by 2.9%, seven by 0.3%. Those are my toy's
numbers, and they say a line of length $L$ needs about $L / 2\sigma + 1$
Gaussians before it stops looking like a string of beads. A spoke one pixel
wide wants a Gaussian every two pixels of its length. The segment needs one
primitive and has no ripple. The paper's Figure 3 shows the same thing on a
real scene: the 3DGS-MCMC centres on the bicycle spokes are scattered dots,
and the Spaghetti segments run along the spokes.

Subdivision is exact, and this matters more for training than it looks.
The convolution is linear, so cutting a segment at its midpoint into two
halves that keep its density per unit length splits $\tau$ into two terms
that sum to the original. Compositing the halves gives
$(1 - \alpha_1)(1 - \alpha_2) = e^{-\tau_1 - \tau_2} = 1 - \alpha$, the
parent's transmittance, and the image does not move. The widget's last
readout checks the density half of that: the two halves differ from the
parent by about $10^{-16}$ of its peak, which is rounding. Training keeps
3DGS-MCMC's relocation, splits long segments without a jump in the loss,
clones short ones, and merges polylines whose endpoints come close, helped by
a loss that pulls nearby endpoints together. The project page adds that each
kernel's local x axis is its segment's direction, so when a simulator bends a
strand, the kernels turn with it.

### A tie on rooms, a clear win on hair

On Mip-NeRF 360 the paper's table has the Beta-kernel version at 28.78 dB
PSNR, against 28.71 for DBS with the same Spherical Voronoi appearance model
and 28.39 for 3DGS-MCMC. The Gaussian-kernel version leads on Shelly with
37.38 against 37.22 and on Fuzzy with 35.03 against 35.00. Those margins are
0.07, 0.16 and 0.03 dB: a tie on general scenes, which is what a superset of
3DGS ought to deliver. The concurrent Line Primitives method of Tojo et al.
scores 8.66 dB on Mip-NeRF 360 because its lines keep a constant screen width
and cannot represent a room.

Frame rate is close without being equal. On an RTX 4090 the paper measures
177 frames per second against 198 for 3DGS-MCMC on Mip-NeRF 360, 452 against
464 on Shelly and 415 against 422 on Fuzzy, between 89% and 98% of the point
version.

Hair is where the primitive pays. On eight GeomHair hairstyles plus Cem
Yuksel's curly model, rendered at 1200×800 with 88 training and 12 test
views, it beats Gaussian Haircut by 2.7 dB (32.25 against 29.56) and by 14.3
points of strand F1 at 2 mm (61.2 against 46.9), with no hair prior, and the
strands go straight into position-based dynamics.

One row of the ablation table deserves more attention than the paper gives
it. The mean connected component is 3.50 segments for the full method and
1.00 with merging off, at PSNR 33.54 against 33.59. Merging costs nothing in
image quality, but the "long connected strands" of the abstract average three
and a half segments on these benchmarks. The conclusion is candid about it:
"broader claims about automatically recovered topology require further
evaluation".

Nothing runs yet. There is a project page with a web viewer of three scenes,
the pasta among them, and a repository whose only file is a README reading
"Code coming on November 23rd, 2026"; that was the state when I cloned it on
12 November.

## AffineGS: one scale and shift is not enough

3DGS renders well and reconstructs geometry badly: layered, floating, overfit
shells. The cheap fix is a monocular depth network as a prior. Depth
Anything V2 and its peers predict relative depth, so the prior has to be
aligned first, and the usual move (DRGS, and the monocular variant of
DN-Splatter) is one global scale and shift solved by least squares. AffineGS,
from dConstruct Robotics and NUS, accepted at NeurIPS 2026, measures how
wrong that is. In its Table 1, on MuSHRoom, one affine transform leaves 41%
of pixels outside a 0.02 residual. Split each image into three regions with
their own transforms and 84.9% fit; with five, 92.7%. ScanNet++ behaves the
same, 58.6% then 84.6% then 91.8%, with RMSE falling from 0.088 to 0.039.

  caption="The paper's case in one figure: monocular disparity against the truth falls on three different lines, one per region (scatter), so one global scale and shift cannot fit it; AffineGS's smoothed scale field (right) follows those regions (AffineGS paper, Figure 1)."

### Scale from a ratio of gradients

Let $d(u,v)$ be the monocular disparity and $\mathcal D(u,v)$ the depth 3DGS
currently renders. AffineGS models the relation per pixel:

$$
\frac{1}{d} = \alpha(u,v)\,\mathcal D + \beta(u,v).
$$

Differentiate with respect to image position, assume the fields vary more
slowly than depth ($\nabla\alpha \approx 0$, $\nabla\beta \approx 0$), and
the shift drops out:

$$
-\frac{1}{d^2}\nabla d \approx \alpha\,\nabla\mathcal D
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\alpha = \frac{\lVert\nabla d\rVert}{d^2\lVert\nabla\mathcal D\rVert + \epsilon},
\qquad
\beta = \frac1d - \alpha\,\mathcal D .
$$

The local scale is a ratio of gradient magnitudes, clamped to its 5th and
95th percentiles, and the shift follows from the model. A guided filter then
smooths both fields, with the disparity image as its guide and a radius of
550 to 650 pixels on MuSHRoom, so they stay flat inside a surface and break
at the edges $d$ already has. The smoothed fields give a corrected depth
$\hat{\mathcal D} = (1 - \beta_s d)/(\alpha_s d)$, which is fitted once more
with a global $a\hat{\mathcal D} + b$ to the render so the scene cannot
drift. It supervises through three losses. A gradient loss matches
$-\alpha_s d^2 \nabla\mathcal D$ to $\nabla d$ in magnitude and direction. A
second copy of it computes $\nabla\mathcal D$ from the rendered normals and
the intrinsics, which constrains the Gaussians' orientations and not only
their depths. An L1 depth loss anchors the scale. Weights 0.1, 0.08 and 0.4,
and every field is re-estimated each iteration.

### Without the filter, the prior says nothing

Before smoothing, the fields are computed from the render, so they reproduce
it. Substitute $\beta = 1/d - \alpha\mathcal D$ into the corrected depth and
$\mathcal D$ comes back unchanged; that is two lines of algebra on the paper's
Eq. 1 and Eq. 9. A per-pixel affine field can fit anything. The prior
carries information only because the guided filter forces the fields to be
nearly constant across a surface, so the target takes its shape from $d$
within each surface and its scale from the render averaged over it. The
filter radius is the real hyperparameter.

The widget is a one-row version of that argument, written for this article,
not taken from the authors' code. Three surfaces, a monocular prediction that
is exactly affine within each surface with different coefficients per
surface, and a render that is right on average apart from a dent, a bump and
blotchy noise.

<AffineAlign />

With the surfaces disagreeing fully, one scale and shift fitted to the true
depth leaves a mean error of 7.9 cm, with 46% of pixels within 5 cm. One fit
per surface leaves none, since the toy's distortion is exactly piecewise
affine; that is the paper's Table 1 in miniature. The estimator, which only
sees the render, gives 4.6 cm against the render's own 5.5 cm at 5 cm of
render noise, so the target pulls the render the right way. Give it a clean
render and it lands at 3.8 cm against 3.3: nothing to add, as the algebra
predicts. In one dimension a wide window hurts, 7.8 cm at a radius of 120 of
360 pixels, because the filter averages across surfaces. I could not
reproduce the benefit of the paper's very large window in 1D, and I can't
tell from here whether that is the toy's limit or a property of images, where
a big 2D window still covers mostly one surface.

### The 0.71 holds, with three asterisks

The X thread that circulated the paper quoted a MuSHRoom mesh F-score of 0.71
against 0.28 for DN-Splatter with monocular depth. Table 4 prints 0.7097
against 0.2765, so the quote is accurate. It is less than it sounds:

- The 0.28 is a baseline the authors built ("For a fair sensor-free
  comparison, we introduce DN-Splatter (Monodepth)"), meshed with the same
  TSDF fusion as AffineGS. Against published sensor-free methods the gap is
  smaller: 2DGS 0.6039, SuGaR 0.6378, Splatfacto 0.5835.
- "Sensor-free" describes the losses. The paper initialises the Gaussians
  from "1M back-projected points from training dataset sensor depths",
  following DN-Splatter, and the released data parser
  (`dn_splatter/data/mushroom_dataparser.py`) loads each scene's
  `iphone_pointcloud.ply`.
- The settings are per scene. The released `run_train_paper.sh` sets the
  guided filter's radius to 550, 600 or 650 and its epsilon anywhere from 0.08
  to 0.5 scene by scene, and halves all three loss weights on `kokko` and
  quarters them on `sauna`, where its comment says the defaults "fill with
  floaters". The appendix lists the same values.

## Gaussian Grids: a feature grid on every Gaussian

This one is about images, not scenes. Neural image codecs come in two
families. Feature-grid methods (Instant-NGP, Neural Texture Compression)
store a regular grid of learned features and decode with a small MLP:
compact and fast, but they spend as much on sky as on a fence. Primitive
methods (GaussianImage, Image-GS) put 2D Gaussians where the detail is, but
each Gaussian carries its own position, shape and colour, and fine texture
needs a great many of them. Gaussian-Grid, from NYU and Intel for WACV 2027,
lets the Gaussians decide where capacity goes and stores the capacity as a
small feature grid attached to each one. The summary that circulated had it
as grids for flat regions and Gaussians for the rest. It is closer to the
reverse: every Gaussian carries a grid, and one low-resolution "canvas" grid
covers the smooth background.

  alt="Five panels of a photo of star trails over a lifeguard tower: the target image; its local frequency map in grey; the frequency map re-rendered from optimised 2D Gaussians; a crop showing rainbow-coloured feature grids laid along the star trails; and the reconstructed image."

### Fit the frequency map first, the image second

For every pixel, take the 8×8 window around it, compute its 2D DFT and keep
the largest magnitude that is not the DC term:

$$
F(i,j) = \max_{\mathbf u \neq 0}\big|\mathcal F[I_{(i,j)}](\mathbf u)\big| .
$$

The map is high on texture and edges and near zero on flat colour. Stage one
fits that map, not the image, with anisotropic 2D Gaussians that each carry a
scalar frequency $f_n$, under an L1 loss plus a pairwise overlap penalty that
makes them tile the image instead of piling up: 3,000 steps in Taichi,
starting from 800 to 2,300 Gaussians depending on the budget.

Stage two gives each Gaussian a share of the bit budget proportional to its
frequency and its footprint,

$$
w_n = \frac{|f_n|}{\max_j |f_j|}\cdot 4\sigma_{x,n}\cdot 4\sigma_{y,n},
$$

raised where the local frequency vectors point many ways and lowered where
they agree, with the grid's height and width following the Gaussian's
anisotropy. The canvas takes 30% of the budget by default, more for flat
images. A pixel samples the top $K = 5$ Gaussian grids covering it,
bilinearly and in each Gaussian's own frame, averages the valid samples, adds
the canvas sample and decodes the 6-dimensional feature with a two-layer MLP
of width 64. Grids train at 4 bits with quantisation-aware training, the MLP
at 8, the Gaussians frozen to fp16, for 75,000 steps. The paper puts that at
24.3 minutes per image on an A100, which is the price of the format.

### Better PSNR, worse LPIPS than a plain grid

On 100 DIV2K validation images it wins clearly at the lowest budget, 25.58 dB
at 0.1 bits per pixel against 24.72 for the next method, and ties or edges
ahead higher up: 29.36 against 29.36 at 0.4, 32.55 against 32.43 at 1.0. The
ablation is less tidy. A single uniform grid given the whole budget reaches
32.08 dB at 1.0 bpp to the full method's 32.55, but the uniform grid wins on
SSIM (0.9069 against 0.8999), MS-SSIM and LPIPS (0.0993 against 0.1167). The
paper explains the trade plainly: capacity moves from flat regions to
textured ones, PSNR goes up and the perceptual scores in smooth areas go
down.

The bits-per-pixel accounting is where I would push back. The application
section compares against JPEG plus ZIP on the same 100 images and lists
dataset sizes. At "0.1 bpp" the 100 encoded images take 4.62 MB, 46.2 KB
each, and the paper gives a decoded image as 8,306 KB, about 2.77 million RGB
pixels. Divide one by the other and the nominal 0.1 is 0.13 bits per pixel;
the other budgets come out near 0.43, 0.73 and 1.03. The excess is an almost
constant 10 to 12 KB per image, which looks like the Gaussians and the decoder
left out of the nominal budget, though the paper does not say. The JPEG
comparison is size-matched, so it stands: 2.33 dB better than JPEG at the same
size, decoded in 5.44 ms against 11.65 on an RTX 5080, and 47 KB over PCIe
instead of 8,306 KB, the abstract's 176×. At 1.0 bpp JPEG is better by 1.00
dB. Whether the primitive baselines' 0.1 bpp counts the same bytes is the
open question.

The paper says the source "is released at" a GitHub repository. The GitHub
API shows that repository was created on 21 October with a size of zero, and
cloning it returns an empty repository.

## Click3R: a few clicks into DUSt3R

DUSt3R and its successors predict a point map for two images in one forward
pass. When they get it wrong (repeated texture, a symmetric building, a
large viewpoint change) the only options are to accept the output or fall
back to optimisation. Click3R, from the University of Virginia, adds an
input: a few correspondences a person clicks, one point in each image per
click, the way SAM takes clicks for segmentation.

### How a click becomes a token

DUSt3R encodes each image with a shared ViT-Large (24 blocks, width 1024),
projects the patch tokens to width 768 and runs two 12-block decoders that
cross-attend to each other. Click3R sits between the projection and the first
decoder block. Each clicked point becomes a token made of three parts: its
coordinates, normalised to the token grid and encoded with SAM's random
Fourier features; a learned embedding for which view it is in; and a learned
embedding for which click it is, shared by both ends. The pair embedding is
what tells the network that this point in image one is that point in image
two. Per view, a two-layer adapter runs self-attention over the clicks,
cross-attention from clicks to image tokens, an MLP, and cross-attention from
image tokens back to clicks, and its output updates the image tokens. The
image positional encoding uses the same Fourier features, so a click and the
patch under it share one coordinate space. With no clicks the adapter is the
identity.

The adapter is small; the training is not. Everything is trained end to end
from CroCo v2 with simulated clicks, a random number per sample, and freezing
DUSt3R to train only the adapter is the worst of the three variants the paper
tried, 71.2 mAA@30 against 75.1. The README lists the checkpoint at 566.6M
parameters against 532.3M for DUSt3R retrained on the same data, so the
clicks cost about 34M parameters. Inputs are 224×224.

### Hard3DBench is mostly wide baselines

The authors built Hard3DBench to make DUSt3R fail: 404 in-distribution and
300 out-of-distribution pairs, plus 5-view tuples. The category file they
released shows what "hard" mostly means. In `categories.json`, 371 of the 404
in-distribution pairs and 235 of the 300 out-of-distribution pairs are
tagged large baseline, many of them large viewpoint as well. Symmetric and
textureless scenes, named in the abstract, are not categories at all. On the
out-of-distribution pairs the retrained DUSt3R scores 52.6 mAA@30; one click
gives 56.6, four give 60.4, eight give 62.4.

Pi3 at the same 224 resolution, with no clicks, scores 71.1 on those pairs,
and VGGT at its native 518 scores 81.3. The paper lists them as references
rather than baselines because they are larger and trained on more data, which
is fair. The practical reading is still that clicks fix DUSt3R, not that they
beat a stronger model. The standard-benchmark gains that look largest also
come from oracle click selection. "1p_best5" picks the best of five random
clicks by reconstruction loss; the "grid oracle" tries one click in each of
about 256 cells and keeps whichever most reduces the pose error. That is where
84.27 to 92.75 on ARKitScenes comes from. It measures headroom, not what a
person achieves.

### What one person's clicks did, and on what

The human study is the part that measures a person. One annotator, 100
out-of-distribution pairs, no access to depth or poses: median rotation error
falls from 17.30° to 8.78°, mAA@30 rises from 36.2 to 56.2, and four random
ground-truth clicks reach 8.40°. The paper's "96% of the gap" is
$(17.30 - 8.78)/(17.30 - 8.40) = 95.7\%$, so that checks.

I read the released `human_clicks.json`. It holds 377 click pairs over the
100 image pairs, a mean of 3.77 and a range of 1 to 7, which matches the
paper's 3.8. It also shows that 77 of the 100 pairs come from DTU, 12 from
ETH3D, 4 each from NRGBD and 7-Scenes and 3 from ScanNet. DTU is tabletop
objects shot from a robot arm, so the human result is mostly a result about
objects. The perturbation analysis adds a practical limit: predictions start
following a wrong click once it is off by half of a 16-pixel patch, 8 pixels.

Of the five, this is the release with the least missing: training and
evaluation code (MIT), weights (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0), a Gradio demo on Hugging
Face Spaces, and Hard3DBench's sample lists, labels, clicks and unrounded
paper numbers (CC BY 4.0). The benchmark does not redistribute images; the
code prepares them from the source datasets.

## StereoSplat and XRStereo: let the rig set the scale

Feed-forward splatting models (pixelSplat, MVSplat, DepthSplat, AnySplat)
take a few images and emit Gaussians in one pass. Their geometry is up to
scale, or comes from cost volumes that blur with distance. A headset or a
robot usually has something better: a calibrated stereo pair, from which
metric depth is $f b / d$ for focal length $f$, baseline $b$ and disparity
$d$. StereoSplat, from Meta and the University of Amsterdam, builds the
splatting model around that.

<Figure
  src="/articles/3d-reconstruction-roundup-4/fig6.png"
  alt="Pipeline diagram. Left: stereo frames enter a grounding module that outputs multi-scale features and depth maps, which a Gaussian decoder turns into Gaussians, which a learnable aggregation module merges into a compact set. Right: the grounding module runs FoundationStereo on the left-right pair and on the flipped right-left pair, producing two disparity maps that become two depth maps using the focal length and baseline, plus multi-scale features."
  caption="StereoSplat: a frozen FoundationStereo gives disparity for both views (the right one by flipping the pair) and a feature pyramid; a decoder predicts per-pixel Gaussians; aggregation merges them in inverse-depth space (StereoSplat paper, Figure 2)."
/>

### Four stages on top of a frozen stereo model

1. *Grounding.* A frozen FoundationStereo computes the left view's disparity.
   For the right view, flip both images and swap them so the right image
   plays "left", then flip the output back. Depth for both views is
   $f b / d$, and the same pass yields a four-level feature pyramid.
2. *Cross-view fusion.* At every pyramid level and pixel position, the
   features of all $2N$ views form a sequence of length $2N$ for a small
   transformer. Attention is $2N \times 2N$ per pixel whatever the image
   size, and zero-initialised output projections make it start as the
   identity.
3. *A gated depth.* The decoder upsamples to full resolution, predicts
   Gaussian attributes per pixel and a refined depth $\hat D$, and blends it
   with the stereo depth by one learned scalar,
   $D' = (1 - \alpha) D + \alpha \hat D$, initialised to trust the stereo.
4. *Merging in inverse depth.* The $2NHW$ Gaussians overlap heavily across
   frames. Each centre maps to $(x/z,\, y/z,\, 1/z)$ and is hashed on a grid of
   size $\delta$, so a cell is about $\delta z$ wide and $\delta z^2$ deep:
   fine nearby, coarse far away. Quantised colour, scale and opacity join the
   hash key so a cell never merges across an edge, and Gaussians in a cell
   are averaged with predicted softmax weights that start uniform.

Training renders the merged Gaussians into held-out views with gsplat and
supervises colour (L1, SSIM, LPIPS) and metric depth (L1), on synthetic data
only: XRStereo, TartanAir and Infinigen.

### The trained part is 5.1 million parameters

I read the header of `stereosplat_v1.safetensors` on Hugging Face with two
range requests. It holds 317 tensors and 5,143,087 parameters, all float32,
about a third of them in the three cross-view fusion blocks. The config
stored in the header sets spherical-harmonic degree 0, so colour is not view
dependent, and a maximum depth of 80. What StereoSplat trains is a small head;
the geometry comes from FoundationStereo, which the README has you clone
separately under NVIDIA's own licence.

On KITTI, outside its training data, the paper reports novel-view depth Abs
Rel of 0.04 against DepthSplat's 0.13, δ1 of 96.0 against 79.8, and PSNR of
25.2 against 22.3 for pixelSplat. On Infinigen, Abs Rel is 0.03 against 0.21.
Read the protocol before reading those as a contest. The baselines run their
public checkpoints, not retrained on stereo; MVSplat, pixelSplat and VicaSplat
run at their native 256×256 against StereoSplat's 320×640; and the pose-free
ones work in a normalised space where metric depth means nothing. The
pose-aware baselines do get ground-truth poses while StereoSplat uses MAC-VO
odometry. The geometry gap is mostly the rig's information, which is the
paper's thesis, and it says little about whether the decoder is better at
what the baselines attempt. The aggregation's compression rate in the
ablation is a modest 1.6.

### XRStereo: what the paper says and what was uploaded

The paper describes 137k frames in 20k sequences across 9 indoor and 4
outdoor environments, objects drawn from 200 assets, rendered at 1K with
horizontal fields of view from 50 to 130 degrees and baselines from 40 to
120 mm. The Hugging Face card and its `manifest.json` describe the upload:
17,922 sequences of 5 stereo frames, 89,610 stereo frames and 179,220 images
at 1024×1024, 372.8 GB in 72 tar shards, CC BY-NC 4.0, and "This release is
the StereoSplat training split". Nothing published reconciles the paper's
137k frames with the upload's 89,610.

I pulled the first 40 MB of shard 0 with a range request, which holds three
sequences. Their focal lengths give horizontal fields of view of exactly 70,
60 and 80 degrees, their baselines are 120, 60 and 40 mm, and the camera moves
4.7, 11 and 8 cm between frames. Left and right disparities agree within one
pixel on 91% to 100% of pixels, the rest being occlusion. The first sequence
is a blank wall about 0.6 m away. The scenes are rooms full of floating,
randomly posed objects, closer to FlyingThings than to a home.

## Where I'd spend time

If you build splatting pipelines, read Spaghetti Splatting and AffineGS
properly; skim the rest.

Spaghetti Splatting is the idea I expect to outlast its paper. The point
inside a Gaussian was a choice, and a segment keeps everything that made the
point convenient: a closed form, the EWA linearisation, anti-aliasing, and a
subdivision that does not change the image. On rooms it ties 3DGS, as a
superset should. On hair, spokes and wires it wins outright. The connected
strands are shorter than the abstract implies, and there is no code until 23
November, so hold judgement on the simulation story until you can run it.

AffineGS makes a point I agree with: a single global scale and shift throws
away most of what a monocular prior knows. The method holds up, with three
asterisks on its headline number: a baseline the authors built, a sensor
point cloud at initialisation, and a filter tuned scene by scene. If you
already regularise splats with Depth Anything, the per-surface alignment is
the part to steal; the code is Apache-2.0.

Gaussian Grids is a careful hybrid, but I don't buy the 0.1 bpp headline
until the Gaussians and the decoder are counted, and there is no code to
check it against. Click3R is the most complete release of the week and the
easiest to try in the browser. Its own data says the human study is mostly
DTU objects and that Pi3 without clicks still leads on its hard pairs, so I
would put clicks on Pi3 before I put them on DUSt3R. StereoSplat's geometry
numbers come from information the baselines never had. What I would actually
download is XRStereo, if non-commercial use is enough for you.

For where these primitives end up, see [SplatAnything](/articles/splatanything),
[InfiniSplat v2](/articles/infinisplat-2), the capture side in

how splat colour is stored in
