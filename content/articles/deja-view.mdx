Every feed-forward reconstruction model of the last two years has grown the
same way. DUSt3R had a ViT-Large encoder and a ViT-Base decoder. VGGT has 1.26 billion
parameters. Depth Anything 3's giant variant has 1.2 billion. The accuracy went
up with the size, so nobody asked whether the size was doing what we thought.

[Déjà View](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30215), from NVIDIA's Dynamic Vision
and Learning group with Modena, Toronto and ETH Zürich (a NeurIPS 2026 oral),
asks it. Its answer is a model with one transformer block (a frame-attention
and global-attention pair) where VGGT stacks 24 such pairs, applied over and
over to the same tokens. The whole thing is
117 million parameters and it leads the five-benchmark averages against models
eight to ten times its size.

I read the paper, the [code](https://github.com/nv-tlabs/dvlt) and the header
of the [released checkpoint](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/dvlt). The parameter
story is real and more lopsided than the headline. The compute story is
narrower than "comparable or less" suggests, and it is narrower for a reason
that is easy to see once the FLOPs are written out: weight tying saves
parameters, and loops cost FLOPs.

## What a feed-forward reconstructor has to produce

Start from the classical pipeline, because the new models are trying to
delete it. Structure-from-motion matches keypoints across photos, estimates
relative poses, triangulates points, and runs bundle adjustment: a nonlinear
least-squares solve that nudges every camera and point until reprojection
error stops falling. It is iterative by construction, and it falls apart on
blank walls, repeated facades and moving things.

DUSt3R replaced that with a network that takes two images and regresses a
**pointmap** for each: an $H \times W \times 3$ array giving, for every pixel,
its 3D position in the first camera's frame. Camera pose and intrinsics fall
out of the pointmap by fitting. Two views at a time means a scene of $N$ photos
needs many pairs and a global alignment step afterwards, which is where
MASt3R-SfM spends its time.

VGGT took all $N$ views at once. Each image becomes DINOv2 patch tokens, plus a
camera token and a few register tokens. The tokens then pass through a stack
that alternates two kinds of attention:

- **frame attention**, where each view's tokens attend only to themselves;
- **global attention**, where every token of every view attends to every
  other token in the set.

Heads on top decode depth maps, pointmaps and camera parameters. VGGT runs 24
frame blocks and 24 global blocks at width 1024. Pi3, MapAnything and Depth
Anything 3 are variations on this skeleton. I've covered several of the
descendants on this site:
[HiP3R](/articles/hip3r) stitching long videos from local reconstructions,
[R³ and AMB3R-SLAM](/articles/da3-streaming-reconstruction) streaming on a
frozen Depth Anything 3, [SplatAnything](/articles/splatanything) putting a
Gaussian head on one, and in today's
[3D reconstruction roundup](/articles/3d-reconstruction-roundup-4#click3r-a-few-clicks-into-dust3r),
Click3R letting a user correct DUSt3R with clicks.

Déjà View outputs a slightly different thing from a pointmap. For each image it
predicts a depth map $\mathbf{D}$ and a six-channel **ray map** $\mathbf{R}$:
an origin $\mathbf{R}^o$ and a direction $\mathbf{R}^d$ per pixel. A world point
is then

$$
\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{R}^{o} + \mathbf{D}(u, v)\,\mathbf{R}^{d},
$$

and camera rotation, translation and intrinsics are fitted from the rays,
following Depth Anything 3. The code computes exactly this in `_decode`:
`pred_points = pred_rays[..., 3:6] + pred_rays[..., :3] * pred_depth`.

The evaluation aligns the predicted points to ground truth with a similarity
transform (Sim(3): rotation, translation, scale) and reports:

- **Rel. L2**, the mean of $\lVert \mathbf{X}_\theta - \mathbf{X}_{gt} \rVert / \lVert \mathbf{X}_{gt} \rVert$ over valid points;
- **inlier ratio (IR)**, the share of points whose relative error is under 3%;
- **pose AUC@3° and AUC@30°**, the area under the cumulative curve of per-pair
  angular pose error up to 3° and up to 30°.

AUC@30 is forgiving. AUC@3 is where a model has to get cameras nearly exactly
right, and it is where the models separate.

## Depth that might be iteration in disguise

The paper's hypothesis comes from two earlier probes. Starý and colleagues
decoded DUSt3R's intermediate layers and found the pointmap getting better
layer by layer, as if each layer were one more step of a refinement, even
though every layer has its own weights. RAPTOR (Jacobs et al., ICLR 2026)
showed the layers of a trained ViT can be approximated by a few blocks looped.

If a stack of different layers is mostly doing the same refinement repeatedly,
the different weights are paying for iteration with unique parameters. Déjà
View makes the iteration the architecture: one block, applied $K$ times, to a
state that starts as DINOv2 features.

Language models have been looping for a while: see
[what separates Ouro from Huginn](/articles/looped-models-done-right) and
[SMELT's compute-matched comparison](/articles/looped-transformers-matched-compute).
Déjà View is the first I've read that does it for multi-view geometry at full
benchmark scale.

## The block

The looped block is two sub-blocks in sequence, VGGT-style: frame attention
with 2D rotary position embeddings, then global attention over all views'
tokens. Each sub-block is the standard pre-norm attention plus MLP, with
LayerScale, and with three extra channel-wise scales the paper calls gates:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{z}' &= \mathbf{z}_k + \mathbf{s}_{\text{attn}} \odot \mathrm{LS}_1(\mathrm{Attn}(\mathrm{LN}_1(\mathbf{z}_k))) \\
\mathbf{z}'' &= \mathbf{z}' + \mathbf{s}_{\text{mlp}} \odot \mathrm{LS}_2(\mathrm{MLP}(\mathrm{LN}_2(\mathbf{z}'))) \\
\mathbf{z}_{k+1} &= \mathbf{s}_{\text{out}} \odot \mathbf{z}''
\end{aligned}
$$

The gates come from a small MLP fed with sinusoidal embeddings of two numbers,
$t_k$ and $t_{k+1}$: where this step starts and where it lands on a $[0, 1]$
time axis. The MLP's last layer is zero-initialised, so at the start of
training every gate is exactly 1 and the block is a plain transformer block.
The design is RAPTOR's; the difference is that Déjà View trains the block end to
end on the reconstruction loss, with no teacher.

Conditioning on an interval of continuous time, instead of a step index, is
what lets one set of weights serve different step counts. Step 6 of 12 and
step 8 of 16 are both "from 0.45 to 0.55 of the way", roughly, and the block
sees them as the same kind of step.

The code is short enough to quote. This is the whole inference loop, from
`src/dvlt/model/dvlt/model.py`:

```python
def _solve_inference_linspace_k(self, x, rope_pos, B, S):
    """Inference for k_sampling='linspace': uniform grid with configurable K."""
    K = self.inference_steps
    ts = torch.linspace(0.0, 1.0, K).tolist()
    for i in range(K):
        t_now = ts[i]
        t_next = ts[i + 1] if i + 1 < K else 1.0
        x = self._interval_step(x, t_now, t_next, rope_pos, B, S)
    return x
```

One detail differs from the paper. Section 3.4 writes the grid as
$t_k = k/K$, so $K$ steps cover $K$ equal intervals ending at 1. The code uses
`linspace(0, 1, K)`, which gives $K$ points, so step $i$ starts at
$i/(K-1)$ and the last step is conditioned on the degenerate interval
$(1, 1)$. The docstring calls it a "terminal-step sentinel". It is trained that
way too, so it is consistent, but if you reimplement from the paper you will
build a slightly different model.

Supervision lands only on the final state. RAFT-style refiners decode and
penalise every step; Déjà View decodes once, which saves $K-1$ decoder passes
per training step. Whether the intermediate states are any good is then an
empirical question, and the answer, further down, is "decodable but not
optimal".

## Where the 117 million parameters sit

The checkpoint is one `model.safetensors` file. Its header lists every tensor
and its shape, so I fetched the 40,920-byte header with an HTTP range request
and summed the shapes. The total is 117,081,731, which matches both the paper's
117M and the Hugging Face API's count.

| Part | Parameters | Share |
|---|---:|---:|
| DINOv2 ViT-B/14 encoder (12 layers, width 768) | 86,582,016 | 74% |
| The looped block (frame + global sub-block) | 14,495,424 | 12.4% |
| … of which the six gates' MLPs | 316,096 | 0.3% |
| Depth decoder (2 blocks at width 384, conv head) | 11,401,506 | 9.7% |
| Ray decoder (2 blocks at width 384) | 4,298,264 | 3.7% |
| Camera head, camera and register tokens | 304,521 | 0.3% |

Three-quarters of the model is the encoder, which runs once per image. The part
that does the multi-view reasoning, the part the paper is about, is 14.5
million parameters. That is less than two of VGGT's 48 aggregator blocks, which at width 1024
with a 4x MLP are about 12.6 million each.

The paper's ablation compares this against a "decoupled" model with 16
independent blocks and no gates. On the same layer shapes, 16 ungated copies
are 226.9 million parameters, so the decoupled model is about 329 million in
total. Same FLOPs, almost three times the parameters.

## What the gates do, step by step

The gate MLPs are small, 1.3 MB of float32 for both sub-blocks together, so I
range-fetched just those tensors and the LayerScale vectors from the
checkpoint and evaluated the gate network myself in NumPy for every step of
$K = 8$, 12 and 16, using the code's own time grid. The widget plots the channel
means.

<GateSchedule />

The paper reports neither of the following.

First, $\mathbf{s}_{\text{out}}$ is below 1 on average at every step. Each
application multiplies the state by about 0.73 to 0.93 per channel (channel
mean) in the frame sub-block and about 0.86 in the global sub-block, then adds
the attention and MLP updates on top. The recurrence is a leaky accumulator:
older contributions decay and new ones are added. The paper's Figure 3 shows
the state norm growing anyway, so the updates outrun the decay.

Second, the two sub-blocks use time very differently. The frame sub-block's
$\mathbf{s}_{\text{out}}$ climbs steadily from 0.73 at $t=0$ to 0.93 at the end:
early steps forget more of the state, late steps keep it. The global
sub-block's $\mathbf{s}_{\text{out}}$ barely moves (0.863 to 0.869), and only
its attention gate drifts, from 0.77 down to 0.67. The time-dependence lives
mostly in the per-view part of the loop.

That bears on [Solv3R](/articles/solv3r), which fine-tunes this exact
checkpoint and argues that a step-conditioned block cannot be continued past
its schedule. The gates are where that conditioning lives. A state gate that
moves from 0.73 to 0.93 between the first step and the last is not cosmetic;
it changes what an "extra" step does to the state.

These are means over 768 channels. In the frame sub-block, individual
channels of the state gate range from 0.02 to 1.33, so a handful of channels are being nearly reset each step and a handful
amplified. I don't have activations, only weights, so I can't say which
channels carry what.

## One checkpoint, any K from 8 to 16

Training samples the step count per batch. The code draws
$K \sim \mathrm{Beta}(2, 1)$, scales it to $[8, 16]$ and rounds. Beta(2, 1)
has density $2x$, so large $K$ is favoured; the expected value is
$8 + \tfrac{2}{3} \cdot 8 \approx 13.3$ steps. The draw is seeded identically
on every rank so all 128 GPUs run the same $K$ in a batch and nobody waits.

There are two ways to spend less than 16 steps at inference, and the paper's
Appendix E compares them:

- **Early exit**: run the 16-step schedule and decode the state after step
  $k$.
- **Re-gridded K**: run $K$ steps on a $K$-point time grid, so every step
  knows it is one of $K$.

Re-gridding wins at every budget, and the gap is largest at the smallest one.
Figure 8 averages the five benchmarks. I read its values from the SVG's own
path coordinates rather than by eye. Its 16-step AUC@3, 0.567, sits a little
under the 57.2 I get by averaging Table 2 (the appendix's Table 8 says 56.8),
so the figure probably averages slightly differently; the gaps are under a
point.

The text of Appendix E says AUC@3 at $K=8$ rises "from 0.31 to 0.44"; the
figure's own coordinates put the re-gridded point at 0.431. Small, but the
text rounds in its own favour.

AUC@30 is close to saturated by $K=8$. AUC@3, the precise-pose metric, is not:
it gains 14 points of AUC between 8 and 16 steps. If you need cameras accurate
to a degree or two, the extra loops are worth paying for. If you need a
roughly right point cloud, half of them are.

The released code has a trap here. The `DVLT` wrapper class defaults to
`inference_steps=12`, and the checkpoint's `config.json` says 12 as well. The
README's quickstart builds `DVLT(img_size=504)` and so runs 12 loops, not the
16 behind every table. The Hydra path used by `python -m dvlt.scripts.test`
passes `inference_steps: None` from `DVLTModelConfig`, which falls through to
16. That is my reading of the code; I didn't run it. It fits the README's
reference table for the `scripts.test` path, whose pose AUCs (AUC@3 of 0.8319 on DTU
and 0.4340 on nuScenes) round to Table 2's 16-step numbers. Same
weights, two defaults. Pass `inference_steps=16` if you want the paper's
numbers.

## The compute bill

Here is the claim to check: Déjà View matches or beats much larger models "at
comparable or lower compute". The paper's Table 3 measures forward-pass FLOPs
on an A100 with 24 input views:

| Model | Params (M) | TFLOPs, 24 views | vs Déjà View | Avg IR | Avg AUC@30 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Déjà View | 117 | 75.9 | 1.00x | 80.3 | 91.8 |
| DA3-L | 356 | 71.4 | 0.94x | 58.3 | 79.7 |
| VGGT-Ω-1B | 1,144 | 99.8 | 1.31x | 74.8 | 90.7 |
| MapAnything | 1,228 | 148.4 | 1.96x | 69.0 | 83.3 |
| Pi3 | 959 | 153.8 | 2.03x | 77.4 | 88.6 |
| DA3-G | 1,201 | 178.7 | 2.35x | 66.5 | 84.7 |
| VGGT | 1,257 | 190.0 | 2.50x | 66.0 | 84.0 |

The ratio column is my division. Pi3 needs 153.8 TFLOPs for the same 24 views
and VGGT needs 190.0, so against the billion-parameter models Déjà View uses
half or less of the compute, which is a real saving. Against DA3-L it uses
6% more and is far more accurate. Against the concurrent VGGT-Ω it saves a
quarter. "Comparable or lower" is accurate; "ten times smaller" is about
parameters only. The project page claims "1.9–2.3× less compute" and names
Pi3 and DA3-G, which by Table 3 come to 2.03x and 2.35x. The range is
conservative rather than wrong.

Why is the saving in parameters so much larger than the saving in FLOPs? Write
the cost out. I assumed square 504-pixel inputs (36 × 36 patches, plus 1 camera
and 4 register tokens, so 1,301 tokens per view) and counted two FLOPs per
multiply-add:

- the linear layers of one looped step cost 0.88 TFLOPs at 24 views;
- frame attention's score-and-mix costs 0.12 TFLOPs, because each view only
  sees its own 1,301 tokens;
- global attention's costs 3.00 TFLOPs, because each of 31,224 tokens sees all
  31,224.

That is 4.0 TFLOPs per loop, three-quarters of it global attention. Sixteen
loops are 64.0, the DINOv2 encoder is another 6.8 and the two decoders'
transformer blocks 0.7, which sums to 71.6. The paper measures 75.9; the
remaining 4.3 is plausibly the convolutional depth head, which upsamples to
full resolution and which I did not model. The same counting gives VGGT's
backbone 175.6 TFLOPs against its measured 190.0, so the method lands near the
paper's numbers twice. It is a model, not a measurement; the resolution in
particular is my assumption.

What it shows is the shape of the bill. Compute is set by how many global
attention layers run and how wide they are. Déjà View runs 16 at width 768;
VGGT runs 24 at width 1024. By my count that is a little under half of VGGT's
global attention work, and it is most of the 2.5x. Parameters don't enter into it. Tying the
weights made the model small; it did nothing for the FLOPs, and the paper is
careful to say "comparable" rather than "lower" for that reason.

Table 3 is also a single point: 24 views. The global term grows with the square
of the view count, so at 100 views it dominates even more, and every model in
the table pays the same quadratic. Peak memory is the other number in Table 3
that favours Déjà View: 4.9 GiB at 24 views against 14.7 for VGGT. Every model
holds about one layer's activations at a time at inference, so the gap is
more likely weights and width: VGGT's 1,257M parameters are about 4.7 GiB in
fp32 against under 0.5 GiB here, and its tokens are 1024 wide, not 768.

split from its training data) and driving (nuScenes).

The averages are the paper's strongest evidence, and I recomputed them from
Tables 1 and 2. Déjà View's mean inlier ratio is 80.3 (Pi3 77.4, VGGT-Ω 74.8)

for VGGT-Ω, 45.2 for Pi3 and 39.5 for VGGT. That ten-point lead on precise
pose is the largest margin in any of the averages, and the paper undersells it
by leaving it out of the summary table.

Cell by cell, the picture is more mixed. Counting all eight comparison models,
including VGGT-Ω, which the paper marks as concurrent work:

- **Pointmaps (Table 1).** Déjà View is best on one of ten cells (DTU Rel. L2,
  a four-way tie at 0.009). It is second on eight. Pi3 leads indoor
  (7-Scenes, ScanNet++) and DTU inlier ratio; VGGT-Ω leads both outdoor sets
  (ETH3D, nuScenes).
- **Pose (Table 2).** Best on five of ten cells (ETH3D AUC@3, ScanNet++ both,
  nuScenes AUC@3, a tie on ETH3D AUC@30), second on four, third on one
  (7-Scenes AUC@3, 13.9 against VGGT-Ω's 21.3). The paper's caption, "first or
  second on nine of ten cells", checks out.
- **VGGT still owns DTU pose.** 96.5 AUC@3 against Déjà View's 83.2, with
  ten times the parameters.

Drop VGGT-Ω and Déjà View is best on most cells. Keep it and the summary is:
best on average, rarely best on a single benchmark, never far from the best.
For a model a tenth the size that is a good result, not a sweep.

## Shared beats untied, at a smaller scale

The most interesting claim in the paper is not about size. Table 4 trains four
variants for 100K iterations on the same data, from 16 independent blocks to
the full gated, shared block:

| Variant | Rel. L2 | IR | AUC@3 | AUC@30 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Decoupled (16 blocks, no gates) | 0.056 | 61.1 | 23.0 | 82.0 |
| Shared | 0.045 | 66.4 | 30.2 | 84.8 |
| Shared + residual gates | 0.042 | 67.0 | 31.5 | 85.9 |
| Shared + state gate (full) | 0.040 | 69.2 | 33.3 | 86.9 |

Sharing alone beats the decoupled model on every metric, with 16 times fewer
parameters in the loop and identical FLOPs. If this holds at full training, the
case is that iteration with shared weights is a better inductive bias for
multi-view geometry than depth with unique ones: the same refinement, applied
again, generalises better than sixteen specialists.

I'd hold that loosely for now. These are half-length runs (100K iterations
against the final 200K), and the absolute numbers are far from the released
model's: inlier ratio 69 here against 80.3 in Table 1, AUC@3 33 against 57. A
decoupled model at three times the parameters may simply train slower and
catch up given the full schedule. The paper doesn't run it to the end, and
that's the experiment I'd want before calling it an inductive-bias result.

## What the loop learns to do

Two of the paper's analyses say something about what happens inside the loop.

Appendix C reads the global attention weights from one query patch onto another
view at each iteration. At $k=1$ the attention is diffuse; by $k=16$ it sits on
the corresponding patch. Nothing in the loss asks for correspondences. The
loop rediscovers feature matching because it needs matches to place cameras,
and it does it iteratively, which is how SfM matching plus bundle adjustment
works too.

The second analysis is Figure 3's "directional refinement". The state does not
converge to a fixed point: its norm drops for the first few steps, then grows
steadily through step 16. But its direction converges. Cosine similarity to the
final state rises to 1, and the relative size of each update falls about
fivefold. Each decoder starts with a LayerNorm, which throws away the norm, so
from the decoder's point of view the state does converge.

That growing norm is also the failure mode. Push $K$ past the trained 16 and a
few channels keep growing.

## Where it stops

- **The dial has two ends.** Below 8 steps is untrained; past 16 the state
  drifts and past about 30 it collapses (Appendix D). Inside the range,
  re-grid; don't early-exit.
- **Variable $K$ costs a little.** Table 5: training with random $K$ matches a
  model trained at fixed $K=16$ to within about 2% at 16 steps, and a fixed
  $K=12$ model to within about 3% at 12. Flexibility is not free, just cheap.
- **Compute is still quadratic in views.** One block, sixteen times, is still
  sixteen global attention passes over every token. Long sequences need
  chunking or a streaming design, as with every model in its table.
- **The weights are non-commercial.** The code is Apache-2.0 (with
  VGGT-licensed files); the checkpoint is under NVIDIA's research-only licence.
- **Two defaults.** The quickstart runs 12 steps, the evaluation 16.

## Fewer parameters, not fewer FLOPs

I buy the parameter argument completely. A 14.5-million-parameter block,
applied sixteen times on top of a DINOv2 ViT-B encoder (fine-tuned at a tenth
of the learning rate), gets the best average
accuracy on these five benchmarks against models eight to ten times its size. That says a lot of what a billion-parameter reconstructor stores is
the same refinement, written out again with different weights.

I'd be more careful with the compute framing. The FLOPs are about half of
VGGT's and Pi3's, and about the same as DA3-L's, because the cost of a
reconstruction transformer is global attention depth times width times tokens
squared, and looping does not change that equation. Where the paper's numbers
show a real compute dial is pose precision: AUC@3 moves 14 points between 8
and 16 loops. If you need cameras for a splat or a survey, run 16. If you need
a quick point cloud, run 8, and run it on an 8-step grid rather than stopping
early.

The open question is whether shared weights beat untied ones at full training.
If they do, the next billion-parameter reconstructor should probably be a
hundred-million-parameter one with more loops.

**How I checked.** I ran: a safetensors-header parameter count (40,920-byte
range request), the gate MLPs evaluated in NumPy from about 1.3 MB of
range-fetched weights, a FLOP model of the forward pass, and the averages and
ranks over Tables 1 and 2. I read Figure 8's values from its SVG path
coordinates. I did not run the model or any of the repository's code, so every
accuracy number is the paper's.
