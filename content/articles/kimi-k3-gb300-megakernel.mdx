Two posts went out within forty minutes of each other on 18 October. vLLM's: *"Kimi K3 decode on
NVIDIA GB300 NVL72: 842 tokens/s at batch 1 with DSpark."* SemiAnalysis's: *"423 tok/s/user on the
2.8T Parameter Kimi K3 model via a new upstream vLLM optimization. 1.68x Faster on the Same
Hardware!"*, credited to *"a 12,000 LoC Megakernel"* and *"the future of advanced AI-assisted kernel
generation."*

Two different numbers, two different speedups, and both describe the same pull request. The
engineering write-up is [Hyper Optimizing Kimi K3 in vLLM](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-10-16-kimi-k3-megakernel)
by Benjamin Chislett (NVIDIA). The code is [vllm-project/vllm#61867](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/61867),
merged on 16 October as commit `642c18d`.

Short answer: **the work is real and the mechanism is sound, but neither headline is the clean
number.** 842 is one user on 16 of the rack's 72 GPUs, at an acceptance length set by a benchmark
switch. 423 is a different workload against a different baseline. The cleanest like-for-like figure
is in the post's own table: **1.88x at batch 1 on the same 16 GPUs, without speculation.**

## What one decode step of Kimi K3 does

[Kimi K3](/articles/kimi-k3) is a 2.8T-parameter mixture-of-experts. From
[the C port's accounting](/articles/kimi-k3-in-c): 93 layers, 69 of them Kimi Delta Attention (KDA,
a linear-attention recurrence whose state does not grow with context) and 24 MLA. 92 of them are MoE
layers, routing each token to 16 of 896 experts. The dense trunk is 108.81 GB of bf16 that every token
reads. The experts ship in MXFP4, at 17,547,264 bytes each. One token touches 1,472 experts, 25.83 GB
of them.

A decode step for one user walks all 93 layers for one row. Per layer that is a residual and
attention-residual mix, the input projections, the KDA or MLA core, the output projection, an
all-reduce across the tensor-parallel group, the router, the shared expert, 16 routed experts and a
second all-reduce. Every matmul is one row against a weight matrix: about one FLOP per byte, so the
tensor cores mostly wait on HBM ([decode is memory-bound](/articles/how-llm-inference-works#decode-memory-bound)
covers the general case).

## Why batch 1 on a rack is not only a bandwidth problem

If bytes were the whole story, a step would cost bytes over bandwidth. A GB300 has 8,000 GB/s of
HBM (NVIDIA's figure, Reported), and the PR shards K3 across 8 or 16 GPUs with tensor parallelism. For
16 GPUs:

$$
t_{\text{floor}} = \frac{108.81\ \text{GB} + 1{,}472 \times 17{,}547{,}264\ \text{B}}{16 \times 8{,}000\ \text{GB/s}}
= \frac{134.64\ \text{GB}}{128{,}000\ \text{GB/s}} = 1.05\ \text{ms}
$$

That is a ceiling of about 951 tok/s for one user with no speculation (Reasoned). The precision
matches what the PR reads. Its projection GEMMs reject any weight that is not bf16 or fp16, and its
expert kernel is documented as *"FP4 x MXFP8 FC1/FC2 MMAs"*: packed FP4 weights, activations
quantised to MXFP8 on the fly (Measured, from `gemm/projection.py` and `moe/experts.py`).

Stock vLLM's batch-1 step on those 16 GPUs is 7.38 ms (Reported, in the PR's commit message). That is
7x the floor. The other 6 ms is everything that is not streaming weights:

- **Launches and gaps.** The stock path runs 23 kernels per layer, roughly 2,100 a step (Reasoned,
  23 x 93). Inside a CUDA graph a launch is cheap, but each kernel still ramps up, drains, and leaves
  HBM idle at its edges.
- **Communication.** Two all-reduces per layer over NVLink, about 186 a step (Reasoned). At batch 1
  each moves a few kilobytes, so each is pure latency.
- **Serial small work.** Router scores, top-16 selection, norms, the KDA recurrence: short, dependent
  and bandwidth-idle.

That is why "use the whole NVL72" does not help one user. 72 GPUs' HBM would put the floor near 0.23
ms (Reasoned), but every extra rank in the tensor-parallel group shrinks each GPU's share of the
bytes, while the all-reduces and the serial chain stay the same or grow. The PR only accepts
`tensor_parallel_size` of 8 or 16 and rejects expert parallelism at startup (Measured, from
`runtime/__init__.py`). So "on GB300 NVL72" means a 16-GPU tensor-parallel group inside one NVL72's
NVLink domain, which the post says outright: *"over 840 tokens/s at batch 1 on 16 NVIDIA GB300 GPUs."*

The post's table gives one more clue that the fixed overhead dominates. Subtract the bandwidth floor
from each batch-1 step. Stock leaves about 6.1 ms on TP8 and 6.3 ms on TP16. The fused path leaves
3.07 ms and 2.85 ms (Reasoned). Doubling the GPUs halves the floor and barely moves the rest. **The
fusion's whole win is in the rest: 3.0 to 3.4 ms a step, or 32 to 37 µs a layer.**

## What the fused path changes

The PR adds an opt-in executor behind `--additional-config '{"decode_backend": "latency_specialized"}'`.
Uniform decode steps and DSpark verify steps of up to 64 tokens run as *"one FULL CUDA graph of
persistent CuTe DSL kernels"* (the commit message). Prefill, mixed batches and everything else run the
stock forward. All of it lives under `vllm/models/kimi_k3/nvidia/`.

  caption="The same layer before and after. Stock vLLM spends 130 µs and 23 kernels; the specialized path spends 69 µs and 13, with banded bars marking single kernels fused across operators (vLLM blog, kernel timeline figure)."
/>

Four changes, in the post's order, each visible in the trace:

1. **Shapes tuned per batch.** Small-matrix kernels at batch 1-2, split-K at larger batches, so a
   one-row GEMM still occupies every SM.
2. **Communication fused into compute.** The attention output projection, its all-reduce and the
   following residual are one kernel (`o-proj+AR+res`). Producers write partial sums into
   NVLink-addressable buffers; consumers combine them and continue. The post calls the exchange
   *"fence-less"*; the code calls the buffers mailboxes. At larger batches it switches to a two-stage
   reduce-then-broadcast.
3. **The MoE front split across ranks.** Stock computes the full router scores and latent input
   projection on every GPU. The fused path gives each rank a share, exchanges the results over
   NVLink, and runs the shared expert on a side stream. The routed experts are one persistent kernel
   doing FC1 and FC2, and the `MoE tail` folds the down-projection reduction and the next norm
   together.
4. **L2 prefetch on a third stream.** While the GPU waits on communication, a small kernel walks a
   table of upcoming weight ranges and pulls them into L2, so the next GEMM starts on a warm cache.

<LayerTrace />

The widget redraws both traces from the figure. The fused layer is shorter for three visible
reasons. There are fewer boundaries. The gap between the routed experts and the final all-reduce is
gone, because the all-reduce is now inside the tail. And the MoE front starts at 23 µs instead of
41 µs. The per-layer ratio, 130 over 69, is 1.88x. Multiplied by 93 layers it predicts 12.09 and
6.42 ms a step; the table's measured batch-8 steps are 12.37 and 6.91 ms (Reasoned; the trace is one
KDA layer, and the difference is MLA layers, the embedding, the LM head and sampling).

**It is not one kernel.** The PR adds 30 functions decorated `@cute.kernel` and 219 `@cute.jit`
helpers across 48 Python files (Measured). The trace shows 13 kernels per layer, chained by
programmatic dependent launch inside one CUDA graph. Compare
[Inferact's TPU megakernel](/articles/kimi-k3-tpu-megakernel), which really is one Pallas call for all
93 layers and stages the next layer's weights across the boundary. The GPU path gets most of that
effect with L2 prefetch and PDL instead of one program. So "megakernel" is SemiAnalysis's word, not
vLLM's.

## What DSpark adds

[DSpark](/articles/deepseek-dspark) is a block drafter: a small model proposes several tokens in one
pass, and the target verifies them in one step. The PR supports DSpark verify with 3-7 draft tokens
and rejects other speculative methods (Measured). The headline run uses 7, so each verify step reads
eight rows: the anchor plus seven drafts.

Eight rows route independently. Each layer touches about 120 distinct experts instead of 16, so a
verify step reads about 303 GB, not 135 (Reasoned, with the expected union
$896\,(1-(880/896)^{8}) \approx 120.3$). The trunk is still read once. That is the cost
[LFM2.5-DSpark](/articles/lfm25-dspark) pointed out: verifying a block takes back much of a sparse
model's advantage.

Tokens per second then becomes acceptance length over step time. The post's setup line is explicit:
*"TP16, DSpark with seven draft tokens, synthetic acceptance length of 6, DL=7, ISL=1, OSL=1k, batch
size 1."* So:

- **Specialized:** 6 / 842 = 7.13 ms per verify step (Reasoned).
- **Stock:** 6 / 488 = 12.30 ms per verify step (Reasoned).

Those are almost exactly the table's batch-8 steps without speculation, 6.91 and 12.37 ms. A verify
of eight rows costs what a batch of eight costs, plus about 0.2 ms of drafting on the fused path. That
consistency is a good sign the numbers are what they say they are. It also means **842 is the batch-8
step rate multiplied by a chosen 6.**

"Synthetic" is vLLM's forcing mode. The recipe page, quoted in the TPU article, warns that it *"skips
real verification: never use it to serve users or to evaluate accuracy."* vLLM's own
[DSpark training post](https://vllm.ai/blog/2026-09-15-kimi-k3-dspark) measured this drafter at a
macro-average acceptance of 4.11 across nine domains. At 4.11 the same step times give about 577
against 334 tok/s (Reasoned, holding the step fixed, which the TPU article found it nearly is).

The widget puts every reported step next to its bandwidth floor. Two things stand out. First, the
fused path's share of the floor rises with batch, from 27% at batch 1 to 48% at batch 64 on TP16
(Reasoned), because more rows amortise the same fixed overhead. Second, stock's share at batch 1 is
14%. **The fusion doubled the fraction of the memory bus a single user gets, from about an eighth to
about a quarter.**

## Reconciling the headlines

### 842 against 423

They are different benchmarks.

| | vLLM, 842 | SemiAnalysis, 423 |
|---|---|---|
| workload | 1 input token, 1,000 output tokens | AgentX: replayed coding-agent sessions |
| context | about 1,000 positions | long, multi-turn, with KV-cache reuse |
| metric | decode tok/s, one stream | P90 interactivity, tok/s per user |
| speculation | DSpark, acceptance forced to 6 | not stated |
| GPUs | 16, TP16 | 16, TP16/DCP16 |

AgentX is the harder workload, so 423 is the more realistic number. The post does not say whether
DSpark was on for it. But 423 is above the 256.4 tok/s the fused path reaches at batch 1 with **no**
speculation and a one-token context, and a longer context can only slow a step. So speculation must
have been running with real acceptance, at no less than about 2.9 tokens a step if a step costs at
least the 7.13 ms above (Reasoned, from 403.2 x 7.13 ms).

### 1.68x against 1.8x and 1.5-2x

Each multiplier divides by a different baseline.

- **1.8x (vLLM, AgentX):** 403.2 against 223.7 tok/s per user, both at concurrency 1. The post says
  plainly that this *"compares the optimized run on 16 GB300 GPUs with the earlier vLLM result on 8
  GB300 GPUs."* Twice the GPUs, as a reply on X pointed out (Reported; 403.2 / 223.7 = 1.80).
- **1.68x (SemiAnalysis):** 423 against 251.3, which is 1.683 (Reasoned). vLLM's AgentX chart labels
  251.3 as the old curve's best point and notes it *"uses 32 GPUs"*. SemiAnalysis's chart writes that
  point as `1xTP8/DCP8+3xTP8/DCP8`, which in Dynamo's notation probably reads as one prefill and three
  decode workers of 8 GPUs each, so a decoding user was still on 8. Either way, "the same hardware"
  means the same GPU model, not the same GPU count. vLLM's chart
  has 403.2 where SemiAnalysis's has 423; the two are separate runs two days apart, and I cannot see
  SemiAnalysis's underlying data.
- **1.5-2x (vLLM, headline):** the no-speculation table. On TP16 it holds everywhere: 1.62x to 1.92x.
  On TP8 it does not at larger batches: 1.31x to 1.43x from batch 8 up (Reported).

**The fair comparison is already in the post.** Same 16 GPUs, same one-token context, no
speculation: 256.4 against 136 tok/s, 1.88x. The commit message gives the step times behind it, 3.90
against 7.38 ms (Reported). On TP8 it is 1.58x. Those are the numbers I would plan around.

How does it compare with the TPU kernel? Inferact reported 249 tok/s at batch 1 and 709 at a forced
acceptance length of 6, on 16 TPU v7 chips. The fused GB300 path is 256.4 and 842 on 16 GPUs
(Reported). Against their own bandwidth ceilings the TPU kernel sits at 32% and this one at 27%
(Reasoned; the TPU figure is from that article). Two different designs, the same rough place on the
roofline, and both well short of it.

## Correctness, and how it was checked

Fusing collectives and reordering loads is where silent numerical bugs live. The post is more candid
than most:

> Our audit discovered that the compiler moved activation reads ahead of a synchronization wait. The
> fix restricted that load type to static weights.

And: *"long-context and long-generation evaluations such as MRCR and AIME25 surfaced sensitive
numerical issues in attention and communication kernels that were not reproducible in standalone
kernel tests."*

The PR description reports AIME25, GPQA-Diamond, GSM8K and MRCR v2 for stock TP8 against four fused
configurations, with and without DSpark. A few rows, as score with standard error (Reported; I read
the table through a page renderer that rebuilt split cells, so check the PR before quoting a cell):

| task | stock TP8 | fused, range over 4 configs |
|---|---|---|
| GPQA-Diamond, no spec | 0.924 ± 0.011 | 0.914 to 0.929 |
| AIME25, no spec | 0.867 ± 0.033 | 0.867 to 0.917 |
| GSM8K, no spec | 0.867 ± 0.009 | 0.856 to 0.873 |
| MRCR v2, no spec | 0.282 ± 0.039 | 0.226 to 0.334 (TP8 only) |

Most cells sit within one standard error of stock. The widest gap is MRCR v2 on TP8 at concurrency 8,
0.226 against 0.282, about one combined standard error on 100 samples. The PR also describes greedy
log-probability comparisons against a one-ulp noise floor and decode-against-prefill rescoring.

What the merge does **not** contain: tests. The 50 changed files in `642c18d` include none under
`tests/`, and nothing under `tests/` references the specialized path (Measured). The PR says why: *"I
am happy with the validation, but still unclear how much we want to put into CI."* The post's own
second half argues for automated validation. It has not landed with the first contribution.

## The LoC and the "AI-assisted" claim

**LoC.** The merge commit adds 12,805 lines and deletes 3, across 50 files. 12,731 of those lines are
in `specialized_decode/`; without blank lines, comments and docstrings, 10,469 are code (Measured,
counted with Python's tokenizer). The largest files are `moe/experts.py` at 1,579 lines and
`moe/tail.py` at 1,031. "12,000 LoC" is fair for the whole PR. "One megakernel" is not.

**AI assistance.** The post says *"we used agent-assisted development to build a unified CuTe DSL
decode path."* The PR description says *"AI assistance was used during the creation of this PR"*
(Reported). In the history, all three non-merge commits carry a `Co-Authored-By: Claude Opus 5.5`
trailer. One merge commit is authored by `vllm-agent <claw@inferact.ai>`, and the squash commit lists
both as co-authors (Measured). Neither the post nor the PR says which parts an agent wrote, how many
iterations it took, or what the human did. The post's argument is about **review**, not generation:
agents should deliver tests, evaluations and documented operating conditions so reviewers can judge
the evidence rather than read every line. That is a reasonable ask, and this PR shows both why it
matters and that it is not finished.

## What I'd take from it

- **At batch 1, the overhead is the target, not the bytes.** Stock K3 decode on 16 GB300s spent
  about 6 ms of a 7.4 ms step on things other than streaming weights. Fusing collectives into
  their producers and consumers, and prefetching into L2 during communication, removed about half
  of it.
- **Quote the step, then the multiplier.** 7.13 ms per eight-row verify is a kernel measurement. 842
  multiplies it by 6, chosen by a flag.
- **Check the denominator of every speedup.** 1.8x divides by half the GPUs; 1.68x divides by a
  32-GPU point; 1.88x is the same 16 GPUs.
- **The scope is narrow on purpose.** SM100/SM103 only, TP8 or TP16, batch up to 64, DSpark with
  3-7 drafts or nothing, no LoRA, no expert parallelism (Measured, from the support checks). The post
  says the same pattern is under way for DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash in
  [#61868](https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/61868).
