Every mechanical CAD program you have used sits on a geometry kernel, and almost all of those kernels are closed. Parasolid is Siemens'. ACIS is Dassault's. Autodesk has its own. The one widely used open option, Open CASCADE, is LGPL and has a reputation (fair or not, and one of the replies under the launch post put it in three words: "occ is very bad") for being hard to make behave. So when NVIDIA published [usd-brep](https://github.com/NVIDIA-Omniverse/usd-brep) on 9 October under Apache-2.0, and the people who make [ClassCAD](https://classcad.ch) said on X that it is SMLib, a kernel they have built products on for more than 20 years, I wanted to know what was actually in the repository. Not the announcement. The code.

I expected a thin wrapper over a binary, or a cleaned-up subset. It is neither. The kernel directory alone is 967,172 lines of C++, about 534,000 of them code once comments and blank lines are stripped. There is a C API, roughly 300 pybind11 bindings, an OpenUSD schema for storing exact solids, a Hydra plugin so `usdview` can draw them, a validator, a reader for Open CASCADE's `.brep` files, and an unusually thorough set of docs written for AI coding agents. And within a day a downstream project, [smlibpy](https://github.com/awv-informatik/smlibpy), had shipped patched copies of two kernel files, one of which fixes a bug NVIDIA lists in its own `KNOWN_ISSUES.md` without knowing the cause.

This article is the long version: what a boundary-representation kernel is and why the hard parts are hard, what this one contains, how it puts solids into USD, what smlibpy adds on top, and what is missing next to the commercial kernels. I read both repositories at their release commits; I did not build or run either.

## What a kernel has to represent

A mesh is a list of triangles. It tells you where the surface roughly is and nothing else. Ask a mesh for the radius of a hole and you get an estimate from vertices that happen to lie near it. Ask which faces touch a given edge and you have to infer it from shared vertex indices. Ask it to fillet that edge and it has nothing to work with.

A boundary representation (B-rep) keeps two things apart. **Geometry** is the exact mathematical shape: an infinite plane, a cylinder of radius 18, a NURBS patch with its control net. **Topology** is the bookkeeping that says which bounded piece of which surface is part of the solid and who its neighbours are. A face is a surface trimmed by loops of edges. An edge is a curve trimmed by two vertices. A shell is a connected set of faces, and a closed shell encloses a region of space. The geometry answers "where", the topology answers "what is connected to what", and a kernel's job is to keep the two agreeing while operations tear them apart and stitch them back together.

The AOUSD problem statement that usd-brep vendors puts the split in one table, and the line I would underline is the one about where each kind of geometry comes from: meshes are "derived from exact geometry (or authored directly)", while B-reps are the "authoritative source". Everything downstream (rendering, simulation meshes, toolpaths) is a view regenerated from the B-rep at whatever resolution the consumer needs.

## NURBS, the shape language

Planes, cylinders, cones, spheres and tori cover most machined parts, and SMLib keeps them as analytic surfaces. Everything else, from a car body panel to the blend a fillet leaves behind, is a NURBS: a non-uniform rational B-spline.

A NURBS curve of degree $p$ is defined by control points $P_i$, a weight $w_i$ on each, and a non-decreasing knot vector $U$:

$$
C(u) = \frac{\sum_i N_{i,p}(u)\, w_i\, P_i}{\sum_i N_{i,p}(u)\, w_i}
$$

The $N_{i,p}$ are B-spline basis functions, built by the Cox–de Boor recursion from the knots. Each one is nonzero over only $p+1$ knot spans, which is why moving one control point bends only a local piece of the curve. The weights are what make it *rational*, and rationality is what lets a NURBS represent a circle exactly instead of approximately. The knots set where the pieces join and how smooth the joins are.

The widget below runs the same algorithms a kernel does: Cox–de Boor for the basis (NLib's `N_Basis*` routines in `source/SMLib/src/NL_Basis*.cpp` implement the same recursion) and Boehm's algorithm for knot insertion.

<NurbsCurve />

Three things are worth trying. Drag one control point of the cubic and watch only the coloured bump under it in the basis plot react; that is local support, and it is why a designer can tweak one region of a panel without disturbing the rest. Then load the circle. It is the textbook nine-point quadratic: the corners of a square, with weights of 1 on the points that sit on the circle and $\sqrt{1/2}$ on the corners. Every sample lands on the unit circle to within rounding error. Load the same control points with all weights set to 1 and the curve bulges outward; I computed the worst case at the diagonals as a radius of 1.0607, about 6% too big. A polynomial spline cannot make a circle. A rational one can, and a CAD kernel that has to report a hole as exactly 18 mm needs that.

Finally, insert a knot. A control point appears, the polygon changes, and the curve does not move: the readout reports the largest displacement, which is rounding error. Knot insertion is how kernels split curves at intersection points, make two surfaces' parameterisations compatible before blending them, and subdivide for intersection searches. It is the quiet workhorse under a lot of the operations below.

Surfaces are the same idea in two parameters, $S(u,v)$, with a grid of control points and a knot vector in each direction.

## Trimming, and why the topology needs "uses"

A NURBS surface is always a rectangle in its own $(u,v)$ parameter space. Real faces are not rectangles: the top of a plate has a hole in it, a fillet ends where it runs into a wall. So a face is a surface plus **trimming loops** that say which part of the parameter rectangle is kept. The outer loop runs one way, holes run the other, and the material is on the left as you walk along.

The trimming curves can live in two places, and the choice matters. SMLib stores the authoritative curve of every edge in 3D, and treats the 2D trim curve in each face's parameter space as optional derived data it can rebuild. The repository's B-rep format reference is blunt about it: a UV trim curve is "optional, derived data, not a defining characteristic of the BRep". The UsdSolid proposal says the same thing from the other side: "Trim curves are optional in this Brep model because the edge curve defines the model truth."

Then there is the question of how faces know their neighbours. The usual answer is that each edge points at the two faces it separates. SMLib uses something more general, Kevin Weiler's 1986 **radial edge** data structure, and the USD schema inherits it. Each topological object is stored once, and every time it bounds something higher up, that incidence is a separate *use* object: a faceuse for each side of a face, a loopuse, an edgeuse for each time an edge bounds a face, a vertexuse.

Why bother? Because a solid is not the only thing a kernel has to represent. When two boxes share a face, that face bounds two regions at once, and each side of it is a separate faceuse. When an edge is shared by three or four faces (which is what you see locally along any intersection curve before it has been resolved), the edgeuses around it form a radial list ordered by the right-hand rule. A twin-edge structure that allows exactly two faces per edge cannot represent either case, and booleans spend their whole middle phase in exactly that state.

Click the bore. Its one loop is four edgeuses: the bottom circle, the seam, the top circle and the seam again, in the opposite direction. A cylinder's parameter space is periodic in $u$, so the face needs an edge where $u = 0$ meets $u = 2\pi$, and the face uses that edge twice. The proposal's rules make this mandatory ("A face has a single outer loop (seam edges are required)"), and seams turn out to be one of the places this kernel struggles most, as we will see. The counts on the right come straight from the model: 10 vertices, 15 edges, 7 faces and 9 loops give $10 - 15 + 7 - (9 - 7) = 0$, which the Euler–Poincaré formula turns into genus 1. One hole, as expected. Every edge is used exactly twice, in opposite directions, which is what "closed and consistently oriented" means for a manifold solid.

## Tolerance is where the trouble lives

If geometry were exact, an edge would lie exactly on both of its faces. It never does. The intersection of two curved surfaces has no closed form, so the kernel fits a B-spline to it within some tolerance, and that curve lies on each surface only to within that tolerance. Imported files carry the tolerances of whatever system wrote them. Every comparison in the kernel ("are these two points the same vertex?", "does this curve lie on that face?") is really "are they within tolerance?".

SMLib's defaults are in `source/SMLib/inc/SmMath.h:52-54`:

```cpp
#define SM_ZONE_TOL_3D    1.0e-5                          /* Local Neighborhood Size, used by SmTol::GetZoneTol3d() methods */
#define SM_XSECT_TOL_3D   (2.0*SM_ZONE_TOL_3D)            /* Default Gap and SmTol::GetXSectTol3d Size                      */
#define SM_APPROX_TOL_3D  (SM_ZONE_TOL_3D/2.0)            /* Default SmTol::GetApproxTol3d Size                             */
```

Three numbers, three jobs: a zone tolerance of 1e-5 for "these are the same point", an intersection tolerance of 2e-5 for gaps and loop closure, and an approximation tolerance of 5e-6 for fitting curves. They are in model units, so they mean something different in millimetres than in metres. Individual vertices and edges can carry their own looser tolerances on top of these, which is how a kernel absorbs a sloppy import without rejecting it. The USD side keeps one number per solid, `brep:intersectTol3d`; the cube in the repository's test files stores `0.00002`, which is the 2e-5 default.

This is the part of kernel engineering that never shows up in a feature list. smlibpy's docs give a concrete sense of the scale: faces the kernel fits (B-spline chamfers, shell offsets, intersections of curved faces) "close up only to about 1e-6 to 2e-5 mm", and the volume of such a body depends on that by 1e-7 to 4e-6 of its value. Small, and enough to make two operations that should agree disagree.

All C++ together is 1,096,957 lines. The test fixtures are another 580 files (STEP-derived solids, fillet and drop-curve cases, Open CASCADE `.brep` files, and 126 USD files for the validator, most of them deliberately broken in one way each). The C++ regression harness `prog_test` runs 17 suites from topology and booleans through fillets, sweeps, surface-surface intersection and stitching, and I counted about 530 Python test functions across the repository, 400 of them in `test_omni_solid.py`.

The layering is clean once you see it:

- **`SMLib`** is the kernel proper: the topology classes (`SmBrep`, `SmRegion`, `SmShell`, `SmFace`, `SmLoop`, `SmEdge`, `SmVertex` and their `*use` classes), the geometry classes, and the algorithms. The `NL_*` files are a lower NURBS library underneath it. The biggest files say where the effort went: `SmBrep.cpp` (38,863 lines), `SmSurface.cpp` (35,380), `SmCurve.cpp` (27,725), the polygon library `SmPoly.cpp` (27,402), `NL_SrfAdv.cpp` (23,322), `SmFace.cpp` (21,752).
- **`SM_API`** is a C-style wrapper (`SmApi*` functions) over the kernel, with headers for primitives, booleans, curves, surfaces, fillets, intersectors, trimmed surfaces, queries and healing.
- **`SM_API_USD`**, `BREP_SM_USD` and `BREP_USD_DATA` convert between kernel solids and the USD schema.
- **`SmPyLib`** builds the Python module `_omni_solid` with pybind11 2.11.1; the pip package `usd-brep` wraps it as `usd_brep` with submodules (`primitives`, `booleans`, `sweeps`, `fillets`, `usd`, …).
- **`HYDRA_USD_BREP`** is a Hydra imaging plugin that tessellates BrepArray prims on the fly so `usdview` can show them; **`OCCT_BREP_IMPORT`/`EXPORT`** read and write Open CASCADE's `.brep` format by parsing it directly, without linking Open CASCADE.

Where does SMLib come from? The repository never says. Every source file carries a 2026 NVIDIA SPDX header and the README says "Copyright (c) 2020-2026, NVIDIA". The rest I pieced together. An archived 2019 copy of smlib.com, the site of Solid Modeling Solutions, describes "SMLib™ Our flagship product built upon TSNLib™ provides fully functional non-manifold topological structure and solid modeling functionality including the ability to perform NURBS boolean operations, fillets, etc.", stacked on GSNLib and on "NLib™ A comprehensive BSpline NURBs library". The `NL_` prefix and the `NL_KNOTVECTOR` types in the source match that stack, and AWV Informatik says it has used SMLib for 20 years. I could not find how or when the code moved to NVIDIA, so I won't guess.

The same archived page lists "Data Translators" for IGES, STEP, SAT, VDAFS, OpenNURBS and Parasolid XT. None of those are in this release. I come back to that below.

## A Boolean, traced through the code

Booleans are the operation everyone uses and nobody sees. The Python call is one line:

```python
import _omni_solid as sm
box = sm.create_box((0, 0, 0), 10, 10, 10)
sph = sm.create_sphere((5, 5, 5), 4)
diff = sm.boolean_difference(box.copy(), sph.copy())   # inputs are consumed; copy to keep them
```

`SmApiBrep.cpp:231-238` dispatches on whether both inputs are manifold solids, to `SmMerge::ManifoldBoolean` or `NonManifoldBoolean`. The manifold path then calls `SmTopologyIntersector::IntersectInsertRelate()` (`SmTopologyIntersector.cpp:1092`), and its comments lay out the phases:

1. **Intersect topology** (`:1184`): find every place the two solids' vertices, edges and faces meet, including surface-surface intersection curves. Results go into a scratch structure of *proto-topology* (proto-vertices, proto-edges) rather than into the solids.
2. **Resolve intersections** (`:1208`): merge near-coincident proto-vertices, decide which overlapping proto-edges are one edge. The comment after each of the first two phases reads "If that didn't work, quit before we mess up the Breps."
3. **Imprint intersections** (`:1232`), with the comment "now we'll modify the Breps": split faces and edges along the resolved curves.
4. **Relate** (`:1263`): record which new pieces of each solid correspond, so `SmMerge` can classify each face as inside or outside the other solid and keep or delete it by operation.

That separation (find everything, decide everything, only then mutate) is what you want in a kernel, because a half-imprinted solid is unrecoverable. The decide phase is also where the judgement calls live, and one of them is right there in the source, at `SmTopologyIntersector.cpp:1145-1158`:

```cpp
// Capture Distance: simply using entities' tolerances will miss intersections
// that could possibly go into the same ProtoVertex.  We should collect candidates
// into the same PVs, and let Resolve() figure it out, making either one or more
// SmVerttices out of each PV.
// This is a concept in progress.  We currently check the tolerance value in
// the TopologyIntersector, which ultimately comes from the tolerance in the Breps. [B669]
// We might want to look at Bounding Box sizes, or min Edge length, or such.
// But currently this works well.
double dCaptureDistance = 0.01;

// Make sure it's not smaller than our tolerance value.
double dTopoIntTol = this->GetThisApproxTol3d();
if ( dCaptureDistance < dTopoIntTol * 2000 )
  { dCaptureDistance = dTopoIntTol * 2000; }
```

With the default approximation tolerance of 5e-6, the floor is 2000 × 5e-6 = 0.01, so the two numbers coincide. It is an absolute distance in model units: a hundredth of a millimetre for a part modelled in millimetres, a centimetre for one modelled in metres. I like that this is visible. Every kernel has constants like this; in a closed one you find them by bisecting failures.

The repository's own guide for booleans adds the practical advice: avoid tangent or coincident surfaces ("Offset one body by a tiny amount if needed"), watch for features thinner than the tolerance, and check `is_manifold_solid()` after each step. Those are the classic failure modes of every kernel, open or not.

## Solids inside USD

The other half of the release is the part only NVIDIA would have built. OpenUSD, the scene format Omniverse is built on, has meshes, subdivision surfaces and untrimmed NURBS patches. It has no solids. When CAD data enters USD today it is tessellated at the door, and the exact geometry stays behind in a STEP file.

usd-brep adds a schema, `omniSolid`, with one concrete prim type, `BrepArray`, and a family of applied API schemas for geometry (`BrepSurfaceNurbAPI`, `BrepSurfacePlaneAPI`, `BrepSurfaceCylinderAPI`, cone, sphere, torus; `BrepCurve3dNurbAPI`, line, circle, ellipse; `BrepCurveUvNurbAPI` for trim curves). The whole radial-edge structure is flattened into parallel arrays of indices: `region:type`, `shell:faceuseCount`, `faceuse:faceIndex`, `face:loopCount`, `loop:edgeuseCount`, `edgeuse:edgeIndex`, `edgeuse:nextRadialEUIndex`, and so on. The schema's documentation explains how it compresses the in-memory structure: "There are no attributes for Vertexuses and Loopuses", and each runtime pair of mated edgeuses becomes one array entry. The test cube shows the result: 2 regions, 2 shells of 6 faceuses each, 6 loops of 4 edgeuses each, and 24 edgeuse records for 12 edges.

Geometry is packed by type. All the NURBS surfaces of every face sit in one set of arrays (`brep:surface:nurb:controlVertices`, `uKnots`, `vKnots`, `weights`, …) in double precision; all the planes in another. Several solids can share one prim, their entries laid end to end.

Why so flat? The proposal, which usd-brep vendors unmodified, reports the designs the working group tried first. One prim per surface and curve "was an untenable design": a car surface model came to about 500 B-reps with 100 geometry prims each, 50,000 prims for one model. Moving geometry into one prim per B-rep "improved performance and shrunk file size by 1/3", and the array design packs further. Treating B-reps as an opaque blob (an embedded STEP file, say) was rejected because it gives up per-face materials, USD composition and overrides.

Some limits are stated plainly. The schema is for *using* CAD data in USD (viewing, measuring, clash detection, re-tessellation), not editing it on the stage; the proposal says live editing would need schemas for "the 11 topology and use objects". The AOUSD proposals (OpenUSD-proposals pull requests 108 and 109, opened 20 May 2026) are still marked Draft, which is why NVIDIA ships the schema under its own `omniSolid` name and keeps the proposal's `prelimUsdSolid` copy beside it for reference. And the round trip is lossy in documented ways: per-entity tolerances "collapse to one per-BRep value", edge curves are exported as NURBS, and analytic faces lose their UV trims. NVIDIA's own asset-generation guide tells agents not to round-trip through `BrepArray` at all when the goal is a mesh.

The supporting tools are solid. The validator (`tools/brep_validator`, also available as `pip install "usd-brep[validation]"`) checks the topology arrays rule by rule, with a broken fixture file per rule. The Hydra plugin tessellates once per prim, in parallel across prims, and never writes meshes back to the stage. `usd.heal_file` heals every BrepArray in a composed stage and writes it back with layers, references and instancing intact.

## The Python layer, and docs written for agents

The stable Python module is broad. The operation index lists 16 primitive constructors, 13 boolean variants (including CSG trees and 2D booleans), 13 sweeps, 9 fillet and chamfer calls, 22 curve calls, 11 surface constructors, offsets, stitching, 8 intersectors, cut, project and trim, tessellation, polygon-mesh booleans, healing and the USD import and export calls. Topology is walkable from Python: `face.edges()`, `edge.faces()`, `vertex.point()`, `face.normal_at_uv(u, v)`.

Two conventions deserve a warning before you write code against it. Booleans *consume* their inputs ("Ownership of the named inputs transfers to the operation; use only the returned Brep afterward"), so you `.copy()` anything you want to keep. And the kernel is not thread-safe: the concurrency guide says the process-global context "holds unlocked mutable shared state", operations run single-threaded in the shipped build, and the unit of concurrency is the process. One worker process per job.

The pip wheel is `usd-brep` 1.0.0, for CPython 3.12 only, about 90 MB on Linux x86-64 because it bundles its own OpenUSD 25.11 runtime. Building from source uses NVIDIA's `repo`/premake/Packman tooling; on macOS the README notes that the premake package "is not on the public Packman server", and someone in the launch thread asked about exactly that.

Then there are the agent docs. `AGENTS.md` is 19 KB, and `.agents/` holds 20 per-operation guides, an error-code decoder and 19 skills ("smlib-kernel-booleans", "smlib-kernel-healing", "usd-brep-schema", "text-to-3d-asset-generation" and so on). They are good. The B-rep format reference alone is 1,211 lines and explains invariants (radial list order, which edgeuse carries the trim curve, how seams at poles go wrong) that I have never seen written down for any kernel. NVIDIA clearly expects a lot of the code that calls this library to be written by models.

The part I found most interesting is not the API. It is `third_party/patches/`. smlibpy compiles the kernel from the usd-brep submodule as published, except for two files it replaces with fixed copies, and its README documents every change. One fixes a write into freed memory "at the end of every Boolean", which they found with AddressSanitizer on `Box(40, 30, 20) - Cylinder(5, 40)`. Another stops a vertex-gluing step from deleting curves another solid still uses; they say fillet removal on an imported valve body crashed the process in 3 of 3 runs before the fix.

The third is the one I checked line by line, because it explains an item in NVIDIA's own `KNOWN_ISSUES.md`: "`remove_fillet` removes nothing and reports success", workaround "none". Here is `SmVertex::GetVertexuseOfFace` upstream, at `source/SMLib/src/SmVertex.cpp:256-267`:

```cpp
// Remove Composite
//      // when VU connects to TgtFace (or one of its member faces)
//      if(   (!bIsComposite && pThisFace == pFace)
//         || ( bIsComposite && ((SmCFace*)pFace)->ContainsFace(pThisFace)))
        {
          // remember any found Vertexuses
          pRet = pVU;

          // Use the first Vertexuse found on an upward Faceuse.
          if ( pThisFace->GetUpwardFaceuse() == pFU )
            { break; }
        }
```

The function is supposed to find the vertex's use on a given face. Someone removing support for composite faces commented out the whole condition, including the plain `pThisFace == pFace` test, and left the braces. The function now returns the use on whichever face comes first, whatever face you asked for. smlibpy restores the face test here and in `GetLoopOfFace`. Their measurement: kernel fillet removal on a box with four rounded edges now gives back 6 faces and a volume of 24000.0, where before it removed nothing and left 10 faces.

The comment `// Remove Composite` appears 458 times across 50 files of the kernel. I did not audit the others. But it suggests that at least part of preparing SMLib for release was stripping a feature by commenting code out, and that the test suite did not catch every place that went wrong. Finding that within a day of release is what an open kernel is for.

smlibpy also replaces some of the kernel's numerics instead of patching them. Its docs say the kernel's mass-property integration "controls its steps by the area alone", so "a volume can be 300 times worse than the accuracy asked for", and that `SmCurve::Length` counts a span starting with a zero tangent as zero (the outline of a cylinder came out with 30 mm top and bottom lines instead of 60). smlibpy integrates volumes and inertia itself, over the trim curves by Green's theorem with adaptive Gauss–Kronrod quadrature, in `src/cpp/measure.cpp`. NVIDIA's known-issues file independently reports area errors "up to about 1.4% at the default `relative_accuracy` of 1e-3" on faces bounded by circles, so the two accounts agree that the kernel's integrals need care. I did not reproduce either number.

Seams come up again in smlibpy's table of kernel behaviour it works around: NX and other systems write cylindrical and spherical faces without the seam edge SMLib requires, so smlibpy inserts one on STEP import, and turns the surface's frame so the seam lies where the angle parameter starts. NVIDIA's known-issues list has ten separate bullets about healing faces on closed surfaces, seams and poles. If you take one thing from the topology explorer, take the bore's doubled seam edge: it is ordinary in theory and a steady source of bugs in practice.

## Where agents come in

AWV's pitch for smlibpy is agents: "It comes with an MCP server, so your agent can build parts too." The server (`smlibpy-mcp`) gives a model a `run_script` tool that executes smlibpy Python with variables kept between calls, a `snapshot` tool that renders the model (drawings, sections, x-ray, highlighted selections, before-and-after diffs), `inspect`, checkpoints, and per-function guides. The skill has about 140 reference pages, and the repository's `ai/training/` directory holds the scripts agents ran while writing them.

I think the case for an open kernel here is real but narrower than the replies suggested. Language models are bad at geometry and decent at writing code that calls a geometry library. What makes the loop work is that a B-rep can answer questions precisely: the volume is 157,979.5 cubic millimetres (in their demo), the selection picked 8 circular edges, the solid is manifold. A mesh pipeline cannot give the model that kind of feedback. Agents that write OpenSCAD or Blender scripts already exist (the [WorldClaw](/articles/worldclaw) paper builds whole terrains that way), but they produce meshes, not parts you can send to a machine shop as STEP. An Apache-licensed kernel removes the per-seat licence question for anyone who wants to host such a server, ship it inside a product, or run thousands of copies as an environment to train CAD agents against, which one reply suggested.

Two caveats from the code. The kernel crashes; smlibpy runs risky operations in a forked copy with a timeout (`smlibpy.defeature`'s guard) and rebuilds sessions from their scripts after a crash, and any hosted agent tool will need the same. And one process per concurrent job is the rule, so a server for many agents is a pool of worker processes, not threads.

## What is missing next to Parasolid, ACIS and Open CASCADE

Plainly, from what I could check in the code and docs:

- **No STEP or IGES translator.** usd-brep reads and writes USD and Open CASCADE `.brep`. SMS sold translators for IGES, STEP, SAT, VDAFS, OpenNURBS and Parasolid XT, and none were released. smlibpy wrote its own STEP reader and writer in Python and C++ and checks its exports by reading them back with Open CASCADE. Open CASCADE ships STEP and IGES translators itself. For most real CAD work, interchange is the first thing you need.
- **Single-threaded, not thread-safe.** Some parallel code paths exist but are compiled out; scale with processes.
- **Known bugs in the core operations.** Healing that reports success when it failed, ten problems with seams and poles, inside-out triangles next to fillets, inaccurate areas on circle-bounded faces, a crash on malformed BrepArray topology. To NVIDIA's credit, all of this is written down.
- **No outside contributions.** The README says "This project is currently not accepting contributions"; issues are welcome. smlibpy's patches therefore live in smlibpy.
- **A kernel, not a CAD system.** There is no feature history, sketch constraint solver or assembly model. smlibpy sends people who want parametric rebuilds to the ClassCAD MCP. Kernels generally leave history and constraints to the application above them, so this is a scope note more than a gap.

What I could not check: robustness and speed against Parasolid, ACIS or Open CASCADE on the same models. Nothing in either repository compares them, and I did not run anything. One reply under the launch post said that on a personal set of difficult chamfer tests, "Occt passes many more cases than Smlib". That is one person's anecdote, and the honest status is that nobody has published a comparison yet. Neither could I verify how NVIDIA acquired SMLib or how much of the commercial product's history survives in this snapshot (the repository is a single release commit).

The licence comparison is easy, though. Open CASCADE is LGPL 2.1 with an exception; usd-brep is Apache-2.0, apart from MIT-licensed bootstrap scripts and the usual third-party notices (OpenUSD, oneTBB, pybind11 and so on). For a company that wants to modify the kernel and ship it inside a closed product or a hosted service, that difference matters more than any benchmark.

## What I would do with it

If you want to model parts from Python today, start with smlibpy, not the raw bindings: `pip install smlibpy` gives wheels for Python 3.10 to 3.14 on Linux, macOS and Windows at 5 to 7 MB, with the two kernel fixes compiled in and the integrals done properly. If you are in the USD world and need exact solids in a stage, usd-brep's schema, validator and Hydra plugin are the reason to look, and they are the part of this release with no open equivalent. If you are building an agent that designs parts, copy smlibpy's structure: scripts in a separate process, a renderer the model can look through, and a numeric check after every step.

And if you work on geometry kernels, read `.agents/skills/smlib-brep-model/references/brep-format.md`. It is the best description of a production B-rep's invariants I have seen in public, and now it comes with the million lines it describes.
