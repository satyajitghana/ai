Every LiDAR-inertial paper has a table where it wins. The tables never agree, because no two of
them ran the same data through the same evaluation on the same machine. I wanted one table where
they had. So I took seventeen open systems, stripped ROS out of every one of them, fed them the
same converted scans and the same IMU samples, scored them with one script, and timed them on
the same four pinned cores of one 28-core box.

The short answer, by use case:

- **Indoor handheld (Hilti basement, 38 m):** Voxel-SLAM at 3.2 cm, then PV-LIO at 4.0 cm. If you
  need speed, Super-LIO gets 15.2 cm at 0.58 ms a scan.
- **Stairs (Hilti attic to gallery, 117 m, two stairwells):** Voxel-SLAM, alone, at 8.2 cm. The next
  best is 64 cm, and that needed GenZ-LIO's own config for this exact recording.
- **Long outdoor loop (Newer College park, 2.4 km, 26 minutes):** a seven-way tie between 24 and
  31 cm. Pick Super-LIO: 26.3 cm at 7.8 ms a scan and 191 MB, against Voxel-SLAM's 24.2 cm at
  24.8 ms and 8.5 GB.

The rest of this page is how I got there, what broke, and the places where the numbers say
something less tidy than that list.

## The field

The roster is everything I could find that is open, recent enough to matter, and runs on a CPU:

- the FAST-LIO2 lineage: [FAST-LIO2](/articles/fast-lio2-lidar-inertial-odometry) itself,
  Faster-LIO, Point-LIO, Super-LIO, [PV-LIO](/articles/pv-lio), [Voxel-SLAM](/articles/voxel-slam)
  (odometry only and with its loop closure and global bundle adjustment), and the estimator from
  [α-LiDAR](/articles/alpha-lidar) on a sensor with no motor;
- the direct and GICP family: DLIO, iG-LIO, GenZ-LIO, BIEVR-LIO, SuperOdom and
  [FAR-LIO](/articles/far-lio), whose registration normally runs on a GPU and here ran on its CPU
  path;
- PRBonn's line: KISS-ICP and KISS-SLAM (LiDAR only, the IMU unused), RKO-LIO, and GLIM on its CPU
  modules.

Faster-LIO and Point-LIO never built. Their ports compile against a shim that stands in for the ROS
headers, and both tripped over headers that ROS used to pull in for free: Point-LIO's
`preprocess.h` uses `deque` without including it, and Faster-LIO failed at the link. They are in
the table as failed rather than quietly left out.

## One harness, no ROS

The rule from the start was that nothing in the measurement path runs ROS: no RoboStack, no
Noetic, no ROS 2 bag playback. The earlier [FAST-LIO2 runs](/articles/fast-lio2-lidar-inertial-odometry)
went through ROS 2 bag playback at half speed, and that adds a timing layer I can't account for.
Here each bag is read once with the pure-Python `rosbags` library and written out as plain files:

```text
lio-data/<seq>/
  imu.csv        t, ax, ay, az, gx, gy, gz        raw IMU frame, absolute seconds
  scans.csv      idx, t_start, t_end, n, file
  scans/*.bin    float32 x, y, z, intensity, dt   raw LiDAR frame, dt since t_start
  gt.tum         the reference, in its own body frame
  meta.json      T_imu_lidar, T_gt_imu, sensor, licence
```

Each system then gets a thin C++ driver that reads those files, hands IMU samples and scans to the
estimator in timestamp order (every IMU sample up to a scan's end before the scan, the order a bag
stamped at scan end gives), times the estimator's own work per scan, and writes a TUM pose per
scan plus the map it built. The estimators themselves are upstream's code at a pinned commit, each
with its own shipped config for the sensor. Where a system ships no config for a Hesai or an
Ouster, it got its generic spinning-LiDAR one with only the sensor facts changed (line count,
extrinsic, time unit), and the row says so. Tuned settings are separate rows: "0.25 m map" for
FAST-LIO2, "per-sequence config" for GenZ-LIO, and so on. The untuned row always stays.

The evaluation is one script for everyone. Each pose is moved into the reference's body frame,
the reference is interpolated at the pose stamps, positions are aligned with SE(3) Umeyama without
scale, and ATE is the RMSE after that. RPE is evo's, over 1 m of travelled path. A run is
**diverged** if any pose is non-finite, if its worst error passes 25% of the path length (10 m at
least), or if it stops before covering 80% of the reference. It is **failed** if it never produced
ten poses.

Two passes per system. The accuracy pass runs everything in parallel, four threads each. The
timing pass runs one system at a time on `taskset -c 0-3` with four threads, so the milliseconds
in the table are not polluted by neighbours. The park sequence skipped the timing pass (26 minutes
a run, seventeen systems), so its timings come from the parallel pass and are marked.

Before trusting any of it I ran the harness against itself. `gtreplay` is a fake system that
replays the reference trajectory as its estimate: it scores exactly 0 on all four sequences. Its
twin adds a yaw drift of 1° a minute, and the same script reports 23 cm on the 74-second basement
and 12.2 m on the 26-minute park. A degree a minute is a gentle drift. Over 2.4 km it costs twelve
metres.

## The four sequences

| sequence | sensor | length | why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hilti 2022 `exp14_basement_2` | Hesai PandarXT-32 | 38 m, 74 s | a small indoor walk; the [FAST-LIO2](/articles/fast-lio2-lidar-inertial-odometry) article's basement |
| Hilti 2022 `exp16_attic_to_upper_gallery_2` | Hesai PandarXT-32 | 117 m, 200 s | down one stairwell about 8 m and up another, in the Sheldonian |
| Oxford Spires `keble-college-03` | Hesai QT64 | 283 m, 286 s | an outdoor walk round college quads |
| Newer College 2021 `park` | Ouster OS0-128 | 2.4 km, 26 min | a long outdoor loop that ends 0.4 m from where it began |

All four have a reference measured independently of the systems under test; the
[Hilti dataset article](/articles/hilti-slam-dataset) covers how Hilti's was made. The stair sequence is the reason this article exists. Of the Hilti 2022 recordings
only three have a dense public 6-DoF reference (exp14, exp16, exp18), and exp16 is the one with
stairs.

## Indoors: two systems in a class of their own

The basement is short and slow, and most systems handle it. What separates them is centimetres.
Voxel-SLAM tracks to 3.2 cm and PV-LIO to 4.0 cm. Both keep planes in their maps rather than raw
points, and so does α-LiDAR's estimator, which comes from the same line as PV-LIO. GenZ-LIO
matches them at 3.5 cm, but only with the config its authors wrote for this exact recording; with
its default Hesai config it is 15.6 cm. α-LiDAR's estimator (4.4 cm, no motor fitted) and BIEVR-LIO
(4.9 cm) are close behind.

FAST-LIO2 as shipped manages 26.5 cm, and the reason is its map. Its default keeps one point per
0.5 m voxel, which is fine outdoors and too coarse for a basement whose rooms are a few metres
across. The same build with a 0.25 m map scores 11.5 cm. Super-LIO, which keeps FAST-LIO2's filter
and swaps the map for a hashed one, gets 15.2 cm, and it does so in 0.58 ms a scan, which is half
of FAST-LIO2's 1.2 ms and a twentieth of Voxel-SLAM's 11.2 ms.

FAR-LIO's shipped config uses 4 m voxels, built for a race car on a racetrack. In a basement that
is 4.56 m of error. With 1 m voxels it drops to 28 cm. LiDAR-only KISS-ICP drifts 3.4 m; without an
IMU a handheld sweep is a hard thing to deskew.

## The stairs

This is the sequence that sorts the field. Voxel-SLAM tracks the whole 200 seconds to 8.2 cm, and
its odometry alone, without the loop closure, gets 9.6 cm. Nothing else finishes under 60 cm.

- **Diverged:** FAST-LIO2 in all three configurations, Super-LIO, FAR-LIO with its shipped voxels,
  GenZ-LIO with its default config, PV-LIO with two of its three configs, KISS-ICP, KISS-SLAM and
  iG-LIO.
- **Stopped:** α-LiDAR's estimator aborted 29.5 s in, and RKO-LIO stopped writing poses at 24 s.
- **Finished, badly:** GenZ-LIO with its per-sequence config at 64 cm, BIEVR-LIO at 97 cm, SuperOdom
  at 1.5 m, PV-LIO's default at 1.7 m, GLIM at 4.3 m and DLIO at 6.3 m.

Reading the aligned trajectories, every FAST-LIO-lineage failure happens in a stairwell. FAST-LIO2's
0.25 m variant leaves the reference about 48 s in, during the first descent; Super-LIO goes near
the bottom of that stairwell; FAST-LIO2 at its shipped settings survives the first stairwell and
comes apart around 140 s, as the walk climbs the second. Once it goes, it goes completely: the
estimate stops matching anything and integrates the IMU into a straight line across kilometres,
which is what the 824 m ATE in its row is.

Why Voxel-SLAM survives is a fair question, and I don't have a measured answer. The plausible one
is its map: an adaptive voxel map that keeps planes at several sizes, so a stairwell's few metres
of wall still give it well-conditioned planes, where a fixed-size point map has either too few
points per voxel or voxels larger than the wall. PV-LIO keeps planes too and finished, at 1.7 m,
so planes alone are not the whole story. The [Voxel-SLAM article](/articles/voxel-slam) goes
through its map and its bundle adjustment in detail.

## The long loop

On 2.4 km of park, the field bunches up. Voxel-SLAM 24.2 cm, Super-LIO 26.3, α-LiDAR's estimator
27.1, GenZ-LIO with its per-sequence config 27.9, FAST-LIO2 29.5, PV-LIO 30.0, DLIO 30.9. That is
seven systems inside 7 cm over a walk of more than two kilometres. FAR-LIO lands at 37 cm, RKO-LIO
at 72 cm and SuperOdom at 80 cm.

Then the cost column decides it. Super-LIO runs at 7.8 ms a scan and peaks at 191 MB. Voxel-SLAM
needs 24.8 ms and 8.5 GB; PV-LIO, α-LiDAR and GenZ-LIO's default each pass 10 GB on this sequence,
because their maps grow with the walk. On a robot with a fixed memory budget that ends the
discussion. Those timings come from the parallel pass, with other runs sharing the box, so read
them as relative, not absolute.

The surprise is how little loop closure buys here. Voxel-SLAM with its loop closure and global
bundle adjustment scores 24.2 cm and its odometry alone scores 24.7 cm, a 5 mm difference over the
whole loop. On Oxford Spires the same switch halves its error (6.6 cm to 3.2 cm), so the machinery
works. On the park the odometry simply didn't drift far enough for closing the loop to matter much.
KISS-SLAM's loop closer, for what it is worth, never changed a number on the basement or Spires:
its scores there are identical to KISS-ICP's.

Two systems did not finish the park at all. GLIM aborted with a `std::length_error` in its deskewing
step, and iG-LIO hit a NaN rotation 19 frames in, as it also did on Spires.

## What the maps say

<MapViewer />

The viewer holds, for each sequence, the best map, FAST-LIO2's, the reference replay's (the scans
placed with the ground-truth poses) and the best from another family. They share one frame, so
switching between them shows the difference between two estimates rather than a jump between two
coordinate systems.

The harness also measures how thick a wall is: the median RMS distance to a fitted plane over 1 m
patches of wall. I expected the reference replay to set the floor on that number, and it doesn't.
On the basement it scores 43 mm, while PV-LIO's own map scores 24.7 mm and Voxel-SLAM's 26.8 mm.
The reference is sampled about ten times a second and interpolated between samples, so small
errors in it smear a wall across a few centimetres. A good odometry is consistent with itself at a
finer scale than that. So the wall number measures how self-consistent a map is, not how correct,
and it should be read beside ATE, never instead of it.

## Against the earlier runs

The FAST-LIO2 article measured FAST-LIO2 and Super-LIO on a different machine (an RTX 3090 box)
through ROS 2 bag playback, with the points pre-moved into a levelled IMU frame. There, FAST-LIO2
scored 7.2 cm on Spires and 50 cm on the basement, and Super-LIO 8.0 cm and 9.3 cm. Here, with
raw-frame input and each system's shipped config, FAST-LIO2 is 6.4 cm and 26.5 cm and Super-LIO
8.6 cm and 15.2 cm. Spires agrees within a centimetre. The basement does not, and the difference is
the pipeline (blind radius, input frame, playback), not the estimators. It is the best argument I
have for one harness.

## What I would use

For a handheld scanner that goes indoors and up and down stairs, Voxel-SLAM, and it isn't close.
It was the only system to keep the stair sequence under 10 cm, and it won the basement and the
park and came second on Spires by 2 mm. It is also slow (11 to 56 ms a scan on the serial pass) and
hungry (up to 8.5 GB on the park), so it is a mapping tool more than a real-time odometry on a
small computer.

For a robot that needs odometry on a budget, Super-LIO. It was within 6 cm of the best on Spires
and 2.1 cm on the park, the fastest system on every sequence, and under 200 MB everywhere. It also
diverged on the stairs, with the rest of the FAST-LIO lineage, so I would not put it on a platform
that climbs.

FAST-LIO2 is still a sound baseline outdoors (6.4 cm on Spires, 29.5 cm on the park), but its shipped
0.5 m map is wrong for rooms, and it fails on stairs. If you run it indoors, halve the map voxel.

I would skip FAR-LIO on a CPU for handheld work, iG-LIO until its NaN is fixed, and KISS-ICP
anywhere an IMU is available.

The ports, the converter, the evaluation and every raw RESULT line are in the shootout's working
files; the systems' own pages ([PV-LIO](/articles/pv-lio), [Voxel-SLAM](/articles/voxel-slam),
[α-LiDAR](/articles/alpha-lidar), [FAST-LIO2](/articles/fast-lio2-lidar-inertial-odometry))
explain each method in depth and use the same numbers.
