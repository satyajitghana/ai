A phone already carries the two sensors a metric map needs: a camera and an IMU. What it does
not carry is a way to turn them into a dense map whose walls sit where the walls are, to the
centimetre, without a LiDAR. Accord (Mahmoud et al., BE2R Lab at ITMO,
[arXiv 2611.25279](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25279)) is a system that tries, and its code is
GPL-3.0 at [be2rlab/ACCORD](https://github.com/be2rlab/ACCORD). A post by @wildmindai put it as
"Turn any phone, drone/action camera into a real 3D scanner", building "accurate metric maps live
at 18fps, no tuning required". Each of those words is checkable, and most of them hold with a
condition attached.

The idea fits in a sentence. The filter's poses are metric, so Accord takes its metres from the
filter, twice: once by anchoring each window of depth to points triangulated under those poses,
and once more by making every pair of images that see one surface agree about how far away it
is. Most of this article is about why the second step is needed and why it can be measured at
all.

## A dense model with no metres, a filter with no surfaces

Two families of methods each solve half the problem. Visual-inertial odometry (VIO) gets metric
motion from the accelerometer, and gravity's direction with it, but its map is a few hundred
sparse landmarks. Feed-forward geometry models (DUSt3R, MASt3R, VGGT, Depth Anything 3) predict
dense surfaces from images in one pass, but not their metric scale. SLAM systems built on those
models align submaps up to a similarity or a projective transform, and are scored after a
similarity fit to the reference, which forgives exactly the error that matters here.

The paper does the arithmetic on why it matters. At three metres, a two-percent scale error is
six centimetres, already outside the five-centimetre tolerance of the F-score the paper reports.
Scale errors of a few percent are what a depth model leaves even when it is told the camera
poses. They hide under a similarity alignment and show up as soon as a map is placed by its own
trajectory, which is how every number in this paper is scored.

Accord builds on DAVIO, the same lab's earlier system, which already conditions Depth Anything 3
(DA3) on the poses of a VIO filter. The new parts are the two scale corrections and a small
change to what the depth model sees.

## Three processes, none waiting for another

The system runs as three processes.

- **Tracking, on the CPU.** An unmodified OpenVINS filter tracks the camera from the images and
  the IMU samples, every frame. Its pose stream is metric and the map never writes back to it.
- **Mapping, on one GPU worker.** Every 0.5 s a frame becomes a keyframe. Five keyframes make a
  window, and consecutive windows share two. DA3 gets the window's images, intrinsics and the
  filter's poses, and returns a depth map and a confidence map per image. Each window becomes a
  submap: a node in a factor graph with a pose and a log-scale, constrained to its neighbours by
  the filter's relative pose and to older submaps by loop closures.
- **Scale correction, in its own process.** The agreement between views, described below. The
  mapper hands it depth and graph nodes and takes whatever corrections it has published; it never
  waits.

/>

A pixel $u$ of image $i$ in window $j$ lands in the map at

$$
p_M = T_{MS_j}\, T_{S_jC_i}\; e^{\lambda_j - o_j - c_i}\; \alpha_j\, s_{ji}\, d_{ji}(u)\, K^{-1}\tilde u .
$$

Read it right to left. $d_{ji}(u)$ is DA3's depth and $K^{-1}\tilde u$ the viewing ray. DA3 also
returns its own estimate of the cameras, and the similarity that best maps those camera centres
onto the filter's gives the window's scale $\alpha_j$. $s_{ji}$ is a relative scale per image,
solved inside the window from matched keypoints. $\lambda_j$ is the window's log-scale in the
graph, held near zero by a tight prior. The two terms the paper adds are $o_j$, the window's
metric anchor, and $c_i$, the image's correction. The poses are already metric, so every scale
acts only along the viewing rays.

After that comes a depth filter: drop the least confident fifth of each image, drop pixels where
the depth in a 3x3 neighbourhood varies by more than 15% (the flying pixels between an object and
the wall behind it), and keep only pixels that agree with another image of the same window.

## The anchor: metres from the filter's own keypoints

A model conditioned on poses does not reliably adopt their scale. So each window is tied to
sparse points whose depth comes from the filter instead. The keypoints of every image in window
$j$ (XFeat, matched with LighterGlue) are matched against the centre images of up to four older
covisible windows and triangulated under the filter's poses. A keypoint that triangulates well
and survives the depth filter becomes a target. For a target $k$ at pixel $u_k$ with triangulated
depth $z_k$:

$$
\delta_k = \log\!\big(\alpha_j s_{ji} d_{ji}(u_k)\big) - \log z_k,
\qquad
w_k = \frac{1}{v_k + \sigma_0^2},
\qquad
o_j = \arg\min_o \sum_{k\in T_j} w_k\, \psi(\delta_k - o).
$$

$v_k$ is the variance of $\log z_k$, propagated from the pixel noise and from the uncertainty of
the filter's relative position between the two cameras; $\sigma_0 = 0.05$ is a floor; $\psi$ is a
Huber loss and $|o_j|$ is capped at 0.3. One offset per window is enough, because the matches
inside the window have already fixed the images' relative scales $s_{ji}$.

The anchor has a blind spot, and the paper is explicit about it. An error $\epsilon$ in the
relative position of two cameras along a baseline $b$ moves every depth triangulated between
them by about the same relative amount, $\epsilon / b$. The targets agree with each other and are
wrong together. So after the anchor, each image still carries a log-scale error $e_i$ of a few
percent, common to all its pixels and invisible in its own residuals. Two images that see one
wall with different $e_i$ draw it twice.

<ScaleChain />

The widget walks the chain on one wall. With the window fit alone, each window's copy of the wall
sits at its own depth. The anchor pulls every window onto the filter's scale but leaves the
per-image spread, which at three metres is several centimetres. The agreement between views
removes the spread. Move the filter-scale slider and see what no stage can fix: every metre in the
map is borrowed from the filter. The paper's appendix measures how good that loan is: over the
anchor's baselines, the filter's displacements differ from the reference's by -0.4% on the EuRoC
rooms and -1.3% on ORI.

## Measuring disagreement on the map, not through the odometry

The missing constraint comes from the map itself. Take two images $i$ and $j$ from different
windows that see the same surface. Lift every pixel of $i$ to 3D with $i$'s depth, carry it into
$j$ with the map's poses, and compare the depth it arrives at, $z_{i\to j}$, with what $j$'s own
depth map reads at that pixel:

$$
r_{ij} = \operatorname{med}_u\big[\log d_j(\pi_{ij}(u)) - \log z_{i\to j}(u)\big],
\qquad
a_{ij} = \tfrac12\,(r_{ij} - r_{ji}).
$$

If $i$'s depth is too long by $e^{e_i}$ and $j$'s by $e^{e_j}$, then $r_{ij} \approx e_j - e_i$ and
the reverse gives the opposite sign. Occlusion behaves differently. Where a point that $i$ sees is
hidden from $j$, $j$ reads the nearer surface in front of it, so its depth is shorter; and the same
happens in the reverse direction. An occlusion pulls both ratios the same way, a scale error pulls
them in opposite ways, and half the difference keeps the scale error while cancelling the shared
pull. A median and a gate (a pixel whose log ratio is beyond 0.5 is treated as an occlusion and
left out) remove most of the pull before that.

<TwoWayAgreement />

Two refinements are in the appendix. The cancellation is exact only for two cameras equally far
from the surface; if $j$ is nearer by $D$ at scene depth $z$, the measurement picks up a
first-order term $-\tfrac{D}{z}\cdot\tfrac{e_i + e_j}{2}$. That term scales with the mean error of
the two images, which the anchor holds near the filter's scale error, so for a camera that
advanced a tenth of the scene depth it is about a tenth of a percent on ORI and less in the rooms.
The corrections themselves are more than ten times larger, with a median size of 1.6% on the
rooms and 1.5% on ORI, so the term is left out. Push the widget's D slider and the measured value
drifts away from $e_j - e_i$. For small D it follows the first-order term closely; with camera j
1.5 m nearer at a 4 m wall the toy drifts about twice as far as first order predicts, which is what
"to first order" means when D/z is not small.

The second refinement is why this is measured on the map at all. A position error $\epsilon$
between the two cameras changes $a_{ij}$ by $\epsilon / z$, with $z$ the scene depth of several
metres. The same error changes a triangulated depth by $\epsilon / b$, with $b$ a baseline of a few
decimetres, roughly ten times more. So the absolute scale comes from the anchor and the relative
scales come from the surfaces.

### One correction per image

Every counted pair gives one equation $c_j - c_i \approx a_{ij}$. A pair counts if the images are
in different windows and at least 500 pixels take part in each direction, sampling every sixth row
and column. The paper solves

$$
c = \arg\min_c \sum_{(i,j)} \psi\big(a_{ij} - (c_j - c_i)\big) + \sum_i c_i^2
$$

with ten rounds of reweighted least squares, each a linear system $(L + I)\,c = b$, where $L$ is
the weighted Laplacian of the graph of images and pairs. Two properties fall out of the algebra.
Each row of $L$ sums to zero and each pair adds $+w a$ to one entry of $b$ and $-w a$ to another,
so summing over any connected group of images gives $\sum_i c_i = 0$: the group keeps the mean
scale the anchor gave it, and the correction cannot drift the map's scale. And the identity term
weighs as much as one pair, so an image with $k$ pairs moves $k/(k+1)$ of the way its pairs ask:
half the way with one pair, nearly all of it with ten, not at all with none. Then each image's
depth is multiplied by $e^{-c_i}$.

### Context keyframes

The last change is to the depth model's input. A window's depth is predicted from its own five
keyframes alone, with nothing tying it to geometry seen earlier. Accord adds up to two older
keyframes to DA3's input: covisible with the window's centre camera, optical axes within 30°,
cameras 0.3 to 3 m apart, and 2 to 20 s older than the window. The age band is a trade. A context
keyframe is passed with its current pose, so its pose relative to the window carries the filter's
drift since then; younger than 2 s it adds no baseline, older than 20 s the drift grows. The graph
models that drift as 0.5 cm and 0.5° per metre of path.

The paper's ablation (Table 4) adds the parts one at a time, on the EuRoC rooms and Hilti-Oxford:

| mean map F-score at 5 cm | EuRoC rooms | Hilti-Oxford |
|---|---|---|
| DAVIO, as released | 0.381 | – |
| Accord's mapper, no anchor or context | 0.488 | 0.120 |
| + the anchor | 0.547 | 0.147 |
| + context keyframes (Accord) | 0.570 | 0.164 |

The largest single step is the rewritten mapper itself, then the anchor. The appendix adds a
limit worth having: given the reference's poses instead of the filter's, the mapper scores 0.614
on the rooms and 0.891 on ORI, against 0.570 and 0.782 with the filter's. A better filter would
buy a better map.

### What the release does by default

One detail separates the paper's description from the code's defaults. The paper describes the
correction as running online, a window or so behind the map. In the release, `config/accord.yaml`
has `online_correction: false`, and the README says plainly that the map of the main table "is
corrected at export (the default)"; the correction while the map is built is
`--set mapping.online_correction=true` and is scored separately as the live column. So the
headline numbers are of a map corrected once at the end of the run, from everything the run
recorded. That is a fair way to score a map. It is not the same as the map you watch grow.

## The results the paper reports

Every map is placed in the laser scan's frame by the rigid fit of the system's own trajectory to
the reference trajectory, with no scale and nothing fitted to the surface. A wrong scale or a
drifting trajectory therefore costs points directly. Hyperparameters were picked on three
development sequences (EuRoC V1_01 and V1_02, ORI R01) and frozen. Accord runs live on one RTX 4090
with DA3-Giant, and each result is the mean of three runs.

<MapScores />

The pattern in Table 2 is the paper's strongest argument. Accord has the best mean map on all four
public datasets with a scan, and the runner-up is a different system each time: VGGT-SLAM 2.0 on
EuRoC (and only after being given the reference's scale), ScaRF-SLAM's mapper on ORI (run on
Accord's own poses), MASt3R-Fusion on Hilti-Oxford, FoundationVIO on Oxford Spires. On the EuRoC
rooms the means are 0.57 for Accord, 0.49 for VGGT-SLAM 2.0, 0.41 for MASt3R-Fusion and 0.38 for
DAVIO with the same depth model. Per sequence,
Accord's map scores highest on 12 of the 17 scanned sequences. The absolute numbers outdoors are
low for everyone: 0.20 on Oxford Spires is the best of the lot, and depth beyond 20 m is dropped, so
the far sides of college quads are never mapped.

The trajectory side is less flattering, and the paper says so. Accord's trajectory, corrected by the
final map, is the best on no dataset: 6.8 cm on the EuRoC rooms against 3.2 for ORB-SLAM3, 4.5 cm
on TUM-VI against 1.4 for OKVIS2-X, 37.0 cm on Hilti-Oxford against 17.1. The better map does not
come from a better trajectory. Loop closure halves the error on ORI (0.142 to 0.072 m), and on
Oxford Spires it makes the mean worse than the filter's (73.0 against 61.0 cm).

FoundationVIO gets a longer look because it is the closest concurrent work. Its own paper scores
ORI under ScaRF-SLAM's protocol, which cuts the path into 10 m pieces and registers each piece to
the scan, forgiving drift. Accord's authors ran FoundationVIO's release under both protocols: under
the local one (with the recall rule FoundationVIO's paper states), its paper reports 73.1% on ORI,
their run of its release reaches 64.5% and Accord 74.3%. Under the whole-map protocol it scores
0.556 on ORI. On every EuRoC room it diverges at initialization, ending 2.2 to 22.3 km off: it fixes
the scale before the drone has moved, and it ships no EuRoC configuration.

## Phones, a GoPro, and how uncalibrated they were

The new dataset is the most useful thing in the release for anyone who wants a map from a phone. A
Samsung Note 9 and an iPhone were carried together on one hand-held frame beside a LiDAR iPhone
running RTAB-Map, whose loop-closed map is the reference, on 11 campus walks: 2 indoors, 7 outdoors
by day, 2 at night, 59 minutes and 2.5 km. It is public as
[BE2R/accord-phone-rig](https://huggingface.co/datasets/BE2R/accord-phone-rig) on Hugging Face,
CC BY 4.0, with faces and number plates blurred.

<Figure
  src="/articles/accord-metric-mapping/fig6.png"
  alt="A grid of eleven campus scenes (corridors, a stairwell, courtyards, a road, a park, sports grounds, a playground, two night scenes under lamps) and a photo of the hand-held rig holding a Note 9 and two iPhones."
  caption="The phone rig dataset: 11 walks, two of them at night, and the rig. The phones are not calibrated (Accord paper, Figure 6)."
/>

"Uncalibrated" is literal. No target and no calibration recording were used. The Note 9's focal
length comes from its metadata, the iPhone's intrinsics are the median of ARKit's estimates, there
is no distortion model, the camera's rotation on the IMU is the device's axes, the lever arm is
zero and the IMU noise is assumed. Every pipeline scores far lower here than on the public sets
(Table 3). Accord has the best mean on the Note 9 (0.21 at 5 cm, against 0.15 for FoundationVIO). On
the iPhone FoundationVIO is slightly ahead, 0.13 against 0.12. Accord's filter fails on three
recordings (the Note 9's VIO02, and the iPhone's VIO06 and VIO12, the latter at night), where
FoundationVIO fails on none; on the recordings both track, Accord's trajectory error is the lower,
0.94 against 1.99 m on the Note 9.

The reference has its own limits, which the paper states: RTAB-Map's map reaches only about 3.5 m
from its track, so map points more than 3 m from it are left out of the score, and the reference is
"the best estimate available, not ground truth in the survey sense".

The GoPro walk in the opening clip is 11.7 minutes and 623 m over two levels of a busy mall: 4K at
30 Hz read at 640x360, its IMU at 198 Hz, intrinsics from the lens's nominal field of view, the
camera-to-IMU rotation taken as identity. The camera's electronic stabilization was on, which
rotates every image away from the IMU, so the authors undo it in software from the orientations the
camera stores with the video. There is no reference, so the result is qualitative.

## The post, claim by claim

**"Live at 18fps."** The 18 is Table 6: Accord on EuRoC on one RTX 4090, against 15 for
FoundationVIO, 12 for MASt3R-Fusion, 23 for VGGT-SLAM 2.0 and 15 for ScaRF-SLAM's mapper. The
appendix defines it: the length of the footage over the processing time, given as frames per second
of the camera's 20 Hz. Accord is played back at sensor rate, so it cannot score above 20, and its time
includes loading the depth model. So 18 means "keeps up with a 20 Hz camera, nearly", not "processes
18 frames a second through the depth model". The depth model runs on keyframes, two a second, and a
window that arrives while the GPU worker is busy is dropped. ORI's two-megapixel frames were played
at 0.3 of real time. The same table puts Accord at 11.1 GB of GPU memory and 2.4 GB of RAM.

**"No tuning required."** One configuration, chosen on three development sequences and frozen,
with nothing tuned per sequence: that holds, and the README repeats it. Each platform still needs a
rig file (the camera's calibration, its pose on the IMU, the IMU's noise), as any VIO does. And one
platform got more: on Hilti-Oxford the IMU noise was re-measured from the release's recording at rest
(the dataset's first configuration made the filter's scale too long by up to 14% on the development
sequences), and the filter gets an update while the rig stands still. The paper says both, and that
the update at rest is on for Hilti-Oxford only.

**"Any phone, drone/action camera."** It ran on a drone, three hand-held rigs, two phones and a
GoPro, with the phones and the GoPro uncalibrated. That is real breadth. On the phones the maps are
weak for everyone (0.21 and 0.12 at 5 cm) and the filter fails on three of 21 recordings. Read "any
phone" as "any phone whose frames and IMU you can record with timestamps", which is what the repo's
converters for Stray Scanner, RealSense bags and an ASL folder expect.

**"No LiDAR needed."** True of the input. The scoring needs one: every public number is against a
laser scan, and the phone numbers are against a LiDAR iPhone.

**What it costs to run.** Linux, Docker with the NVIDIA container toolkit, and an NVIDIA GPU with
6 GB for DA3-Base or 24 GB for DA3-Giant, the model behind the paper's maps. The code is GPL-3.0. The
DA3-Large and DA3-Giant weights are CC BY-NC 4.0, so the paper's configuration is non-commercial;
DA3-Base is Apache-2.0, and in the paper's Table 5 it scores 0.499 on the rooms and 0.677 on ORI
against Giant's 0.570 and 0.782.

## Where it sits on this site, and what I would use it for

The LiDAR route to metres is the one this site has measured most: [FAST-LIO2](/articles/fast-lio2-lidar-inertial-odometry),
[Voxel-SLAM](/articles/voxel-slam) and the seventeen systems of the [LIO shootout](/articles/lio-shootout) get
centimetre trajectories because the sensor measures range directly. [Mobile SLAM](/articles/mobile-slam-iphone)
puts that sensor on a phone. [Depth Pro 2](/articles/depth-pro-2-single-image-splats) gets metres from one photo's
focal length, and learned scale is exactly what Accord refuses to trust. The [360 capture pipelines](/articles/3dgs-capture-pipelines)
piece is where a map like this would be consumed, and it ends on scale as the open problem.

Accord answers that problem the way I would want it answered: the metres come from an IMU through a filter you can
inspect, the depth model only shapes surfaces, and the correction provably cannot move the overall scale. For
reality capture from a phone or an action camera, it is the first open system I would try before reaching for SfM
plus a tape measure. I would not use it as a survey. Its best outdoor map has a fifth of its points within 5 cm,
its trajectory trails ORB-SLAM3, and the weights behind the headline numbers are non-commercial.
