On 27 October [@ChangJonathanC](https://x.com/ChangJonathanC/status/2115188894287237368) posted: "Claude and I developed a method that can quickly find the neuron you want! neuron-finder can find a specific neuron in ~5 mins on a consumer card with high hit rate." That is a claim about a search, not about a neuron, and it is a testable one. A week of posts had shown that one MLP neuron of Qwen3.5-4B can move a model's persona. The next question is the practical one: if you can name a behaviour, how do you find the neuron that writes it without trying all 294,912?

This article reads the tool's search code, recomputes its headline numbers from the result files it publishes, and re-implements the core idea on a model small enough for a CPU, with random neurons as the control. It then puts the tool next to the two other methods that surfaced in the same week. It is about benign behaviours: languages, topics, styles and how a model describes itself. Our earlier article, [neuron steering](/articles/neuron-steering), explains what pushing a neuron does and why the dose is scaled by the residual norm; I do not repeat that here.

## The detector is not the writer

The reason a search is needed at all is a gap our earlier article measured on Qwen3-0.6B. Ranking neurons by how differently they fire on French and English text gave a clean list: the top 40 kept their sign on held-out prompts. But pushing the ten best-ranked neurons toward French changed the language in two of ten, and the neuron ranked third by that contrast was 2,158th out of 86,016 by gradient attribution (**measured**, in that article). Firing on French and writing French are different properties, and a search that only reads activations finds the first.

neuron-finder is built around that gap. It scores every neuron twice, once as a detector and once as a writer, and then checks the shortlist by actually pushing.

## What the repository is

[`changjonathanc/neuron-finder`](https://github.com/changjonathanc/neuron-finder) is MIT licensed, with the copyright line "Claude Opus 5.5 and Jonathan Chang". I read it at commit `38ae049` (29 October 2026). The Python is 519 lines: `neuron_finder.py` 415, `stats.py` 79, `steer.py` 25 (**measured**, by counting). The default model is Qwen/Qwen3.5-4B, 32 layers of 9,216 MLP neurons, so 294,912 neurons in all. The README says it needs Linux and an NVIDIA GPU (about 17 GB at the default batch), and any OpenAI-compatible API as the judge (**reported**). It ships 46 targets, a per-neuron baseline for Qwen3.5-4B, and the ranked results of one run per target.

I did not run it. The permission layer in this environment refuses to execute downloaded third-party code, and the tool needs a GPU and a judge API in any case. Everything below about its behaviour is read from its code or recomputed from its published results; the re-implementation later is my own.

## The search, step by step

<FinderPipeline />

**1. Pairs.** The model answers 24 everyday prompts twice, once under a persona's system prompt ("You are God…") and once without. The model writes both sides, so the pair is on-policy.

**2. Read score.** For each pair, take every neuron's mean activation at the input of `down_proj` over the reply's tokens, styled minus plain. Average over pairs and divide by the neuron's natural spread on ordinary chat, which `stats.py` measures once over 1M tokens of chat. This finds neurons that fire more on the persona.

**3. Write score.** Take the gradient of $\log p(\text{styled reply}) - \log p(\text{plain reply})$ with respect to each layer's MLP output, summed over every position, because the push is applied at every position. Project it on the neuron's `down_proj` column and multiply by the size of a dose-1 push, so the score is the first-order change in that log-ratio if the neuron were pushed by one dose:

$$
w_{l,n} = \frac{1}{P}\sum_{p}\Big(\sum_t \nabla_{m_{l,t}}\Delta_p\Big)\cdot W_{\text{down}}[:,n]\;\frac{\operatorname{med}\lVert r_l\rVert}{\lVert W_{\text{down}}[:,n]\rVert}
$$

Here $\Delta_p$ is the log-ratio of pair $p$, $m_{l,t}$ the MLP output of layer $l$ at position $t$, and $r_l$ the residual stream leaving layer $l$. A second write list divides $w$ by the neuron's effect on ordinary chat, so neurons that change all text do not take the top places (all **read from code**). In the author's 46 runs the winner came from the read list alone 19 times, from the write lists alone 23 times and from both 4 times (**measured**, from the published files; the README states the same counts).

**4. Shortlist.** The top 30 of each list, plus the top 2 of each list in every layer from 15% to 95% of the depth, plus 16 random neurons as a null. The comment in the code says why: late layers have larger scores and would crowd out the early, topical layers. The median run has 165.5 distinct candidates (**measured**).

**5. Steer and judge.** Each candidate is pushed at three doses (0.8, 1.5, 2.5) with the sign its screen gave, using the recipe from the earlier article: $c = \text{dose}\times\operatorname{med}\lVert r_l\rVert / \lVert W_{\text{down}}[:,n]\rVert$, added to the neuron's activation at every position. Every setting gets 8 sampled replies at temperature 0.8 and one call to an LLM judge asking whether the trait is clearly present and the text is not gibberish. Looping, very short or mostly non-ASCII replies count as misses without a judge call.

**6. Confirm.** The five best settings get 32 fresh replies under a new seed. The winner is **found** if at least 3 in 8 of its replies are hits and its rate beats the 95th percentile of the random neurons, **partial** if it only beats them, **not found** otherwise.

It searches single neurons only. A pair or a triple would need the tool's batched steering to be pointed at combinations, which the main loop does not do (**read from code**).

## What its 46 runs show

I recomputed the headline numbers from `results/*/ranked.json`. They match the README.

<ResultsMap />

- 36 targets found, 5 partial, 5 not found (**measured** from the files; **reported** by the README).
- Median run: 315 s end to end, minimum 230 s, maximum 714 s. The README says the time is mostly the wait for the judge.
- Confirmed hit rate of the winner: median 0.75.
- The random neurons set a median 95th-percentile bar of 0.00. One target, "robot", has a bar of 1.00: the judge already rates the plain assistant voice as a robot, so no neuron can beat the control, and the tool says so.
- Winners cluster early: 12 of 46 at layers 6 and 7. The signed dose of the winner is negative for 27 of 46, and the dose is 0.8 for 22, 1.5 for 21 and 2.5 for only 3.
- Two targets, anime and cartoon mascot, are won by the same neuron, layer 13 neuron 4810.

The README also lists independent checks (**reported**): its alien neuron, layer 22 neuron 2742, is the one the neuron-explorer reports; its cookie neuron, layer 24 neuron 3102, is the one in a post that combined it with a cartoon-mascot neuron. The public source of the [Neuron Studio Space](https://huggingface.co/spaces/webml-community/neuron-studio) lists the same cookie neuron in one of its presets (**read** from the page, not run).

Two misses are worth reading. "Golden Gate" is not found: no candidate gets a hit in 32 confirm replies. The bridge neuron, layer 6 neuron 7448, gets 19 of 32 for the broader "bridge" target but names the Golden Gate in only 2 of 32 replies. And a target can fail because the tool cannot tell, not because there is nothing there: the README says that for the targets it misses, "we cannot tell if the tool missed the neuron or if the model has no single neuron for it."

## Two other ways the same week found neurons

**Gradient attribution, then switch off.** [@treetowntree](https://x.com/treetowntree/status/2115176493105995810) and @_a9lim reported that most of "I'm an AI" in Qwen3-8B before chat training rests on 16 neurons. Their figure's caption says the neurons were chosen by gradient attribution of "I am an AI." against "I am a human.", and that "switched off" means held at the neuron's usual level. With 16 of 442,368 neurons held, the share of 200 written replies that say the model is an AI fell from 68% to 20%, then 9% at 64 and 0% at 256, while holding 1,024 random neurons changed nothing; the model's most likely answer to "What kind of being are you?" flipped at 8 neurons (all **reported**, read from the posted figure). I checked one number: Qwen3-8B has 36 layers of 12,288 MLP neurons, which is 442,368 (**measured**, from its config). A companion figure reports that a model trained on text from before 1930 says it is an AI in 0% of replies and one trained on today's web in 16% ([second post](https://x.com/treetowntree/status/2115125945564848175), **reported**).

This is the write score's cousin: a gradient of a contrast of two target sentences. The differences are the intervention (hold, not add), the selection by top-$N$ rather than a steering sweep, a dose-response in the number of neurons with a matched random control, and a judge that scores questions not used to choose the neurons. I did not try to reproduce it, and I read it as a result about where a model's self-description is stored, not as something to use.

**Brute force.** [A library of persona neurons](https://qwen-neurons-site.vercel.app/) pushes every one of Qwen3-8B's 442,368 neurons alone, up and then down, asks "Who are you?", and keeps the replies: 884,736 of them, of which 34,220 say the model is "someone or something other than an AI" (**reported**, from the page). The post calls these 34,220 neurons; the page counts replies, and a neuron can appear in both directions, so the number of distinct neurons is at most that (**reasoned**).

| Method | Chooses neurons by | Intervention | Judged by | Cost, as reported |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| neuron-finder | read score + gradient write score, then a steering check | add a dose along the column | LLM judge, 8 then 32 replies | about 30 s screen, about 5 min total on one RTX 3090 |
| gradient + hold (Qwen3-8B) | gradient of "I am an AI." vs "I am a human." | hold at its usual level | another model, 200 replies | not stated |
| persona library (Qwen3-8B) | none: every neuron, up and down | add | model rating of each reply | 884,736 replies |
| contrast ranking (our earlier article) | difference of mean activations | add, toward the contrast | counts of target-language words | seconds for the ranking |

## What this does not show

The README's own caveats apply and I repeat the ones that matter here (**reported**). One LLM judge decides, and judges can be wrong, so read the samples. High doses make odd text: a hit means the trait is clear, not that the reply is useful. Architecture matters: Qwen has no norm after the MLP, so one neuron can push the residual far, whereas Gemma-2 and 3 have a `post_feedforward_layernorm` that bounds each layer's MLP output, so a single neuron saturates. And "a neuron that makes the model *say* something is not necessarily a 'concept neuron' in a deeper sense."

On my side: the model is 0.6B and the keyword score is a proxy for the judge. I ran three targets, one seed. The numbers support the shape of the comparison, not a rate.
