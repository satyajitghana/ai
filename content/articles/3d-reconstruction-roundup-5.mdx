The [last roundup](/articles/3d-reconstruction-roundup-4) was about new
primitives. This one keeps running into the same old idea. A year ago the
pitch for feed-forward reconstruction was that one forward pass replaces
structure from motion. Three of this week's four posts say the opposite, in
different ways. Kosmo-GFM is a big geometry model whose authors built a
bundle-adjustment engine around it and say so in the first paragraph ("The
objective is not to eliminate optimization altogether"). A slide that went
round says bundle adjustment on top of VGGT is "still powerful". And Solv3R,
which I [covered last week](/articles/solv3r), is a Transformer trained to
behave like an iterative solver. The fourth, Budget3D, is about a different
kind of compute: how many tokens an image-to-3D generator spends per object.

I read all of this as someone who turns 360 and fisheye site walks into
metric splats, meshes and point clouds for BIM. Each section ends with what it
changes for that kind of work, which is sometimes nothing.

## Kosmo-GFM: a geometry model built to feed an optimiser

The post that circulated it says Kosmo-GFM keeps "depth and pose accurate even
in the wild" when "camera poses are noisy or unknown". The paper makes a
narrower and more useful claim. It is a technical report from Kosmo Research,
with no affiliation printed, but the project page's KosmoWorld section links
to the Kosmo One product page at kosmoai.com. That is the handheld lidar and
fisheye scanner from [Hesai's Kosmo launch](/articles/hesai-kosmo), so this looks
like the reconstruction model behind a capture product.

There are three parts. GFM is the network. PoseEngine is the optimiser that
turns its predictions into a consistent scene. KosmoWorld is a data pipeline
that renders training views out of real reconstructions.

### What the network predicts

GFM's encoder follows Depth Anything 3: a ViT-Giant on 14×14 patches,
alternating attention within each image and across all of them, with a camera
token and four register tokens per view. Features from blocks 19, 27, 33 and
39 are concatenated to 3,072 dimensions for a DPT-style depth head, to which
the authors add residual blocks after each upsampling stage because they
"reduce spurious 3D points near depth discontinuities". Anyone who has cleaned
flying pixels off a depth-fused wall knows why that line is there.

The camera head is a four-block Transformer that refines a nine-number camera
(translation, a quaternion, horizontal and vertical field of view) over four
iterations, supervised at each. The first frame is the world origin. A pixel
becomes a world point the usual way, with predicted depth $\hat z$, intrinsics
$K$ and world-to-camera $[R \mid t]$:

$$
\hat P^{\text{world}} = R^\top\!\left(\hat z\,K^{-1}\bar x - t\right).
$$

Two more heads share the encoder. A dynamic-point decoder, borrowed from
UniQuery4R, answers a query $(u, v, s, t)$: where is pixel $(u, v)$ of frame
$s$ at time $t$, is it visible, and how did it move. A matching decoder,
borrowed from MVLoMa, samples descriptors at keypoints and matches one centre
view against several neighbours at once. It trains with the encoder frozen.

There is also a camera-conditioned variant, which is the part I care about
most. Given known intrinsics and extrinsics, a prompt encoder turns each
camera into a token. The authors put it in place of the first register token,
not the camera token, and explain why: if the prompt replaced the camera
token, "the camera head can reproduce the supplied parameters while patch
tokens make limited use of them". They also train on samples with no
co-visibility at all, so the model has to use the cameras rather than the
pixels to place the views. Depth in this mode is normalised by the largest
camera-centre distance from the first camera, and a second version predicts a
per-frame scale ratio so that small camera motion in a large room does not
push depth targets into huge numbers (the paper's Eq. 13 to 15).

### PoseEngine is GLUEMAP with a better front end

PoseEngine is described in half a page. For unordered photos it builds
"STARs" the way GLUEMAP does: retrieve neighbours with SALAD global
descriptors, reject look-alike pairs with Doppelgangers++, and group each
centre image with neighbours into sets of 26. For video it takes mostly
adjacent frames plus a few retrieved ones. One GFM pass per STAR gives cameras,
depth and matches together, and the engine finishes with bundle adjustment and
motion averaging. The design principle is one sentence: "The stronger GFM
becomes, the simpler PoseEngine can be: more accurate camera and geometry
priors require fewer bundle-adjustment rounds." A Flash variant runs in
FP8/FP16 with "a simple bundle-adjustment scheme".

This is the same lesson as the VGGT slide further down. The network gives a
starting point close enough that the optimiser converges in a few steps
instead of needing a careful incremental build. The widget in that section
shows the mechanism on a toy.

### The numbers, and whose they are

On SpatialBench's Sparse subset, Table 2 puts GFM at an AbsRel of 0.063
against 0.077 for VGGT-Ω, 0.084 for π³-X and 0.095 for DA3-Giant, and at 0.823
camera AUC@30 against 0.803, 0.741 and 0.785. Medium is the same picture, 0.059
AbsRel and 0.828 AUC@30. Three things about that table:

- The caption says the baseline rows are "taken from the official SpatialBench
  release". The Kosmo-GFM row is the authors' own run, and it matches the
  "with KosmoWorld" row of their data ablation.
- GFM is bigger than every model it beats on depth: 1,655 million parameters
  against 1,356 for DA3-Giant and 1,144 for VGGT-Ω. Only DA3-Nested, at 1,690
  million, is larger.
- The paper does not list the datasets the released model trained on. The
  scaling study used 26 public datasets and 187,880 trajectories for its own
  separate models, but I found no list for GFM itself, so I can't check
  whether any SpatialBench test scenes overlap its training data.

KosmoWorld's own ablation is the cleanest evidence in the paper that real
reconstruction data helps: adding it moves Sparse AbsRel from 0.0854 to
0.0630 and AUC@30 from 0.8104 to 0.8226 (Table 1). That is a 26% drop in
depth error from data rendered out of real scans, which says as much about
Kosmo One's captures as about the model.

PoseEngine's evaluation (Table 10) runs COLMAP, GLUEMAP, VidMap and both
engines on 34 scenes from ETH3D, Mip-NeRF 360, Tanks and Temples and SMERF,
11,336 images in all. PoseEngine has the best AUC@1 at 78.45, against 75.73 for
COLMAP and 69.13 for GLUEMAP, in 14.07 hours against 47.08 and 69.67. Flash
takes 2.62 hours and drops to 62.50, below COLMAP. The comparison has the
usual soft spots. COLMAP ran with exhaustive SIFT matching, the slowest way to
run it. VidMap failed on ten ETH3D scenes, which is why there are 34 and not
44. And the timing protocol lists twelve CPU threads and data-loader workers
but no GPU model.

Then there is the table the post did not mention. Table 12 trains LiteGS
splats for 30,000 iterations from each method's cameras. On Mip-NeRF 360,
reference poses give 28.19 dB, GLUEMAP 28.40, PoseEngine 26.69 and Flash
26.34. On Tanks and Temples the gap is smaller: 25.90, 25.80, 25.53 and 24.18.
Every non-reference run also optimises its training poses and gets twenty
steps of test-pose refinement against the test image, which the paper flags
twice as "not a fixed-pose held-out test". So the method with the best pose
AUC produces the worst splats of the three non-Flash options on the
benchmark most splatting papers report. The text explains that Mip-NeRF 360
was initialised "from sparse camera chunks"; it does not explain why that
should cost 1.7 dB.

For dynamic scenes (Table 7), GFM has the lowest mean endpoint error for
points (0.342 m against 0.551 for ARROW) and scene flow (0.188 against 0.291),
while ARROW still has the higher threshold accuracy (77.0 against 76.4 APD, 75.1
against 71.1 flow accuracy). Lower average error with fewer points inside the
threshold usually means fewer catastrophic misses rather than tighter hits.

### What it does with poses you give it

The post's claim is about noisy or unknown poses, and the release has no
PoseEngine, so the only way to test it is with the two models that did ship.
I wrote a script that Satyajit's beast2 box ran on its RTX 3090: the RGB-only
GFM and the camera-conditioned GFM, at commit `4596bdb`, on two clips from TUM
RGB-D, `freiburg1_desk` and `freiburg1_room`. Those are Kinect sequences with
640×480 colour, metric depth and motion-capture poses. Sixteen frames were
taken evenly across each clip; the cameras move up to 2.1 m (desk) and 2.4 m
(room) from the first frame. TUM is one of SpatialBench's datasets, though the
paper doesn't say which sequences, so these clips may not be unseen.

**Unknown poses: the RGB-only model is good.** Across all 120 frame pairs,
the median error in relative rotation against motion capture is 1.3° on the
desk and 2.1° in the room, and in translation direction 1.4° and 2.8°. Pose
AUC@30 is 0.94 and 0.85. Per-frame depth, after scaling each frame by its
median (the model's output has no metric scale), is 2.9% and 3.9% AbsRel
against the Kinect. That is in the range of the paper's own TUM row in Table 5
(0.0460 AbsRel, 0.7928 AUC@30), with a different protocol. Each 16-frame run
took about 30 s including loading a 6.6 GB checkpoint, peaking at 11 to 17 GiB
of GPU memory; 48 frames of the room took 59 s and about 20 GiB, and gave 0.87
AUC@30.

**Known poses: worse depth, not better.** I then gave the camera-conditioned
model the motion-capture poses and intrinsics, in metres, and perturbed them:
every frame but the first rotated about a random axis (σ of 1°, 2° and 5°) and
shifted (σ of 2%, 5% and 10% of the path's extent). The columns are the desk
clip, then the room:

| Poses given | Depth AbsRel, median-aligned | Depth AbsRel, metric | Median 3D error per pixel |
|---|---|---|---|
| None (RGB-only model) | 0.029 / 0.039 | n/a | n/a |
| Exact | 0.085 / 0.084 | 0.22 / 0.16 | 22 cm / 22 cm |
| 1°, 2% | 0.087 / 0.086 | 0.23 / 0.15 | 24 cm / 23 cm |
| 2°, 5% | 0.077 / 0.087 | 0.22 / 0.16 | 27 cm / 31 cm |
| 5°, 10% | 0.088 / 0.101 | 0.24 / 0.18 | 39 cm / 45 cm |

The 3D error is each pixel's point, placed with the pose it was given, against
the same pixel's Kinect depth placed with the motion-capture pose. Three things
come out of it. Exact poses make the per-frame depth two to three times worse in
shape than no poses at all, 0.085 against 0.029 on the desk. The paper's
Table 4 points the same way on SpatialBench (0.082 against 0.063), and the
released checkpoint is the plain camera-pose-scale version, the one that table
shows degrading most; the per-frame-scale variant that recovers some of it was
not released. The metric depth is off by 16% to 22% on average, so even with
perfect poses the fused cloud sits 22 cm from the truth in rooms a couple of metres
across. And noise passes straight through: the depth barely moves, the points
go where the noisy pose puts them, and the error grows from 22 to 39 cm on the
desk. That is what "condition on the prompt" means, and it is the right design
for a model whose poses come from an optimiser. It is not robustness to noisy
poses. In the pipeline the post describes, that robustness comes from
PoseEngine's bundle adjustment, which isn't released.

The camera-conditioned model also outputs its own camera estimate, which the
script saves "for pose-quality diagnostics". Fed the exact poses, that estimate
is 14° to 18° off in median relative rotation on both clips, so it is not a
check you can lean on; I can't tell from outside whether that is the head or
how the export hands it back. What I ran has the usual limits: Kinect v1 depth
is noisy past about 3 m, the colour frames are not undistorted, sixteen
frames is a small sample, and two clips are two clips.

**What it means for a 360 site walk.** The walkthrough bias is the good news;
a site walk is a walkthrough. I hoped the camera-conditioned model would
replace the MVS step behind a rig SfM that already has metric poses from IMU
gravity and scale. On the TUM clips it doesn't: with exact poses its depth is
worse than the RGB-only model's, and 16% to 22% off in metres. The RGB-only
model is the useful part, as a pose and depth initialiser for my own bundle
adjustment, after cutting each fisheye frame into pinhole views, since
PoseEngine and the fisheye support are not something you can run.

## Bundle adjustment on VGGT is a table from March 2025

The second post is a slide: a results table beside the words "BA is still
powerful" and "Geometry is very much alive". The author's own reply names the
source, the VGGT project page. It is Table 1 of the
[VGGT paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11651) (CVPR 2025), unchanged.

The numbers check out against the paper. With ten random frames, feed-forward
VGGT scores 85.3 AUC@30 on RealEstate10K and 88.2 on CO3Dv2 in about 0.2
seconds; with BA, 93.5 and 91.8 in about 1.8. On the IMC photo-tourism
benchmark (the paper's Table 10) BA lifts AUC@3 from 39.23 to 66.37. The
paper's own reading is that VGGT's depth maps and tracks are good enough to
initialise BA directly, so the triangulation and incremental registration
VGGSfM needs are skipped, "making our approach significantly faster". So the
slide is accurate. It is also twenty months old, and the thing it argues for
is exactly what Kosmo-GFM and its baseline GLUEMAP are built on. A reply
pointed at [EPO](https://github.com/mattiadurso/EPO) (ECCV 2026), which refines
VGGT's poses by gradient descent with an edge-based objective instead of
feature tracks; I have not checked its numbers.

Why does a little optimisation recover so much? A pose error is not one bad
camera. Every point that camera sees is triangulated along slightly wrong
rays, and the error in the point grows with its distance. Bundle adjustment
treats poses and points as unknowns together and finds the set that makes
every observation agree. A good learned initialisation means it starts inside
the basin where Gauss-Newton converges in a few steps. The toy below does that
in two dimensions: a 360 camera walked down the middle of an 8 by 5 metre room,
each frame seeing every wall point as a bearing, with its heading perturbed.

<PoseNoiseBA />

At the defaults (six frames, 2° of heading noise, 0.05° of bearing noise) the
points triangulated with the noisy headings are off by 18.3 cm RMS. Bundle
adjustment, from the same bearings, brings them to 0.4 cm and the headings
from 1.38° RMS error to 0.026°. That is better than triangulating with the true
headings by the linear method (0.8 cm), because BA minimises angular error,
which is what the noise is in. At 5° of noise the triangulated points are off
by 64 cm and BA still lands at 0.4 cm, because the starting point is still in
the basin. Not always: over 100 noise draws in the toy at 0.05° of bearing
noise, BA with six frames lands in a wrong minimum in none at 3° and in 7 at
5°, and with three frames in 18 at 3° and 32 at 5°. Press "new noise" at 5°
with three frames and you will see one. That is the case for a good learned
initialisation in one sentence: the optimiser is only as good as where it
starts. Those are my toy's numbers, and the toy is kind: the camera
positions are known exactly, so only rotations are wrong and there is no gauge
to fix. Real BA also solves for positions and the scale, and that is where a
learned prior earns its keep.

## Budget3D: one image-to-3D checkpoint, any token count

The third post, from @_akhaliq, links only to a Hugging Face organisation.
There is no paper. The [model card](https://huggingface.co/BudgetAny/Budget3D-Idol)
calls it an "Unofficial, open-source implementation" of the method in
[BudgetPix](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12307), which I
[wrote up in October](/articles/budgetpix), extended "beyond the paper to
image-to-3D generation". The project page's BibTeX credits BudgetAny to
Ozgur Kara, who is also BudgetPix's first author, so "unofficial" here means
"not the paper's release" more than "someone else's".

The base model is [Idol](/articles/idol-image-to-3d), Sperid Labs' one-image to
3D Gaussian generator. Idol's diffusion transformer denoises 8,192 latent
tokens that sit at fixed 3D positions, then decoders unfold them into up to a
million splats. Budget3D fine-tunes the denoiser so that you can choose, at
inference, how many of those tokens it actually runs on.

### The octree hidden in Idol's latent

The mechanism depends on a fact about Idol that the code checks before it
does anything. Idol's 8,192 token positions are a stratified 3D Sobol
sequence, and they occupy a 16×16×16 grid exactly twice per cell, one point in
each x-half. `budget3d/octree.py` turns that into a three-level octree:

| Scale | One token covers | Tokens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | one Sobol point | 8,192 (100%) |
| 2 | one 16³ cell, two points | 4,096 (50%) |
| 4 | one 8³ cube, sixteen points | 512 (6.25%) |

A layout is a top-down split. $k_4$ of the 512 cubes open into their eight
cells, and $k_2$ of those cells open into their two points, so the token
count is exact:

$$
N = 512 + 7k_4 + k_2, \qquad 0 \le k_2 \le 8k_4 .
$$

Which cubes open is decided by a detail score: the spread of the latent over
the points a token would merge, $\log_2\!\big(1 + \sqrt{\overline{\operatorname{Var}}(z)}/0.08\big)$.
Empty space has no spread and stays coarse. A shared threshold offset is
bisected until the rule lands on the requested $N$, then realised by rank so
every object in a batch gets the same sequence length with no padding.

The odd part is where $z$ comes from, since at step 0 there is no object yet.
BudgetPix ran a few dense warm-up steps and read the layout off the model's
clean estimate. Budget3D's sampler builds the first layout "from pure noise",
with no dense warm-up, refreshes it once at step 5 from the running clean
estimate, "by which point the object's macro-occupancy has settled", and
freezes it for the remaining 45 of 50 steps. The training recipe
(`configs/idol.yaml`) mixes in 15% layouts placed with content-independent
random blobs, so the coarse and fine adapters stay calibrated everywhere
rather than only where objects usually are. In the safetensors header I count
378,089,525 fp32 parameters, matching the card's 378 M, of which the tensors
prefixed `bp_` (the mixers, refiners, scale embedding and positional
encoding Budget3D adds) are 7,563,424, two percent.

The toy below is a two-dimensional version of the same decision: sixteen
coarse blocks over an object on an empty background, each able to open into
four medium blocks and each of those into four fine tokens, 256 at full
budget. One layout opens the blocks whose contents vary most; the other opens
blocks in raster order regardless of content.

<TokenBudget />

In the toy, the content-aware layout reproduces the full-budget image exactly
down to 50% of the tokens, because two thirds of the fine tokens cover empty
space, which costs nothing to merge; the content-blind one is already at 36.7 dB at 90%, and at
40% it sits at 20.9 dB against 40.6. Those are the toy's numbers. They show
why the method works on objects floating in an empty box, which is what Idol
generates.

### Speed is measured, quality is not

The card's tables are careful. On one H100 with bf16 blocks, fused attention
and `torch.compile`, 50 steps with guidance, sampling drops from 5.67 s per
object at 100% to 3.18 s at 50% (1.79× faster) and 2.66 s at 40%, a 2.14×
speed-up, with
peak memory from 9.9 to 7.8 GB. In fp32 they go down to 10% of the tokens:
63.44 s to 14.48 s, 4.38×. Decoding the Gaussians (2.53 s) and rendering an
orbit (3.60 s) do not change with the budget, so end to end at 40% is 8.62 s
against 11.80. The Turbo variant on an RTX PRO 6000 goes from 0.48 s to 0.25 s.

The speed-up is less than the token ratio would suggest. At 40% of the tokens,
attention alone would be 6.25× cheaper and the MLPs 2.5×; the measured 2.14×
says fixed per-step work (conditioning, the adapters, guidance's second pass)
is a real share. That is my reading of their table, not something they break
down.

What is missing is any number for quality. The repository has
`scripts/eval_budget.py`, which renders every budget's object from several
views and scores PSNR, SSIM and LPIPS against the same model's 100% result,
with a noise floor from decoding twice with different seeds. That is a good
protocol. No results from it are published; the card has hand-picked clips,
and the tech report is "coming soon". I did not run it. Idol reads its input through two
image encoders. The FLUX.2 VAE is ungated (Budget3D loads it from
FLUX.2-klein-4B, Apache-2.0), but DINOv3 ViT-H+ sits behind Meta's licence
acceptance. Budget3D's converter script fetches that checkpoint from an
unofficial re-upload instead, and I would not route around the gate for a
benchmark.

**What it means for reality capture.** Little, directly. Budget3D generates
objects from one picture; it does not reconstruct a site. The idea transfers
though: a room scan is mostly empty air and flat walls, and a reconstruction
model that spent its tokens where the geometry varies would be far cheaper on
a 4,000-frame walk. Nobody has shipped that one yet.

## The Solv3R demo, and the panel that isn't in the code

Jonathan Stephens posted a 39-second screen recording of the Solv3R demo,
streaming a desk sequence at 2× speed, then training splats on the result in
"about a minute". Solv3R's model is the subject of [my earlier
article](/articles/solv3r). What has changed since is the
[demo](https://huggingface.co/spaces/jgkwak/solv3r), added to the repository in
commit `c3851d3` on 11 November: solve photos or video offline, incrementally
or as a stream, watch the solve call by call, and export a `.ply`, a COLMAP
model or the depth maps.

23.55 dB is on twelve views of

my work the piece that matters is the COLMAP export, which is in the public
code: a streamed solve that drops straight into an existing splat or MVS
pipeline, with the non-commercial weights as the obstacle.

## What I'd test on a real walk

Kosmo-GFM is the release to spend time on, and the paper is worth reading in
full for the camera-prompt design and the honest limitations section. I don't
buy "production-ready" for what was released: no pose engine, no fisheye,
splats from its own poses that trail GLUEMAP's on Mip-NeRF 360, and weights
whose terms the repository itself calls unresolved. On my two clips the
RGB-only model's poses were good and the camera-conditioned model was the
weak half. I'd use the first as an initialiser and keep my own bundle
adjustment.

The VGGT slide is right and old. If you run a feed-forward model for poses,
put a bundle adjustment after it; ten frames in VGGT's own table went from 85.3
to 93.5 AUC@30 for 1.6 more seconds.

Budget3D is a tidy piece of engineering with honest speed tables. Wait for
the quality curve before choosing a budget below 100%.

The Solv3R demo is the best demo in this roundup, but the splat training in
the clip is not in it yet.

For the capture side of all this, see
[Gaussian-splat capture in practice](/articles/3dgs-capture-pipelines) and
[GoFish's fisheye splatting](/articles/gofish-fisheye-gaussian-splatting);
for other feed-forward reconstructors with an optimiser behind them,
[HiP3R](/articles/hip3r) and [Déjà View](/articles/deja-view).
