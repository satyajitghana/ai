## What earshot is

earshot calls itself "ears for coding agents". Most of it is listening: `describe`, `match`, `find`, `timeline`, `critique` and `sheet` turn a sound file into words and numbers an agent can read. That half uses two open models, the CLAP text-audio embedder `laion/larger_clap_general` and the AudioSet tagger `MIT/ast-finetuned-audioset-10-10-0.4593`, about 1.4 GB on first use (reported). The figure below is `earshot sheet` on the project's launch film.

The other half makes sound: `earshot forge` from code, `earshot fit` from a recording to code. Both are numpy and scipy.

## What a "sound type" is

A sound type is a parameterized synthesizer. Each recipe module in `earshot/forge/recipes/` defines a `SPACE` of named parameters with ranges, `DEFAULTS`, and a `render(p, r)` that builds the sound from those parameters and a random generator. The click recipe, for example, sums four layers and runs them through a finishing chain:

1. a 0.15 to 1.5 ms band-passed noise burst (the transient);
2. one to three damped partials at `pitch` (the "tink");
3. a short damped sine at 150 to 1200 Hz with a small pitch drop (the body);
4. a short band-passed noise tail (the grit), and an optional release click 25 to 90 ms later.

Then a high-pass, a dip at 2.5 to 4 kHz, light `tanh` saturation, a small space and an end gate. No recorded audio is used.

Twenty modules cover the 34 categories: 18 named after real-recording reference sets (`whoosh_fast`, `click`, `impact`, `typing_key` and so on) and 16 `pro_*` ones modelled on effects measured in professional launch films (reported, `CHANGELOG.md`). Each category is a family of four to six named characters, which are narrowed parameter ranges: `click` has `soft`, `thock`, `tink`, `snap` and `double`.

The "183 sounds" are not audio files in the repository, which holds no audio at all. They are parameter sets. Each recipe's seed bank in `earshot/forge/seeds/*.json` marks the members that were delivered, and those marks add up to exactly 183 across 19 files (measured). Because the parameters and the seed are committed, any of them re-renders: I rendered the `pro_notify` ding, a `pro_pop` chirp and the `crisp` fast whoosh from the bank and used them as fit targets below.

Where do those parameters come from? From a search. `earshot forge` renders candidates from a recipe's `SPACE`, pre-screens them with model-free measurements (third-octave balance, envelope, artifacts), and sends the best to a critic that does use the models: CLAP's match to a text description against named alternatives, a realness score (logistic heads over CLAP embeddings and signal measurements, shipped as plain JSON), and closeness to licensed reference recordings that are not in the repository. An evolution strategy keeps the best and mutates them. That is where the machine learning in earshot lives: in judging sounds, not in making them.

## How fit turns a recording into code

`earshot fit` is analysis by synthesis. It does not train anything. It measures the sound, fits small synthesis models whose parameters are things a sound designer would name, renders each one, and keeps the one whose render is closest to the recording. Four steps, from `earshot/forge/fit/auto.py`:

**1. Name the class.** `character()` measures attack, how long the sound takes to fall 40 dB, crest factor, tonal share (the energy in spectral peaks 12 dB over the local median), how persistent those peaks are from frame to frame, the glide of the strongest partial, and spectral flatness. From those it names a class: glide, ringing, struck, gesture or mixed. The class orders the candidate models.

**2. Fit the candidates.** There are three fitters, each a classic DSP method:

- `modal.py` for struck and ringing sounds: per detected onset, damped sinusoids found by ESPRIT on 15 sub-bands, amplitudes and phases by least squares, a fast "contact" made of very short modes, and band noise envelopes for whatever is left.
- `sines.py` for tonal sounds: McAulay-Quatieri partial tracking (STFT peaks joined frame to frame, glides kept), damped-sinusoid "atoms" at each strike, and the residual as noise in third-octave bands.
- `spectral.py` for noisy sounds: a time-varying 48-band noise gesture, stereo per band group, and texture statistics for crackle and flutter.

Hybrids stack them: the first layers keep only their deterministic part, and the next layer is fitted on what remains. Under 1 s all six candidates are fitted (the three fitters and three hybrids); at 1 s or longer, the class's first four.

**3. Calibrate.** Each candidate gets a post stage measured from its own renders: a smooth third-octave EQ onto the original's balance, the level, and a clamp that stops the render from standing above the original's envelope before the onset.

**4. Score and keep the lowest.** `quality.score` folds the metrics into one number:

$$
s = \text{mel} + 0.3\,\text{tone} + 8\,(1 - r_\text{env}) + \min(\text{onset}, 2) + 1.5\,\text{excess} + \text{missing}
$$

Here mel is a multi-resolution log-mel distance in dB, tone is the RMS third-octave balance difference in dB, $r_\text{env}$ is the correlation of the 5 ms dB envelopes, onset is the octave error in onset sharpness, and excess and missing are dB of energy one sound has where the other is quiet. With `--clap`, $20\,(1 - \cos_\text{CLAP})$ is added. The weights were set on the same 12 recordings the project then measured on, which the documentation says itself (reported).

There is no gradient descent over synthesizer parameters and no neural network in the default path. The parameters come out of estimators (ESPRIT, least squares, peak tracking, band energies), and the only search is a choice among six candidates. I checked that bluntly: torch and transformers were not installed in my environment, and every fit ran.

## What the emitted code looks like

This is the recipe `fit` wrote for my synthetic struck bar, abridged. The full file is 498 lines and 31.1 KB (measured). I cut the noise tables and replaced the absolute path in `REFS` with the file name.

```python
"""synth_bar_fit: a forge recipe fitted from a real recording by `earshot fit` (sines model).

auto chose sines of sines (4.30), modal (5.33), hybrid:sines+spectral (5.36), spectral (12.13)
strike at 30 ms (1 strike(s)), 3 partials 523-2770 Hz, lowest strong 523 Hz, inharmonic (ratios 1.00, 2.70, 5.30), ...

Fit quality (original vs this recipe at DEFAULTS, seed 0):
  mel 1.74 dB, env r 1.000, tone 7.34 dB, onset err 0.03 oct (sharpness 47.9 vs 49.0 dB/ms), ...
"""
import numpy as np

from earshot.forge.fit import auto as _engine

TARGET = 'a bright ringing chime'
ALTS = []
REFS = ['synth_bar.wav']
KIND = 'sines'

SPACE = {
    'transpose': (-3.0, 3.0),   # semitones (partials and the residual spectrum together)
    'stretch': (0.85, 1.18, 'log'),   # time factor about the first strike
    'decay': (0.7, 1.43, 'log'),   # factor on every decay after its peak
    'brightness': (-3.0, 3.0),   # dB / octave about the partial centroid
    # ... vibrato, drift, residual, transient, vary, inharm
}
DEFAULTS = {'transpose': 0.0, 'stretch': 1.0, 'decay': 1.0, 'brightness': 0.0, 'vibrato': 0.0, 'drift': 0.0,
            'residual': 1.0, 'transient': 1.0, 'vary': 0.0, 'inharm': 0.0}   # the identity: re-renders the fit

MODEL = {
    'kind': 'sines',
    'sr': 48000,
    'duration': 1.0,
    'onsets': [0.03],
    # strongest first: birth t0 s, rise s, last breakpoint t1 s, release s, phase, [t, Hz], [t, dBFS]
    'partials': [
        {'t0': 0.03, 'rise': 0.00198, 't1': 1.00267, 'rel': 0.00533, 'ph': -0.005,
         'freq': [[0.03, 523.1], [0.976, 523.0], [1.00267, 521.9]],
         'amp': [[0.03, -6.0], [0.98133, -33.1], [0.992, -34.6], [0.99733, -37.3], [1.00267, -42.7]]},
        {'t0': 0.03, 'rise': 0.00198, 't1': 0.88533, 'rel': 0.00533, 'ph': 0.896,
         'freq': [[0.03, 1415.81], [0.048, 1414.01], [0.88533, 1413.66]],
         'amp': [[0.03, -12.1], [0.72533, -71.2], ...]},
        {'t0': 0.03, 'rise': 0.00198, 't1': 0.352, 'rel': 0.00533, 'ph': 2.041,
         'freq': [[0.03, 2770.28], [0.352, 2770.16]],
         'amp': [[0.03, -15.6], [0.32, -77.7], ...]},
    ],
    # per strike: damped sinusoid atoms [Hz, tau s, amplitude, phase]
    'transients': [{'t': 0.0295, 'atoms': [[15222.2, 3.47e-05, 7.6429916, -1.3443], ...]}],   # 31 atoms
    # noise in 1/3-octave bands (Hz centres): dB envelopes at `times`
    'residual': {'bands': [50.0, 63.0, ...], 'times': [...], 'db': [[...], ...]},   # 26 bands x 93 times
    'post': {'eq_db': [...], 'gain_db': 0.77, 'pre': {...}},
}


def render(p, r):
    """one take: p = knob values (missing keys take DEFAULTS), r = numpy Generator (noise / jitter)"""
    q = dict(DEFAULTS)
    q.update({k: v for k, v in p.items() if k in DEFAULTS})
    return _engine.render(MODEL, seed=int(r.integers(0, 2 ** 31 - 1)), **q)


def render_seed(seed=0, **knobs):
    """render with an explicit seed: render_seed(0) is the fit as measured"""
    q = dict(DEFAULTS)
    q.update(knobs)
    return _engine.render(MODEL, seed=seed, **q)
```

Three things to notice.

- **The code is mostly data.** The sound is the `MODEL` literal: three partials, each a few breakpoints of frequency and level. The two functions only hand it to the engine. Most of the 498 lines are the residual table, which here models the -70 dBFS noise floor I added to the target.
- **It is not standalone.** `render()` calls `earshot.forge.fit.auto.render`, so the module needs earshot installed. Each fitter also has a `to_code()` that writes a standalone numpy script, but the CLI writes the recipe module.
- **The knobs are the point.** `transpose`, `decay` and `brightness` are what make a recipe more useful than the WAV. `earshot fit --forge recipe.py` hands that `SPACE` to the forge's critic-driven search. The `TARGET` line is a guess for that critic, made from the fit; on the twig snap it guessed "an air whoosh".

The engine checks that the module's own render equals the model's render sample for sample. It did in all six runs (`code_renders_fit: true`, measured).

## What I ran

A shared cloud VM: four cores of an Intel Xeon at 2.1 GHz, 15 GB of RAM, no GPU, other jobs running. A Python 3.12 venv with earshot installed from the clone and its DSP dependencies, but not torch or transformers: `fit` does not import them unless CLAP is asked for. Every command ran with two threads under `nice -n 19`.

The test suite (`pytest`, the fast set) gave 488 passed, 10 failed, 20 skipped in 237.68 s (measured). The 47 fit tests all passed. The ten failures are in `test_interfaces.py`: three need the `mcp` extra I did not install, and seven Gemini-client tests read their fake HTTP session's call log in a shape it did not have. None touch `fit` or `forge`.

The six targets:

1. **Struck bar**, synthesized by me: three damped modes, 523 Hz with a 0.30 s decay time constant, 1414 Hz with 0.10 s, 2771 Hz with 0.04 s, amplitudes 0.6, 0.3 and 0.2, a 1.5 ms noise contact at 30 ms and a -70 dBFS noise floor. 1.0 s.
2. **Glide**, synthesized by me: a tone rising from 600 to 1200 Hz over 0.25 s, a second harmonic at 0.3, a 5 ms rise and a 0.12 s decay. 0.5 s.
3. **earshot's `pro_notify` ding**, rendered from its seed bank. 0.26 s.
4. **earshot's `pro_pop` chirp**, from its seed bank. 0.162 s.
5. **A twig snapping**, a real recording: "Snapping twig (single)" by Stephan, CC0, from [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Snapping_twig_(single).ogg), decoded to 48 kHz mono. 0.83 s.
6. **earshot's fast whoosh** (`whoosh_fast`, member `crisp`), from its seed bank, stereo. 1.06 s.

Each fit was one CLI call, `earshot fit target.wav -o target_fit.py --json`, with no `--clap` and no `--report`. Peak memory was 251 to 261 MB per call (measured).

## Did it find the parameters?

The struck bar is the clean test, because I wrote down its parameters before fitting it.

The auto fit kept the partials model. Its three partials start at 523.1, 1415.8 and 2770.3 Hz, against 523, 1414 and 2771 (measured). The decay time constant is not stored directly, but each partial's level is a straight line in dB, so it falls out of two breakpoints: 27.1 dB over 0.951 s is a time constant of 0.305 s, 59.1 dB over 0.695 s is 0.102 s, and 62.1 dB over 0.29 s is 0.0406 s, against 0.30, 0.10 and 0.04 (reasoned from the measured breakpoints). The starting levels are -6.0, -12.1 and -15.6 dBFS, or 0, -6.1 and -9.6 dB relative, against 0, -6.0 and -9.5 for amplitudes of 0.6, 0.3 and 0.2 (reasoned).

Forcing `--kind modal` gives the exact answer, because modal is the model I synthesized the bar with: three modes at 523.0, 1414.0 and 2771.0 Hz, time constants 0.29998, 0.099976 and 0.040015 s, phases 0.0001, 1.0001 and 1.9982 rad against 0, 1 and 2 (measured). The auto engine scored that model 5.33 and the partials model 4.30, so it kept the partials. The modal fit's onset was twice as sharp as the target's, 103.2 against 47.9 dB/ms, and its recipe was 160 KB because it modelled my noise floor band by band (measured). The selection score cares about how the render sounds, not about which model is physically right, and here those disagree.

The glide is the weakest fit. The fundamental is tracked from 605.2 to 1200.6 Hz, against 600 to 1200 (measured). The second harmonic is not one partial: one track carries it from 1222 Hz to 0.273 s, a second is born at 0.137 s at 1667 Hz and carries it to the end, and a fourth, 40 dB down, wanders from 837 to 1190 Hz in between. That handover is the blip near 0.14 s in the spectrogram. The onset is blunted too: 61.5 dB/ms in the target, 16.7 in the fit, an error of 1.885 octaves, the worst of the six (measured). The documentation lists zips and long glides as untested, and a 0.25 s glide is where I would expect trouble to start.

On the real twig, envelope correlation was 0.894, the lowest of the six (measured), and the docstring's analysis says the 11 partials carry almost none of the energy ("residual -0.0 dB of the energy"): the snap is reproduced as third-octave noise envelopes with a few partials on top. That is a reasonable model of a snap. It is also why the generated `TARGET` reads "an air whoosh".

## What surprised me

**The partials model won everything.** The documentation's own validation chose sines for 6 of 12 references and modal, hybrids or a sines-plus-spectral pair for the rest (reported). On my six it was sines every time, including the whoosh, where the class plan put the spectral noise model first and the whoosh's 11 partials sit between 109 and 442 Hz under a residual that holds most of the energy. Six sounds is a small sample, but here the tracked-partials model plus a band-noise residual was the general-purpose answer.

**earshot's tone metric is not a quality score on sparse sounds.** The struck bar fit reports `tone_db` 7.34, the second worst of the six, on the fit whose parameters are within 2.5% of the truth (measured). The bar has almost nothing between its three lines except a -70 dBFS floor, and a third-octave balance over those empty bands moves with the floor, not the sound (reasoned). The selection score weights it by 0.3, so it does not decide much.

**Fitting is quick; long and dense sounds are not.** The 0.162 s pop took 5.27 s; the README says 12 s for a 0.2 s pop on the author's machine (reported). The twig took 36.12 s, 14.27 of them in the three-layer hybrid alone (measured), and the documentation reports 250 to 340 s for 4.5 to 6.5 s impacts (reported). I did not fit the 4.8 s impact from the seed bank.

## What I did not run, and the limits

- **The quality claims.** Median CLAP cosine 0.945 on 12 references, 10 of 12 with the same `describe` tag, and a 12-trial blind test in which one listener could not tell recording from render: all reported, on licensed recordings that are not public. I did not install CLAP, so I measured no cosine. One listener is a small test, and the project says so.
- **Variations and morphs.** I did not run `--forge` (it needs the CLAP critic and, for the forge's own categories, the reference sets), `--morph`, or the `vary` knob. The limits page says knob variations of a whoosh fitted as hundreds of modes "may sound metallic" (reported).
- **The two videos in the thread.** Real-against-code audio in a video is the author's comparison; I did not extract or measure it.
- **Selection is in-sample.** The score's weights were set on the same 12 references they were checked on, and the selection has no held-out test yet (reported).
- **Not tested upstream:** zips and long glides, risers, swipes, shimmers and stereo-heavy designed sounds (reported). My glide falls in that gap and was the weakest fit here.

## Where it fits

earshot is aimed at the same agents that write launch videos in code; [the one-shot launch videos piece](/articles/one-shot-launch-videos) looked at skills that ship 260 bundled sound effects to do that job, which is the problem `forge` and `fit` try to replace with parameters. For neural audio generation on the same kind of CPU, [speech.cpp](/articles/speech-cpp) runs six speech model families in ggml, and [Paradee](/articles/paradee-tiny-tts) distils a text-to-speech model to 8M parameters. earshot is the other end of audio: short effects, where a few partials and some band noise are enough and no network is needed to make the sound.

The honest summary of `fit`: it is careful, well-documented classic DSP, analysis by synthesis over three model families, with a hand-weighted score to choose between them. It recovered a known sound almost exactly, reproduced noise sounds about as closely as noise allows, and was weakest on a pitch glide. The code it writes is a readable parameter set plus a call into earshot, and its value is the knobs, not the bytes.
