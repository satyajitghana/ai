Long-video pose estimation has spent the last year going the transformer way.
LingBot-Map carries geometric context across frames in structured attention
memory; HorizonStream couples local attention with a bounded recurrent state.
Both need training on long sequences to learn to remember. **MetricTrail**,
from NVIDIA with Shanghai Jiao Tong, Toronto and Stanford, goes the other
way. Its first author's thread puts it plainly: "revisit good old DPVO, add
MoGe-2 depth priors and DINOv3 features." The paper is
[MetricTrail: Revisiting the Power of Metric Depth and Local Optimization for Long-Sequence Streaming 3D Reconstruction](https://research.nvidia.com/labs/sil/projects/metrictrail/assets/MetricTrail.pdf),
the code lives inside NVIDIA's [ViPE repository](https://github.com/nv-tlabs/vipe)
under `vipe/metrictrail/`, and the trained matcher is on Hugging Face as
[nvidia/MetricTrail](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/MetricTrail).

The claim worth taking apart is narrow and checkable: a sparse visual
odometry with a **fixed, small optimisation window** stops drifting over
thousands of frames once each patch's depth is tied to a single-image metric
depth prediction. No bigger window, no long-sequence training, no
correspondence network retraining for that part. This piece explains why that
works from the residuals up, shows what the released code actually does, and
reproduces the effect in a toy I could run on a CPU. I could not run the real
pipeline: it needs CUDA kernels, and this box has no GPU.

## Why a small window drifts

DPVO (Teed, Lipson and Deng, NeurIPS 2023) is the estimator underneath. Every
frame contributes a few dozen 3x3 patches. A patch $k$ has a fixed pixel
centre $\mathbf{u}_k$ in its source frame and one free number, its inverse
depth $\rho_k$. An edge connects a patch to a nearby target frame $j$, and
the projection of the patch into that frame is

$$
\mathbf{q}_{kj} = \pi_K\!\left(\mathbf{T}_j\,\mathbf{T}_{s(k)}^{-1}\,\pi_K^{-1}(\mathbf{u}_k, \rho_k)\right).
$$

A recurrent network looks at feature correlations around $\mathbf{q}_{kj}$
and predicts where the patch really is, $\hat{\mathbf{q}}_{kj}$, plus a
confidence $\mathbf{w}_{kj}$. Bundle adjustment then moves the poses and the
inverse depths to agree with those targets:

$$
E_{\text{DPVO}}(\mathbf{T}, \rho) = \sum_{(k,j)} \left\lVert \mathbf{q}_{kj}(\mathbf{T}_j, \mathbf{T}_{s(k)}, \rho_k) - \hat{\mathbf{q}}_{kj} \right\rVert^2_{\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{w}_{kj})}.
$$

The loop runs inside a bounded window. In the shipped `metrictrail_local`
preset that is `OPTIMIZATION_WINDOW: 10` poses and `PATCH_LIFETIME: 13`
frames, with 96 patches per frame. Older poses leave the optimiser and are
frozen into the output trajectory.

Here is the problem. Every term in $E_{\text{DPVO}}$ is a pixel residual, and
pixel residuals are blind to scale: multiply every translation and every depth
by the same factor and nothing changes. Inside one window the scale is pinned
only by the frozen poses at its old end. Each window inherits the scale of the
last one, plus a little error, and over 4,541 frames of KITTI 00 those little
errors compound into a random walk. DPVO makes it worse at the seam where new
geometry enters: a new patch starts at **the median inverse depth of the
patches from the preceding three frames**. That seed carries no information
about where this particular patch is, and it copies forward whatever scale the
estimator has already drifted to. The paper's own phrasing is that the median
"carries forward the current monocular scale rather than correcting it."

The result, in the paper's Table 1: the official DPVO checkpoint with
calibrated intrinsics averages **52.69 m** of trajectory error across KITTI
00-10, and 113.11 m on sequence 00 alone. That is the number MetricTrail
starts from.

## Change one: metric depth that stays in the optimiser

Modern single-image depth models predict depth in metres. MoGe-2 does it with
intrinsics estimated from the same image. MetricTrail uses that prediction
twice.

1. **Initialisation.** A new patch starts at $\rho_k^{(0)} = 1 / \hat{D}_{s(k)}(\mathbf{u}_k)$,
   the predicted depth at its own pixel, in metres.
2. **A persistent factor.** The same observation stays in bundle adjustment as
   a Huber-weighted unary on log inverse depth:

$$
E_{\text{ours}}(\mathbf{T}, \rho) = E_{\text{DPVO}}(\mathbf{T}, \rho) + \lambda_m \sum_{k \in \mathcal{M}} \operatorname{Huber}_\delta\!\left(\log \rho_k - \log \hat{\rho}_k\right).
$$

The second is what does the work over long sequences. A seed is forgotten the
moment BA moves the patch; a factor keeps pulling. Because every patch in the
window now has a term that measures its depth in metres, the window's scale is
anchored to the depth network's notion of a metre, not to the frozen poses
behind it. The scale random walk loses its integrator. DPVO's own scale
normalisation is switched off in metric runs, since it would wash that anchor
out.

The code is short enough to read in full. In `vipe/metrictrail/dpvo/ba.py`,
`_metric_unary_terms()` turns each patch's prior into one diagonal entry and
one right-hand-side entry of the depth block of DPVO's Schur system, as an
iteratively reweighted Huber:

```python
# vipe/metrictrail/dpvo/ba.py, _metric_unary_terms (abridged)
residual = safe_prior.log() - safe_current.log()          # log(rho_hat) - log(rho)
robust = torch.where(abs_residual <= huber_delta,
                     torch.ones_like(abs_residual),
                     huber_delta / abs_residual.clamp_min(1e-6))
weight = prior_lambda * safe_confidence * robust
jacobian = safe_current.clamp_min(1e-4).reciprocal()      # d log(rho) / d rho
C = weight * jacobian.square()                            # added to the depth Hessian diagonal
rhs = weight * residual * jacobian                        # added to the depth gradient
```

The constants are in `configs/slam/metrictrail_local.yaml` and match the
paper's appendix: `METRIC_DEPTH_PRIOR_WEIGHT: 1.0` ($\lambda_m$),
`METRIC_DEPTH_PRIOR_HUBER: 0.3` ($\delta$), and
`METRIC_DEPTH_PRIOR_WARMUP: 12`, so the factor is off for the first 12
accepted frames and the first regular BA update with it lands on frame 13.
Depths outside 0.2-500 m count as invalid: such a patch is seeded with the
frame's median and gets zero factor weight.

Two details matter. The residual is in **log** inverse depth, so the factor
measures ratios: a 10% error costs the same at 3 m and at 80 m, which is
what you want from a depth network whose errors scale with depth. And
$\delta = 0.3$ in log units is a depth ratio of about 1.35. Inside that band
the prior pulls in proportion to the disagreement; past it, the pull is
capped. A wrong prediction on a window or a sky patch cannot drag the geometry
further than a merely imprecise one, and the reprojection terms from the other
frames get the final say. The widget evaluates the exact formula above.

## Change two: DINOv3 features in the matcher, through 41,536 parameters

The prior fixes where the matcher starts looking. It does not help the matcher
choose between two similar-looking candidates in a row of identical windows.
For that MetricTrail adds features from a frozen DINOv3 ViT-S/16. A 1x1
convolution projects the 384-dimensional DINO tokens to 64 channels, split
into two 32-channel branches: one is projected to 128 channels and added to
DPVO's matching feature map, the other is sampled at each patch centre,
projected to 384 channels and added to the recurrent context. The final
projections are zero-initialised, so the fused model starts out exactly
reproducing the pretrained DPVO predictions.

I downloaded the released checkpoint (`dino_dpvo.pth`, 13,739,570 bytes) and
loaded it with `torch.load(..., weights_only=True)` in PyTorch on CPU, which
reads tensors and runs no pickled code. **Measured:**

| Part | Parameters |
|---|---|
| Whole checkpoint (fp32) | 3,425,860 |
| `update` (the recurrent update operator) | 3,004,804 |
| `patchify` (feature and context encoders, with adapters) | 421,056 |
| Adapters: `dino_projection` 64x384 conv + bias | 24,640 |
| Adapters: `match_fusion` 32 to 128 + bias | 4,224 |
| Adapters: `context_fusion` 32 to 384 + bias | 12,672 |
| **All adapter parameters** | **41,536** |

So 41,536 of 3,425,860 parameters, 1.2%, are new; the rest is DPVO's
architecture, fine-tuned. The DINOv3 backbone itself is not in the file: the
checkpoint's metadata lists `patchify.dino.backbone.` under
`omitted_external_state_prefixes` and records the SHA-256 of the
`dinov3_vits16_pretrain_lvd1689m` weights it expects, which you fetch
separately under Meta's DINOv3 licence. The zero-initialised projections
did move in training: `match_fusion.weight` ended at a Frobenius norm of 0.52
and `context_fusion.weight` at 3.40, so the context branch is the one the
network learned to lean on more.

Training is short and local. Every sample is a 15-frame clip from TartanAir,
TartanGround, CarlaOcc, LightwheelOcc or OmniWorld-Game; 15,000 steps on 16
A100s, 240,000 clips, about seven hours. MoGe-2 never appears in training.
The metric prior enters only at inference, which is why the paper can bolt it
onto the original DPVO weights and still get most of the gain.

## What the depth prior does, rebuilt on a CPU

The real pipeline needs three CUDA extensions (`cuda_corr`, `cuda_ba`,
`lietorch_backends`), the 1.3 GB MoGe-2 ViT-L checkpoint and the gated DINOv3
weights, and this machine has no GPU or `nvcc`. So I rebuilt the one idea I
wanted to test, in about 170 lines of numpy: a DPVO-shaped estimator in the
plane, small enough to run thousands of frames on one core.

The toy keeps DPVO's shape and drops everything learned. A camera drives
3,000 frames along a winding path of about 3 km at roughly 1 m per frame,
like a car in KITTI. Every frame gets 16 patches: a pixel on a 1-D image
(focal 300 px) and one inverse depth, with true depths drawn between 4 and
80 m. The "matcher" is the true projection plus 1 px of Gaussian noise. The
window is 12 frames: patches from all 12, edges to every other frame in it,
the 10 newest poses free and the 2 oldest frozen, 4 Gauss-Newton steps per
new frame. The "single-image depth network" is the true depth times a
log-normal error: 10% per patch, 5% shared by the whole frame, and 5% of
patches off by a factor of 1.6 to 4.5. Three rules for a new patch's depth:

- **median**: DPVO's rule, the median inverse depth of the previous three frames' patches.
- **init**: the noisy metric prediction as the seed, then nothing.
- **prior**: the same seed, plus the factor exactly as `_metric_unary_terms()` builds it ($\lambda = 1$, $\delta = 0.3$, off for the first 12 frames).

The factor is ported by reading the code: I meant to call the repository's
own `_metric_unary_terms()` on the same inputs as a cross-check, and the
sandbox refused to execute the downloaded code, so that comparison is not
done. Same trajectory, same scene and same measurement noise for all three. ATE is
the RMSE after one Sim(2) alignment of the prefix, so a constant scale error
is forgiven and only drift counts. **Measured**, mean of three seeds, on one
CPU core at about 34 frames per second:

| Frames | median seed | metric init only | init + factor |
|---|---|---|---|
| 500 | 12.00 m | 2.40 m | 1.54 m |
| 1,000 | 32.20 m | 8.57 m | 2.54 m |
| 2,000 | 105.09 m | 44.32 m | 4.34 m |
| 3,000 | 192.30 m | 122.08 m | **6.52 m** |
| Sim(2) scale needed at 3,000 | 3.80 | 1.72 | 1.04 |

<DriftLab />

Three things fall out, and they line up with the paper's argument.

**The median seed drifts without bound.** By frame 3,000 the estimate has
shrunk to about a quarter of true size (the alignment needs a scale of 3.80),
and the error is still growing. That is the scale random walk, with the
median rule copying each step forward.

**A metric seed alone only delays it.** Seeding from the depth network keeps
the first few hundred frames honest, because every new patch arrives at
roughly the right depth. But the BA is free to move patches once they are in,
the frozen poses still pass scale from window to window, and by 3,000 frames
the error is 122 m on average, with one seed at 198 m. This is the paper's
point that "initialization alone cannot prevent later BA updates from
drifting away from the predicted metric scale", reproduced.

**Keeping it as a factor stops the drift.** With the factor the error at
3,000 frames is 6.52 m on average (4.23 to 8.14 m across seeds) and the
scale stays within 4% of true the whole way. What is left grows slowly, which
is heading drift: the depth prior pins scale, not rotation.

Two variations, one seed or three as marked, also **measured**:

- **Huber versus plain least squares.** With $\delta$ set to infinity, so every
  prior pulls in proportion however wrong it is, the 3,000-frame error rises
  from 6.52 m to 9.16 m (mean of three seeds: 5.70, 10.72, 11.07 m). The cap
  is doing its job on the 5% of gross outliers.
- **A biased depth network.** If every prediction is 10% too far, the factor
  still stops the drift (4.58 m, seed 1) but the trajectory comes out about
  10% too large: the alignment needs a scale of 0.94 against 1.04 unbiased. The
  metric scale is exactly as good as the depth network's. Init alone with the
  same bias: 152.05 m.

Tripling the per-patch noise to 30% costs the factor little (7.06 m, seed 1)
and does nothing for init alone (167.74 m). What the toy does not have: a
learned matcher, outliers in the matches, keyframing, rotation drift of any
realistic size, dynamic objects. It tests one mechanism, and that mechanism
behaves the way the paper says it does.

The swap mostly holds. On KITTI and VBR every provider takes DPVO far below
its no-prior error. On Oxford Spires, where DPVO alone is already at 2.59 m,
two providers make it worse (Pi3X at 5.57 m, UniDepthV2 at 3.89 m), which is
what a confident, wrong prior does to a working estimator. DA3Metric and
Metric3Dv2 beat MoGe-2 in this calibrated setting; the paper keeps MoGe-2 as
the default because it also estimates intrinsics from RGB alone, and the
other two need a focal length to produce metric depth at all. If you have
calibrated cameras, the paper's own table says to try DA3Metric first.

Against the rest of the field, under the RGB-only protocol (intrinsics
estimated from the video), Table 1 and Table 2:

| Average ATE (m) | KITTI | Oxford Spires | VBR |
|---|---|---|---|
| ViPE | 28.68 | 7.06 | 46.20 |
| LingBot-Map | 25.29 | 5.15 | 27.53 |
| HorizonStream | 19.75 | 8.75 | 25.30 |
| MetricTrail, uncalibrated | **14.05** | **1.77** | **16.36** |
| MetricTrail, uncalibrated, loop closure | 9.78 | 1.77 | 10.88 |

The thread's "cuts ViPE's average trajectory error by 51% on KITTI and 75% on
Oxford Spires" checks out against these rows: 28.68 to 14.05 m is 51.0%, and
7.06 to 1.77 m is 74.9%. With calibrated stereo on KITTI the error falls to
3.66 m, and 3.00 m with loop closure. The loop closure is simple: SALAD
retrieves near-duplicate frames, and each pair adds a weak identity
relative-pose edge to an SE(3) pose graph, with no geometric verification of
the pair.

Three things the headline rows do not say.

**VBR depends on an offline focal refinement.** The 16.36 m uses a
"revisit-based focal refinement" from the appendix: it takes the base
trajectory, finds temporally distant revisit pairs, matches them with RoMa,
fits fundamental matrices with MAGSAC and re-estimates the focal length. That
step reads the whole trajectory, so it is not streaming, and it averages 7.1
minutes per routed sequence. The paper says it "was motivated by our analysis
of VBR", where the base calibration overestimated focal length by 7-9% on six
of seven sequences. Without it, Table 16 reports **33.51 m** on VBR, which is
worse than LoGeR* (25.24 m) and HorizonStream (25.30 m). The paper discloses
all of this, and the thread's percentages are for KITTI and Oxford Spires only.

**Dynamic scenes are not a win.** On TUM-Dynamic dense reconstruction,
MetricTrail's 0.13 m Chamfer distance trails TTT3R's 0.06 m and LingBot-Map's
0.08 m. The paper says so itself: "the gains on static indoor and large
outdoor scenes do not extend uniformly to scenes with dynamic content."

**The runtime number is calibrated.** 12.70 FPS on KITTI-02 with 3.21 GB peak
GPU memory and about 0.7 s to first output, on an A100, assumes supplied
intrinsics. Estimating them from RGB adds about 175 s of start-up and raises
peak memory to 6.81 GB; throughput afterwards is the same 12.70 FPS. For
comparison the paper measures LingBot-Map at 11.37 FPS and HorizonStream at
9.92 FPS on the same sequence.

To run it you need an NVIDIA GPU, the ViPE install, then
`python -m vipe.metrictrail.build` to compile the DPVO kernels, then
`python run.py pipeline=metrictrail streams.base_path=<video>`. The
evaluation scripts live on a separate `metrictrail_eval` branch. I did not run
any of it.

## Where this leaves long-video pose

The interesting result is not that MetricTrail beats LingBot-Map. It is how
little it took. The trajectory-level problem everyone has been throwing memory
at is, for a classical sparse estimator, mostly one missing term: an absolute
reference for scale at every patch, which a single-image depth network now
supplies for free. The window stays at 10 poses. The matcher never sees a
sequence longer than 15 frames. What changed is that the window can no longer
lose track of what a metre is.

The costs are real. You inherit the depth network's biases, and the provider
table shows that a confident wrong prior hurts a working estimator. You
inherit its calibration needs, which is where VBR's offline focal fix comes
from. And dynamic content still breaks the static-scene assumption that both
DPVO and the prior make. For the transformer side of this same problem, see
[R³ and AMB3R-SLAM on a frozen Depth Anything 3](/articles/da3-streaming-reconstruction)
and [the second 3D reconstruction roundup](/articles/3d-reconstruction-roundup-2);
for what a metric depth model needs from its wrapper,
[Depth Anything 3 in ROS 2](/articles/depth-anything-3-ros2); and for
odometry with a sensor that measures metres directly,
[FAST-LIO2 from scratch](/articles/fast-lio2-lidar-inertial-odometry).
