Sundar Pichai's post called EmbeddingGemma 2 a "lightweight, modular 740M parameter form factor"
that handles text, code, images, video and audio. The word I didn't understand was *modular*. A
multimodal embedding model is usually either one big network that reads everything, or a set of
separate towers glued together by a contrastive loss. "Modular" could mean either, and the two
behave very differently when you try to put one on a phone.

So I read the files. The [model card](https://huggingface.co/google/embeddinggemma-2), the
safetensors header (fetched with two HTTP range requests, no weights downloaded), the
`embedding_gemma2` model code in transformers, the processors that turn a JPEG or a WAV into
tokens, and Google's three launch posts. The short version: every input, whatever it started as,
ends up as a sequence of 512-wide vectors fed through one small text encoder, and that encoder is
the only part you can't remove. The vision and audio towers are front ends. And the audio front
end turned out to be something I didn't expect: it is Gemma 4's audio encoder, unchanged, down to
the byte.

## What is in the 744 million

The safetensors header is a JSON index of every tensor with its shape. Summing them gives
744,371,992 parameters, all bf16. The Hub's own counter says 744,371,512. The 480 missing are
scalars: the audio tower stores a minimum and maximum for the input and output of every clipped
linear layer, 40 per conformer layer across 12 layers, each a zero-dimensional tensor that the
Hub's counter skips. Either way, 740M is a fair rounding.

Grouped by which module the tensor belongs to:

| Module | Parameters | What it is |
|---|---:|---|
| `language_model.embed_tokens` | 134,217,728 | the vocabulary table, 262,144 tokens x 512 |
| `language_model.ple`, `embedding_projection`, final norm | 6,685,696 | per-layer input projection, and the 512 to 768 output head |
| `language_model.layers` (24) | 130,099,224 | the transformer |
| `vision_tower` + `embed_vision` | 167,757,824 | 16-layer ViT, 768 wide, and a 768 to 512 projection |
| `audio_tower` + `embed_audio` | 305,611,520 | 12-layer conformer, 1,024 wide, and a 1,536 to 512 projection |

That matches the card's own split to within rounding: a 130M transformer, a 140M "embedder", a
170M vision encoder and a 300M audio encoder. Two things stood out.

Half of the text model is one lookup table. The 262,144-token vocabulary times a 512-wide
embedding is 134.2M of the 271.0M. The transformer that does the actual reading is 24 layers of
about 5.25M each (6.3M for the four global-attention layers, which have wider heads). This is the
usual shape for a small Gemma: a huge multilingual vocabulary on a narrow model.

And the audio tower is the largest single module in the file, bigger than the whole text model.
If you want audio, you are carrying 306M parameters for it.

## An image is 280 words the vocabulary does not have

The mechanism is easiest to see in `EmbeddingGemma2Model.forward`. The processor writes the text
with a placeholder for every piece of media: `<|image|>`, `<|video|>`, `<|audio|>`. It then
expands each placeholder into as many placeholder tokens as the media will produce, wrapped in
begin and end markers. The model embeds the text tokens normally, runs each tower, projects its
output to 512 dimensions, and overwrites the placeholder slots with those vectors:

```python
# transformers/models/embedding_gemma2/modeling_embedding_gemma2.py:797-799
inputs_embeds = inputs_embeds.masked_scatter(
    image_mask.to(inputs_embeds.device), image_features.to(inputs_embeds.device)
)
```

After that line, the text encoder can't tell a word from a patch of a flamingo. It sees one
sequence of 512-wide vectors and reads all of it with the same 24 layers. Google's own diagram
draws it that way, with the encoders feeding a single box labelled 270M:

How many slots each piece of media takes is decided by the towers.

Images go through a 16-layer ViT with 16-pixel patches. Its output is pooled three by three, so
each output token stands for nine patches. At the default budget of 280 tokens an image gets up to
2,520 patches, roughly an 800 x 800 picture at its own aspect ratio. The budget can be set
anywhere from 70 to 1,120, the same ladder Gemma 4 uses, which I covered in
[the Gemma 4 piece](/articles/gemma-4). Video is the same tower run on frames, sampled at one per
second, at 140 tokens a frame.

Audio is resampled to 16 kHz mono and turned into 128-bin log-mel frames every 10 ms. Two
stride-2 convolutions shrink that by four, so one audio token covers 40 ms: 25 tokens a second.
The conformer's attention is chunked and local (`attention_chunk_size: 12`,
`attention_context_left: 13`, `attention_context_right: 0` in the config): a token attends within
its own 12-token chunk, 480 ms, plus about half a second of the past, and never past the end of
its chunk. It was built to stream. Any
understanding of a whole clip has to come from the text encoder, which sees every audio token at
once.

The text encoder itself is a Gemma 4-style stack turned bidirectional. The attention class sets
`self.is_causal = False` (line 331) and the masks come from `create_bidirectional_mask`. Five
sliding-window layers alternate with one global layer, 24 in all. The config says
`sliding_window: 512` while the card says 1,024, and both are right: the bidirectional window
lets a token see 512 positions on each side, about 1,024 in total. The global layers use one
key-value head (multi-query attention) with 512-wide heads; the local ones use two key-value heads
with 256-wide heads.

The last step is the one that makes it an embedding model. The final hidden states are normalised
and projected from 512 to 768 per token, then `sentence-transformers` takes the mean over every
position, prompt included (`include_prompt: true` in `1_Pooling/config.json`), and L2-normalises
it. The code comments why projecting before pooling is allowed:

```python
# modeling_embedding_gemma2.py:504-505
# Projecting per token is equivalent to projecting after mean pooling
self.embedding_projection = nn.Linear(config.hidden_size, config.embedding_dim, bias=False)
```

A linear map commutes with an average, so it costs more compute but gives the same vector. That
head is one 512 x 768 matrix with no bias. EmbeddingGemma 1 used two dense layers after pooling,
768 up to 3,072 and back down to 768, according to [its report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.20354).
The new model is narrower inside (512 against 768) and has a simpler head.

Mean pooling has a consequence worth stating plainly. The embedding is an average over positions,
and a default image is 280 positions while a caption is twenty. Attention mixes them all before
the average, so a 280-to-20 split is not a 93% share of the meaning, but the media does dominate
the arithmetic. I come back to this when budgeting the context window below.

## Per-layer embeddings without the lookup table

One detail in the text encoder took me a while, and it is the most interesting design choice in
the file.

Gemma 3n and the small Gemma 4 models use per-layer embeddings (PLE): besides the normal embedding
table, every token id also looks up a small extra vector for every layer, from a second, very
large table, and each layer mixes its slice into the residual stream. It is how Gemma 4 E2B keeps
5B parameters on disk and runs like a 2.3B model, as I explained in
[the Gemma 4 article](/articles/gemma-4). The catch, for an embedding model fed images and audio,
is that a soft token has no token id. There is nothing to look up for a patch of a flamingo.

EmbeddingGemma 2 keeps the per-layer signal and drops the table. The per-layer inputs are
computed from the input embeddings themselves, through one linear layer:

```python
# modeling_embedding_gemma2.py:193-200 (EmbeddingGemma2TextPLE)
def forward(self, inputs_embeds):
    per_layer_projection = self.per_layer_model_projection(inputs_embeds) * self.per_layer_model_projection_scale
    per_layer_projection = per_layer_projection.reshape(
        *inputs_embeds.shape[:-1], self.num_hidden_layers, self.hidden_size_per_layer_input,
    )
    return self.per_layer_projection_norm(per_layer_projection)
```

The class docstring calls it "projection-only PLE: the signal is derived from `inputs_embeds`
alone, with no token-identity lookup". The projection is 512 to 24 x 512, which is the 6.29M
`per_layer_model_projection` in the header. Each layer then gates its slice in with a small
bottleneck after attention and the MLP (`hidden_states * per_layer_input` at line 224).

This works the same for a word and an image patch, because both arrive as a 512-wide vector
before the projection. It also explains part of why the text model is 271M and not several
billion: there is no per-layer table to carry. I'd read it as a requirement of native
multimodality more than a cost trick, though Google doesn't say which.

## Modular means the front end, not the model

Now the word from the post. In `EmbeddingGemma2Model.__init__`, each tower is built only if its
config exists:

```python
# modeling_embedding_gemma2.py:646
self.vision_tower = AutoModel.from_config(config.vision_config) if config.vision_config is not None else None
```

Pass `config_kwargs={"vision_config": None, "audio_config": None}` to `SentenceTransformer` and
neither tower nor its projection is constructed; the loader is told to ignore their weights
(`_keys_to_ignore_on_load_unexpected`, line 637). What is left is the 271M text encoder. Add
vision for 440M, audio for 570M, both for 740M. Those are the card's four configurations, and the
header sums agree with them.

The part that matters for an index: a text input never touches either tower. The placeholder masks
are empty, the `masked_scatter` calls are skipped, and the same 24 layers run on the same token
embeddings. So a text embedding from the 270M configuration is the same vector as one from the
full model, and the developer guide says exactly that: "Embeddings you have already computed do
not need to be re-computed." You can index a document store with the small configuration on a
laptop today and add photos to the same index later with the full one.

What you can't do is drop the text encoder. An image embedding is the mean of the text encoder's
output over the image's soft tokens. There is no vision-only embedding path, and the vision tower
alone produces nothing you can compare. "Modular" here means a model with swappable front ends
and one shared reader, not a set of independent towers.

## The audio encoder is Gemma 4's, byte for byte

The launch blog says EmbeddingGemma 2 "shares its text tokenizer and audio encoder" with Gemma 4,
so that the two can run together "with a lower combined total memory footprint". The developer
guide softens that to sharing the "audio encoder architecture". Those are different claims: one
means the same code, the other means the same weights you could load once.

I checked the weights. Safetensors stores each tensor as a contiguous byte range listed in the
header, so I fetched the same tensors from `google/embeddinggemma-2` and `google/gemma-4-E2B`
with range requests and hashed them. All 36 audio tensors I sampled, three from each of the 12
conformer layers (attention projections, convolution input projections and the clipping bounds),
plus the tower's output projection, were byte-identical. They are also identical in
`gemma-4-E2B-it` and `gemma-4-E4B`. The tokenizer vocabulary is the same 262,144 entries in the
same order.

The vision tower is a different story. Same shapes, different bytes: none of the eight vision
tensors I sampled matched Gemma 4 E2B's. So the vision encoder was trained for embeddings, and the
audio encoder was either frozen throughout or never trained here at all, with only the 1,536 to
512 projection (`embed_audio`) and the text encoder learning to read it.

Two practical consequences. If you run Gemma 4 E2B for generation and EmbeddingGemma 2 for
retrieval on the same device, the 305M audio tower can, in principle, be loaded once; whether a
runtime does that is up to the runtime, and I couldn't check what LiteRT does. And EmbeddingGemma
2's audio understanding is bounded by what Gemma 4's speech encoder already captures, which is
mostly speech. That fits the benchmark numbers below.

## How it was trained, as far as the files say

There is no EmbeddingGemma 2 paper. The model card covers data (web text in over 140 languages,
code, images, video, speech and sounds, paired cross-modal samples, a January 2025 cutoff) and
nothing about the loss or the stages. So what follows is partly inference, and I'll mark it.

EmbeddingGemma 1's report describes the recipe that this almost certainly builds on: a
noise-contrastive loss over in-batch negatives with harder negatives weighted up, a spread-out
regulariser that penalises squared dot products between unrelated embeddings in a batch, a loss
that matches the student's embeddings to Gemini Embedding's, Matryoshka versions of those losses
applied to nested prefixes of the vector, a large pre-finetuning stage on unlabelled pairs, a
finetuning stage with hard negatives on several mixtures, and a final model that averages the
checkpoints from those mixtures. The card's task prefixes (`task: search result | query: `) are
the same family as EmbeddingGemma 1's, and the blog says EmbeddingGemma 2 is "built from the same
technology as Gemini Embedding models".

For the multimodal part, the contrastive loss itself doesn't care what the positives are. A batch
can pair a spoken question with a passage, a caption with a photo, or a text query with a video,
and InfoNCE pulls each matched pair together and pushes the rest apart. I walked through that
loss, and the two failure modes a shared index has (the right concept in the wrong modality, and
a modality gap that clusters captions with captions), in
[the Ovis-Embedding article](/articles/ovis-embedding).

What the files do tell me: the audio tower didn't move, the vision tower did, the text encoder is
a new 512-wide network rather than EmbeddingGemma 1's 768-wide one, and MRL is trained in: the card
says so, and its truncation table degrades gently down to 256, which plain truncation of an
untrained vector would not. The AI Edge post adds that
quantization-aware training produced the int4 and int8 versions. Everything else about the
recipe, including whether a Gemini Embedding teacher was used for images and audio, I couldn't
check.

## Spending the window

The card advertises an 8,192-token context "capable of processing minutes of audio or video", and
a table: about 29 images, 58 video frames or 327 seconds of audio. Those are 8,192 divided by 280,
140 and 25. They describe the window, not what the shipped preprocessing will actually feed it.

The video processor in transformers has `max_frames = 32` and `overflow_strategy = "uniform"`
(`video_processing_embedding_gemma2.py:192-193`). Hand it two minutes of video at one frame per
second and it keeps 32 frames spread evenly, one every 3.75 seconds. The audio side goes through
Gemma 4's feature extractor, whose `__call__` defaults to `max_length=480_000` samples with
truncation on, which is 30 seconds at 16 kHz. A five-minute recording becomes its first 30
seconds. Both caps can be overridden, and the LiteRT runtime streams audio in its own way, but the
default `model.encode({"audio": ...})` path is what most people will run first. I couldn't run it
to confirm the end-to-end behaviour, so treat this as a reading of the code with a test to do,
not a measured result.

There is also a third number in the repo's own `processor_config.json`, `audio_seq_length: 280`,
which caps the token count reported by `_compute_audio_num_tokens` (the helper serving engines
call to reserve placeholder slots) at 280 tokens, 11.2 seconds. The class default is 750. I don't
know which value vLLM ends up using; if long audio fails to embed there, this is where I'd look.

None of this is a defect. A single vector is a poor summary of five minutes of anything, and
Google's own Video Moments Finder demo indexes chunks of a video, not the whole file. The
practical rule is to chunk long media yourself, a few seconds of audio or a handful of frames per
embedding, and let the index hold many vectors per file.

In numbers: text costs one token per subword, an image its budget plus two marker tokens, a video
frame 142, and an audio clip 25 per second plus two. A product listing with a 40-token

seconds of video and it is 3,444 tokens and the text is about 1%. If the words matter, I'd embed
the description on its own as well and keep both vectors, or lower the vision budget to 70 for
the photos in an interleaved input.

## Shorter vectors

Matryoshka training means the first 512, 256 or 128 numbers of the 768-dimensional vector are
themselves a usable embedding, once renormalised. The card's truncation table shows how much each
benchmark keeps.

<MrlDial />

The cost is uneven. At 256 dimensions every benchmark keeps at least 95% of its full score, and
storage drops threefold: a million vectors go from 3.07 GB to 1.02 GB in float32, or 1.54 GB to
512 MB in bfloat16. At 128 the text rows keep 94% to 96% of their scores (multilingual text goes
from 61.36 at full width to 57.89 cut), but the multimodal average, MMEB v2 overall, falls from
59.01 at full width to 45.65 cut, about 77%, and MSEB spoken retrieval from 69.54 to 56.71. The
card's own advice is "128d is best suited to text-only workloads", and the numbers back it.

Two small inconsistencies in the launch material. The AI Edge post says MRL cuts index footprints
"by up to 8x"; 768 to 128 is 6x, which is what the card and the main blog say. And the card's
re-normalisation warning is worth taking seriously: a cut vector is no longer unit length, and
cosine scores on unnormalised prefixes still look plausible while ranking worse.

## Ten million vectors in 0.4 GB, and what that leaves out

*Added 7 October 2026.*

The day after launch, Qdrant's Shivendu (@KShivendu_) posted the storage side of the story: "We pushed
10M of Google's EmbeddingGemma 2 vectors from ~30GB of RAM down to ~0.4GB", by quantizing each
dimension to one bit with TurboQuant and then cutting the vector to 256 dimensions with the
Matryoshka truncation above. "77x less RAM for a ~5% drop in quality." The
[Qdrant blog post](https://qdrant.tech/blog/embeddinggemma-2) behind it has the numbers, and most of
them hold up. Two things about how they're framed don't, so here is the arithmetic first.

Ten million 768-dimensional float32 vectors are 10,000,000 x 768 x 4 bytes, which is 30.72 GB. The
blog says 30.7, the post rounds to 30. At one bit a dimension, 768 dimensions are 96 bytes, but the
blog gives 104 bytes a vector; at 512 dimensions it gives 72, where the bits alone are 64; at 256
it gives 40 against 32. So every quantized vector carries 8 bytes beyond its bits. Qdrant's
[TurboQuant write-up](https://qdrant.tech/articles/turboquant-quantization/) says what goes in them:
per-vector scalars, as far as I can tell a stored length and RaBitQ's correction for how much
quantization shrank the vector, which the scoring multiplies back in. That accounts for the whole gap you'd otherwise puzzle over. Bits alone
would give 96x at 256 dimensions; 3,072 bytes over 40 is 76.8x, the "77x". The post's own "30 GB
to 0.4 GB" is 75x, but that is just the rounding of 30.72. At 768 dimensions the same overhead
turns a 32x bit ratio into 29.5x, which the blog calls "30x".

The footnote on that chart is the first thing I'd hold onto. "Vector RAM excludes graph indexes,
payloads, and original vectors stored for rescoring." The 94.5% belongs to the rescoring bar, and
rescoring means Qdrant fetches 40 candidates with the 1-bit codes and ranks them again with the
original vectors, which have to be stored somewhere. At 256 dimensions in float32 that is another
10.24 GB, normally on disk and read on every query. So "0.4 GB of RAM with 94.5% kept" is true of
the RAM, and the 94.5% also needs ten gigabytes of originals the RAM figure doesn't count. Without
rescoring the same 0.4 GB keeps 88.1%, which is a 12-point drop, not 5.

The graph is the other thing left out, and at this compression it is the bigger one. Qdrant searches
an HNSW graph, and with its usual m = 16 each vector keeps up to 32 neighbour ids of 4 bytes at the
bottom layer. That is up to 128 bytes a vector, three times the 40-byte vector itself, and about
1.28 GB for ten million. That is my estimate of an upper bound, not a number Qdrant published, and
real graphs fill fewer links than the maximum, but once vectors get this small the index structure
around them is what you're actually paying for.

<VectorRam />

None of that makes the result bad. The configuration the blog itself recommends starting with is
the full 768 dimensions at 1 bit with rescoring off: 104 bytes a vector, about 1 GB for ten million,
and 99.0% of the baseline's nDCG@10. On Quora's 523,000 documents Qdrant reports it took less than
half the query time of float32 search. That is the number I'd take from the post, more than the
77x. Two caveats the blog states itself. The retention is an average over five small BEIR sets
(SciFact, NFCorpus, ArguAna, SCIDOCS and FiQA), so the ten million is a byte calculation, not a
measured run. And 99% of the nDCG is not 99% of the same results: without rescoring, about 74% of
the top 10 matched exact search.

The comparison I found most useful is one the blog makes in passing. At 72 bytes a vector you can
have 512 dimensions at 1 bit, which kept 97.3%, or 128 dimensions at 4 bits, which kept 84.0%. Same
memory, thirteen points apart. Cutting bits is cheaper than cutting dimensions, which fits the MRL
table above: at 128 dimensions the model itself has already thrown away what the extra bits would
have preserved. Qdrant's results are all text retrieval. Shivendu says in the replies that MRL and
TurboQuant "work on any kind of embeddings", and mechanically they do, but the card's own table has
the multimodal scores falling much faster under truncation than the text ones, so for an index of
photos and recordings I'd keep 512 or 768 dimensions and take the savings from the bits.

### Whose 1-bit quantizer it is

TurboQuant ([Zandieh et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.19874)) rotates each vector by a random
orthogonal matrix so that every coordinate follows the same known distribution, then quantizes each
coordinate with one fixed Lloyd-Max codebook. I walked through it for the KV cache in
[the TurboQuant article](/articles/turboquant-kv-cache), and for vector search in
[TurboVec](/articles/turbovec). At four or two bits the codebook is the interesting part. At one bit
it isn't: the optimal one-bit quantizer for a symmetric distribution is the sign, so 1-bit
TurboQuant stores the sign of each rotated coordinate. That is also exactly what
[RaBitQ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12497) stores.

Qdrant's own write-up is candid about this. The 8 bytes of per-vector scalars come from RaBitQ's
length renormalization, not from the TurboQuant paper, and "The 1-bit scoring path follows RaBitQ
as-is, without specific modifications": the query is scalar-quantized into bit planes and scored
against the stored signs with an AND and a popcount per plane. One reply to the post, from
@gaoj0017, quoted that line and called the "TurboQuant" label misleading. Going by Qdrant's own
documentation, I agree. Every configuration in the EmbeddingGemma 2 chart is a 1-bit one, so what
was measured is a RaBitQ-style 1-bit code with a Hadamard-type rotation in front of it. The name is the only thing wrong. Where Qdrant's TurboQuant does add something is at two
and four bits, and its own benchmark shows the 1-bit version beating its older binary quantization
on every dataset it tried.

That chart is the honest summary. Plain binary quantization takes the sign of the raw coordinates;
a random rotation first, plus a length correction and a multi-bit query, is worth 9 to 21 points of

vectors keeps 99% of the ranking quality. One other reply linked
[arXiv 2608.30384](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.30384); that is RSLM, a separate training-free
rotate-and-Lloyd-Max codec, not the TurboQuant paper.

## On a phone

The [LiteRT bundles](https://huggingface.co/litert-community/embeddinggemma-2-740m-litert-lm) use
quantization-aware training: int4 per-channel for the transformer and the embedding table, int8 for
the vision encoder, and a mix of int2, int4 and int8 for the audio encoder. The downloads are 165
MB for text only, 388 MB for text and vision, and 485 MB for everything.

LiteRT card is the one I find most useful: 8.3 ms on a Pixel 11 Pro's TPU and 41.8 ms on an
iPhone 18 Pro's CPU, both for a 128-token input.

## Using it

The card's calls, put together the way I'd start a text index. I haven't run this here; it is
assembled from the card's and the developer guide's own examples, and needs
`sentence-transformers` 6.1.0 or later.

```python
import torch
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer

# bf16 or fp32 only: the card says fp16 overflows and returns NaN or degraded vectors
dtype = torch.bfloat16 if torch.cuda.is_available() and torch.cuda.is_bf16_supported() else torch.float32

# Text and code only: the vision and audio towers are never built (271M parameters)
model = SentenceTransformer(
    "google/embeddinggemma-2",
    config_kwargs={"vision_config": None, "audio_config": None},
    model_kwargs={"torch_dtype": dtype},
    truncate_dim=256,
)

files = {"pool.py": "def mean_pool(h, mask):\n    return (h * mask[..., None]).sum(1) / mask.sum(1, keepdim=True)"}

# Documents: format the title yourself; prompt_name="Document" would write "title: none"
docs = [f"title: {name} | text: {code}" for name, code in files.items()]
doc_emb = model.encode(docs, normalize_embeddings=True)

# Queries: the task prefix matters, and queries and documents must share a width
q_emb = model.encode("average token states ignoring padding", prompt_name="CodeRetrieval", normalize_embeddings=True)
print(model.similarity(q_emb, doc_emb))
```

Later, the same index takes photos and recordings from the full model, without re-embedding the
text:

```python
full = SentenceTransformer("google/embeddinggemma-2", model_kwargs={"torch_dtype": dtype}, truncate_dim=256)

photo = full.encode({"image": "IMG_0412.jpg"}, normalize_embeddings=True)    # no text prefix for media
memo = full.encode({"audio": "memo_16k_mono.wav"}, normalize_embeddings=True)  # chunk long audio first
listing = full.encode({
    "text": "Waterproof trail shoe. <|image|> Grip test on wet rock: <|video|>",
    "image": "shoe.jpg",
    "video": "grip.mp4",
}, normalize_embeddings=True)
```

The prefixes are listed in `config_sentence_transformers.json`: `SearchQuery`, `QuestionAnswering`,
`FactChecking`, `CodeRetrieval`, `Classification`, `Clustering`, `SentenceSimilarity` and
`Document`. Retrieval is asymmetric (a query prefix on one side, `title: … | text: …` on the
other); classification and similarity put the same prefix on both. Media go in without a prefix.
Because the pooling includes the prompt, the prefix changes the vector; mixing prefixed and
unprefixed text in one index is a quiet way to lose recall.

## Half a gigabyte, and which half

A few hours after launch Unsloth posted that EmbeddingGemma 2 "runs locally on 0.5GB RAM", in the
same breath as "740M parameter ... combines a 270M text model with vision (170M) + audio (300M)",
and linked [its own GGUFs](https://huggingface.co/unsloth/embeddinggemma-2-GGUF) and
[a guide](https://unsloth.ai/docs/models/embeddinggemma-2). Read together, that sounds like the
whole multimodal model in half a gigabyte. I wanted to know which configuration the number
belongs to, so I read the GGUF headers of both repos the same way I read the safetensors one:
range requests for the first few megabytes, then a parse of every tensor's name, shape and type.

The [ggml-org conversion](https://huggingface.co/ggml-org/embeddinggemma-2-GGUF), made by the
same people who merged EmbeddingGemma 2 support into llama.cpp (pull request 30054, merged on
launch day), has four files: a text model in BF16 and Q8_0, and an `mmproj` in BF16 and Q8_0.
Unsloth's repo has those four with byte-identical tensors (same 413 and 963 tensor lists, same
types, the last 4 MB of each pair hash the same; the files differ by 64 to 96 bytes of name
metadata), plus three "UD" text quants, Q4_K_XL, Q5_K_XL and Q6_K_XL, and F16 copies of both
files. That is the whole difference.

| File | Size | What is inside |
|---|---:|---|
| text, UD-Q4_K_XL (Unsloth) | 175.7 MB | 271,002,648 params; Q4_K almost everywhere |
| text, UD-Q5_K_XL (Unsloth) | 210.1 MB | Q5_K, with Q6_K on `attn_v` and `ffn_down` in 12 of the 24 layers |
| text, UD-Q6_K_XL (Unsloth) | 248.8 MB | Q6_K throughout |
| text, Q8_0 (both) | 309.9 MB | Q8_0, the per-layer projection kept in BF16 |
| text, BF16 (both) | 558.0 MB | unquantised |
| mmproj, Q8_0 (both) | 554.8 MB | 473,369,344 params: vision 167.4M, audio 304.8M, two projections 1.2M |
| mmproj, BF16 (both) | 982.1 MB | unquantised |

The text file is exactly the 271M path from the safetensors header: token table, 24 blocks, the
projection-only PLE (`per_layer_model_proj`, 512 x 12,288) and the 512-to-768 output head, stored
as `output.weight`. There is no per-layer token table in it, which confirms the reading above from
a second, independent implementation; llama.cpp's graph comments the function "this model has no
per-layer token embeddings, the per-layer inputs come only from the projection". The header also
answers the window question from earlier: it stores `sliding_window = 1024`, and llama.cpp's
symmetric mask divides it by two, masking anything more than 512 positions away on either side.
Same window, written down two ways.

Two things in the files surprised me. About 15.8 MB of every text GGUF, 9% of the Q4 file, is
not weights at all: it is the header, holding the 262,144-token vocabulary and 514,906 BPE merges.
And the Q4_K_XL is less exotic than the "UD" label suggests. Every large matrix, the embedding
table included, is plain Q4_K; what gets more bits are the small pieces, the per-layer gate and
projection in every block (Q8_0), the PLE projection (Q5_K) and the output head (Q6_K). For a
model whose last step is one 512 x 768 matrix that every embedding passes through, protecting
that matrix is the sensible choice. The quantisation was calibrated with an importance matrix
over 230 chunks of a text file (`quantize.imatrix.dataset = data/calib.txt` in the metadata),
which is fine for a text index and says nothing either way about how image or audio tokens fare,
since images and audio pass through the same quantised blocks.

On the multimodal side there is only one mmproj, and it holds both towers. I checked what
llama.cpp does with that: `clip_init` builds a vision context if the file has a vision encoder
and an audio context if it has an audio encoder, with no switch to skip either (the only
exception in the code is Gemma 3n). So the four configurations from the model card collapse to
two in this runtime, text only or everything. The 440M "text and vision" setup doesn't exist in
GGUF form; you carry the 305M audio tower whether you use it or not. The smallest mmproj is Q8_0,
and even that one is 70.5 MB of F32, most of it the vision tower's 768 x 10,240 x 2 position
table (62.9 MB), upcast from the BF16 it was stored in.

### The arithmetic for 0.5 GB

For an embedding model llama.cpp builds no KV cache (`build_attn_inp_no_cache` in the
`gemma-embedding2` graph): every input is read once, in one batch. So the memory is the weights
plus whatever the forward pass needs at once, and the forward pass has one large resident:
the per-layer inputs. They are computed for every position before the first layer runs and kept
until the last, 24 layers x 512 values x 4 bytes, which is 49,152 bytes per token.

At the 2,048-token context Unsloth's guide uses, that tensor is 100.7 MB. Add the 175.7 MB Q4
file, and the scratch for one layer (the 2,048-wide feed-forward activations are about 34 MB; the
global layers' attention scores, if flash attention is off, are 4 heads x 2,048 x 2,048 x 4 bytes,
67 MB), and the total comes out around 0.4 GB. So for text and code, at that context, "0.5 GB" is
believable. At the full 8,192 tokens the per-layer inputs alone are 402.7 MB, and a text-only Q4
run is already past half a gigabyte before anything else.

With pictures and sound it isn't close. The smallest files that can embed an image are the Q4
text model plus the Q8_0 mmproj, 730.5 MB on disk before a single activation. The figure that
does fit the full model is Google's own: about 567 MB of active RAM for the full multimodal model
on a Pixel 11 Pro, but that is the LiteRT bundle, whose audio encoder is partly int2, not a GGUF.

So I'd read Unsloth's 0.5 GB as true of the 271M text configuration at a modest context, and of
Google's LiteRT build for the whole model. Through Unsloth's own files, the whole model needs
about 0.73 GB before it starts. These are sums from the headers and the graph code; I didn't run
llama.cpp here, so the scratch sizes are an estimate of what its allocator holds, not a
measurement.

One more thing the repo contains that I wouldn't use: F16 copies of both files. The model card
says EmbeddingGemma 2's activations exceed float16's range and come back as NaN or silently
degraded, and Unsloth's own guide repeats "Do not use FP16". In ggml's CPU path an F16 weight
matrix is multiplied against activations converted to F16, so the F16 GGUF is the one file where
that warning plausibly applies. Take BF16 or Q8_0.

### Running it

Unsloth's llama.cpp recipe serves text and code only. The part worth copying is the flags:

```bash
# unsloth.ai/docs/models/embeddinggemma-2, "Run EmbeddingGemma 2 GGUFs in llama.cpp"
./llama.cpp/build/bin/llama-server \
  --model "embeddinggemma2-gguf/embeddinggemma-2-UD-Q4_K_XL.gguf" \
  --alias embeddinggemma2 \
  --embeddings \
  --pooling mean \
  --ctx-size 2048 \
  --batch-size 2048 \
  --ubatch-size 2048 \
  --parallel 1 \
  --host 127.0.0.1 \
  --port 8080
```

`--pooling mean` matches the sentence-transformers pooling; the GGUF also carries
`pooling_type = 1`, which is mean. The three sizes have to move together because a non-causal
model can't split one input across batches: every token attends to every other, so an input has
to fit in one physical batch. And the server does nothing about prompts. The guide's request
writes them by hand, `task: search result | query: ...` for the query and `title: none | text: ...`
for the document, and that is required: as noted above, the prompt is part of the mean.

The guide's closing hint is honest about the rest: "For images, video or audio, use a runtime
with explicit support for those encoders and their processor files." I didn't check whether
llama-server's embeddings endpoint feeds an mmproj today. If you need media embeddings outside
Python, LiteRT is the path Google tested.

Unsloth also wired EmbeddingGemma 2 into Unsloth Desktop as the embedding model for its
document chat. The settings screenshot in the guide labels it "Not downloaded · 135 MB". That
matches none of the files: the smallest GGUF is 175.7 MB and the safetensors is 1.49 GB. I
couldn't find which artefact it refers to.

## Fine-tuning moves every modality at once

Unsloth's guide trains the text model with LoRA. It saves the text-only configuration first, then
loads it in 4-bit and attaches adapters to every attention and MLP projection:

```python
# unsloth.ai/docs/models/embeddinggemma-2, "Prepare the model"
model = FastSentenceTransformer.from_pretrained(
    model_name = "embeddinggemma2-text",
    max_seq_length = 1024,
    dtype = torch.bfloat16,
    load_in_4bit = True,
    load_in_16bit = False,
    full_finetuning = False,
)

model = FastSentenceTransformer.get_peft_model(
    model,
    r = 16,
    target_modules = [
        "q_proj", "k_proj", "v_proj", "o_proj",
        "gate_proj", "up_proj", "down_proj",
    ],
    lora_alpha = 32,
    lora_dropout = 0,
    bias = "none",
    use_gradient_checkpointing = "unsloth",
    random_state = 3407,
    task_type = "FEATURE_EXTRACTION",
)
```

Training is sentence-transformers' `SentenceTransformerTrainer` with
`MultipleNegativesRankingLoss`, which is the in-batch-negatives contrastive loss from the training
section above, a no-duplicates batch sampler (so a repeated query never becomes its own negative),
`bf16=True` and the query and document prefixes applied to the training columns. The guide's
advice to hold out queries and compare before and after is the part people skip and shouldn't.

Two remarks on the recipe. Loading a 271M model in 4-bit saves about 0.4 GB of weights, which is
small next to the activations at a training batch; the text notebook's own log puts peak reserved
memory at 11.3 GB on a T4. Plain 16-bit LoRA costs little more here and trains against the weights
you will actually deploy. And the adapters don't reach the per-layer gates, the PLE projection or
the 512-to-768 head, which is fine for adapting a domain.

The bigger point follows from the architecture. An image embedding is the text encoder's average
over the image's soft tokens, and so is an audio one. LoRA on `q_proj` through `down_proj` of the
text model therefore changes the vector for every photo and recording too, even though the
training data was only text. The "embeddings you have already computed do not need to be
re-computed" property from the modular section holds for adding towers, not for training the
reader: after a fine-tune, everything in the index has to be re-embedded, and the image-text
alignment has been nudged by a loss that never saw an image. If the index is mixed, train on
mixed pairs, or keep the fine-tuned model for text and test cross-modal recall before swapping it
in.

Unsloth does have mixed-pair notebooks. The image one trains on 3,000 Flickr8k images with five
captions each and tests on the Flickr30k 1K split, adding `finetune_vision_layers = True` so the
vision tower gets adapters too; the audio one trains on Clotho clips with
`finetune_audio_layers = True`. Note what the second does to the claim about sharing an audio
encoder with Gemma 4: once those adapters are merged, the tower is no longer byte-identical to
Gemma 4 E2B's, and a runtime that loads it once for both models can no longer do so.

None of the three notebooks shows an EmbeddingGemma 2 result yet. The image and audio ones have no
saved outputs. The text one does, but they come from the earlier model: its log reads "Fast
Gemma3 patching" with transformers 4.57.3, and "Trainable parameters = 13,074,432 of 315,937,536".
Rank-32 LoRA on EmbeddingGemma 1's 24 layers (768 wide, 1,152 MLP) is 8,355,840 parameters, and
its two dense head layers, 768 x 3,072 each, add 4,718,592; together that is exactly the
13,074,432. The NDCG@10 of 0.9267 before and 0.9351 after on the medical set are EmbeddingGemma 1
numbers, in a notebook titled for EmbeddingGemma 2.

## Typed Decision: a decision head on the 271M

*Added 12 October 2026.*

Five days after launch, Manuel Romero (mrm8488) published
[Typed Decision](https://huggingface.co/mrm8488/embeddinggemma-2-ft-typed-decision), the text

a level on a rubric, or a yes/no probability, the three System One primitives this site has
followed since [Jev](/articles/reproducing-jev). His
[post](https://x.com/mrm8488/status/2109406679045423394) put it at "76.4% on typed-decisions,
~6 pts behind a 27B LLM with ~100× fewer params". One of the first replies asked the obvious
question: the AI Edge post above already sells EmbeddingGemma 2 as a zero-shot "decision engine",
so what does the fine-tune add? The answer is in the code, and it's a nice small design.

/>

The state, each question and each option are embedded separately by the same 271M text encoder,
with the `task: classification | query: ` prompt and mean pooling. Before embedding, the state is
flattened from JSON into `key: value` lines, so `{"trace_summary": {"constraint_violations": 1}}`
becomes `trace summary · constraint violations: 1`, which an embedding model reads far better than
braces. Every option is embedded as a whole sentence,
`question: <instructions> | answer: <key>: <description>`; a score level becomes `level 2 of 3: significant risk`, and a yes/no question is
just two options whose answers are "no" and "yes". Then a head of about a million parameters turns
those vectors into one logit per option:

```python
# modeling_gemmadecision.py:210-222
S, Q, O = self.state_proj(s), self.question_proj(q), self.option_proj(o)
f = self.field_norm(self.field(torch.cat([S, Q, S * Q], -1)) + self.type_emb(kind))
On = self.option_norm(O)
...
joint = self.log_joint_scale.clamp(max=cap).exp() * F.cosine_similarity(f, On, dim=-1)
residual = self.residual(torch.cat([f, On, f * On, (f - On).abs()], -1)).squeeze(-1)

anchor = F.normalize(s + q, dim=-1).unsqueeze(-1)
prior = self.log_prior_scale.clamp(max=cap).exp() * (F.normalize(o, dim=-1) @ anchor).squeeze(-1)

logits = prior + torch.sigmoid(self.gate) * (joint + residual)
```

If that formula looks familiar, it is Cloudflare's Clef joint-schema head, the one
[Unsloth vendored](/articles/unsloth-train-a-decision-model#what-a-clef-head-is): a fixed `prior`,
plus a gated sum of a learned cosine and a small MLP over the usual matching features. The
difference is what feeds it. Clef reads an LLM's hidden states, where each option's tokens can
attend to the whole prompt. Here every text is pooled into one 768-wide vector before the head
sees it, so the option never reads the state; the only place they meet is a 256-wide layer in
the head. And the `prior` term is exactly the reply's answer: the cosine between an option's
embedding and the normalised sum of state and question is what an untrained embedding model does
when you use it as a zero-shot classifier. The fine-tune keeps that and learns a correction on
top.

I read the trained scalars out of the safetensors file with range requests. The prior's scale is
20.5 and the joint scale 11.0, both close to where they were initialised (20 and 10). The gate went
from -2.0 to -1.92, so the learned term is still multiplied by σ(-1.92), about 0.13. The residual
MLP's output is unbounded, so I can't say from the weights alone how big the correction gets on a
real input. But the card's own numbers suggest where the gain came from. Training the head alone on
frozen embeddings scored 67.7%; unfreezing the encoder lifted it to 76.4%. Most of the work went
into making the encoder's cosines sharper, with a head that barely opened its gate.

The header totals 272,120,354 values: the 271.0M encoder (half of it, 134.6M, is the token
embedding table, which phase 2 kept frozen), a head of 1,117,700 parameters, and the three
temperatures. The card says the head is "~0.5M"; the file says about twice that.

There's a deployment property here that the card doesn't mention. A case in the test split needs
22 to 26 texts embedded (one state, five questions, their options), and the test split has only
four distinct schemas. Everything except the state is the same for every case of a workflow, so a
server could embed the schema once and pay one 271M forward pass per new state. The shipped
`decide()` doesn't cache across calls, it re-embeds the schema each time, but the design allows it,
which an LLM-backed head never can.

### Checking the numbers

The 76.4% is on the right split: `LocalLLaMA/typed-decisions`, subset `all`, the 400-case test set,
argmax against the gold label. The per-type figures add up. Weighting 71.2% on 600 choice
decisions, 74.0% on 800 score decisions and 84.8% on 600 yes/no decisions gives 1,528 of 2,000,
which is 76.4%. Score questions are the weakest, and the card says why: numbers inside the state
are hard for an embedding model.

Two qualifiers belong next to it. It was trained on that benchmark's `train` split, so it belongs
in the leaderboard's second table, the fitted models, not next to zero-shot rows like Jev's 0.727.
And the card says plainly that "hyperparameters were chosen while looking at the test split, so
treat these numbers as slightly optimistic". I respect that sentence; few cards carry it.

The 27B LLM isn't named anywhere on the card. The only 27B on the benchmark's Hub leaderboard is
[Darwin-27B-ZTC-v5](https://huggingface.co/FINAL-Bench/Darwin-27B-ZTC-v5), released on 11 October, the
same day, at 0.812, and also fine-tuned on `train`. Against that the gap is 4.8 points, not six.
The Hub counts 25.6B parameters in it, about 94 times this model's 272M, so "~100×" is fair. Its zero-shot sibling,
Darwin-27B-ZTC, scores 0.743, which this model beats; the comparison only flatters the 27B when you
pick the fine-tuned one, which is the right one to pick.

Then there's the Laya row this site has quoted since [the relational-choice
piece](/articles/jev-scores-zero): "Laya (fine-tuned), 2,000 typed decisions, 0.766". Same
benchmark, same test split, same `train`-fitted table, same accuracy definition, both
self-reported. So the two are directly comparable, and Typed Decision is 0.764 to Laya's 0.766,
four decisions out of 2,000. It isn't the same number travelling under a new name; Laya is a 421M
ModernBERT-large, this is EmbeddingGemma 2. It's a coincidence, and a gap that size is noise. In
the same fitted table soft-decider-421m reports 0.774 and OpenDecider-nano, a 400M encoder, 0.796.
Typed Decision is a respectable mid-table entry for its size, not a new high.

### What "calibrated" rests on

The card reports a soft cross-entropy of 0.887 and a Brier score of 0.065, against 0.968 and 0.113
for the frozen-encoder phase. It reports no ECE and no KL. Calibration in the training recipe is one
temperature per question type, fitted on held-out training rows, and the three stored in the file
are 1.005 for choice, 1.144 for score and 1.056 for yes/no: the fitted model was already close to
calibrated on held-out rows and needed only a slight softening of its score answers.

The benchmark's leaderboard ranks KL from gold, not cross-entropy, but one converts to the other.
KL is cross-entropy minus the gold distribution's own entropy, and I computed the mean entropy of
the 2,000 test gold distributions: 0.767 nats. If the card's 0.887 is a mean over the same 2,000
decisions in nats, the KL is about 0.12. That puts it level with Aplomb 1 (KL 0.123, Brier 0.065 on
its own submission) and behind the best fitted rows, which sit near 0.08 KL and 0.043 Brier. The
Brier looks like the leaderboard's definition, squared error summed over options: the code's loss
sums it that way, and the same definition reproduces the leaderboard's uniform reference of 0.238
on the test gold. I couldn't check the evaluation script itself; it isn't in the repo.

The card's limitations section makes the point I'd want every decision-model card to make: on this
benchmark "calibration means 'matches annotator disagreement'". The gold is the mean of three
samples from a teacher, so a 43% answer where the teacher split 43/28/25 is correct, and the
model is being calibrated against a roughly 4B-class teacher's uncertainty, not against the world.
The benchmark's own README adds that its teacher only agrees with itself 73.5% of the time, which
76.4% is already above.

Would I use it? For a fixed set of workflows on a phone or a CPU, yes, ahead of a prompted LLM: one
forward pass per state, typed output that can't fail to parse, and Apache 2.0 like the base. The
multilingual claim is backed by one probe, cosine between English/Spanish translation pairs moving
from 0.869 to 0.901, which shows the encoder didn't forget Spanish, not that the decisions transfer;
the card itself says the head was trained on English schemas only. I would not expect it to answer
a question where one option's meaning depends on another; with every option pooled on its own, it
has the same blind spot [Jev showed on relational choice](/articles/jev-scores-zero).

## An embedding model that transcribes, and what it actually runs

*Added 13 October 2026.*

On 12 October Shahir (@TslShahir), an on-device ML engineer, [posted](https://x.com/TslShahir/status/2109660368381395414) that he
had "turned EmbeddingGemma 2, an embedding model, into a speech-to-text model", and that on a base
M3 MacBook Air it "transcribes an hour of audio in about 13 seconds: 288× real time". The pitch is
the one Google makes for the model: a spoken word and a written word land next to each other in the
shared space, so you ought to be able to read a transcript straight out of it. His follow-up post
gives the recipe in one line. The model stays untouched, and "a small reader on top takes each slice
of audio, finds the nearest of ~229,000 words in the model's own text space, and strings them
together."

My first reaction was that this can't work as described. Embedding a 20-second clip with
EmbeddingGemma 2 gives you one pooled vector, and one vector can't hold a sentence. So the reader has
to be getting at something before the pooling. The code isn't public yet ("Soon", he replied when
asked), but the free Mac app downloads its models from a public manifest, and Core ML packages
describe themselves. I read those instead.

### Four files and a dictionary

The current manifest (version 5, 714,975,724 bytes, which is the "715 MB download") holds four
networks and one big table. I sized each from its Core ML metadata and weights file:

| File | What it is | Size on disk |
|---|---|---|
| `eg2_audio_l11` | EmbeddingGemma 2's 12-layer audio conformer, 2,000 mel frames in, 500 vectors of 1,024 out | 227 MB, 6-bit palettes |
| `reader_P6` | a 12-layer, 768-wide transformer, 86.45M parameters by its own config | 66 MB, 6-bit palettes |
| `word_unit_f16.bin` | 229,457 words × 768 dimensions, float16, unit length | 352 MB |
| `scorer_blk_d128…` | a 768-to-128 projection and a 128-wide copy of the word table, for shortlisting | 59 MB |
| `punct_headonly` | punctuation and capitalisation from the word vectors | 7 MB |

The 352 MB is exactly 229,457 × 768 × 2 bytes, so half the download is a dictionary, kept at
16-bit. The "6-bit" in his reply about resource use covers the two networks, not the whole thing.

Then the part I'd been suspicious of. The encoder is cut at its last conformer layer: the graph's
output is called `l11` and is 1,024 wide, and nothing in it is 1,536 wide, so the tower's output
projection isn't there. Nor is `embed_audio`, the 1,536-to-512 layer that hands audio to the text
encoder, nor the 271M text encoder itself. The parts of EmbeddingGemma 2 that put sound *into* the
shared space aren't in the app. The reader takes the conformer's raw 40 ms frames (500 per 20-second
window, the same 25 a second as in [Spending the window](#spending-the-window)), runs full attention
across all 500 of them, and emits, for each frame, a 768-wide vector and a single "blank" score.

That shape is CTC, the non-autoregressive recipe behind most fast recognisers: every 40 ms slice
either says "nothing new here" (blank) or names a word, and a cheap pass collapses the repeats. The
twist is the output layer. A normal CTC head learns a weight row per vocabulary item. Here the rows
are frozen: they are the 229,457 unit vectors in `word_unit_f16.bin`, and the reader's config
carries a temperature of 39.86 and an offset of -0.46 to turn cosines into scores. The dictionary
even has a `<rep>` entry, which looks like a marker for a word said twice in a row. Of all 229,457
entries only one contains a digit (`dev2`), so numbers come out spelled.

Why the temperature matters is visible in the table itself. I pulled a few rows with range requests.
"cat" sits 0.944 from "kitten" and 0.872 from "dog", but also 0.73 from "the": everything in this
space is close to everything. The homophones the reader must split are closer still, "two" at 0.869
from "too" and 0.845 from "to". Multiplied by 39.86, the 0.07 between kitten and dog becomes about
2.9 logits, enough for a softmax to pick a winner. The vectors behave like text embeddings, which
fits his description, but I didn't run EmbeddingGemma 2's text encoder to confirm they came out of
it.

Scoring 500 frames against 229,457 words in full would be a 500 × 768 × 229,457 matrix multiply per
window. The scorer does it in 128 dimensions instead (a learned projection, not a Matryoshka cut; it
isn't the identity on the first 128 columns), groups the words into blocks of four, and keeps the 16
best blocks per frame. That leaves 64 candidates to rescore at full width, which he says happens on
the GPU while the networks sit on the Neural Engine.

### Untouched, as far as I can tell

The app's NOTICE says "EmbeddingGemma 2's audio encoder was fine-tuned". The post says it stayed
untouched. The weights side with the post. The encoder's normalisation vectors are stored as plain
float16, so I compared four of them in each of layers 0, 5 and 11 against `google/embeddinggemma-2`:
identical, every value. The big matrices are stored as 6-bit indices into small lookup tables, so I unpacked
the query projections of layers 0 and 11, the value projection of layer 6 and the second
feed-forward matrix of layer 11 and compared them too. Also identical, with zero error.

Zero error from a 6-bit palette surprised me until I looked at Google's weights. Each row of those
matrices holds at most four distinct values; the audio tower already shipped quantised that hard, so
a 64-entry table per 16 rows loses nothing. And since I showed [above](#the-audio-encoder-is-gemma-4s-byte-for-byte)
that this tower is Gemma 4's, byte for byte, the encoder in this app is Gemma 4's speech encoder.

That changes how I read the headline. Gemma 4's audio tower was trained to feed a language model
that transcribes speech, so the frames coming out of layer 11 should already carry what was said. What the app proves is that an 86M reader trained in under two hours (his figure, on
8×H100) can decode those frames into a fixed word list, and that EmbeddingGemma 2's text space makes
a good fixed word list. It doesn't show that the pooled embedding contains a transcript. "An
embedding model turned into speech-to-text" is true of the download; "read straight out of the
embedding model" is a nicer story than the graph tells.

I still think it's a clever piece of engineering. A frozen 229,457-way output layer means the
vocabulary is just a file: add a word by embedding it, no retraining. That's also why it can be
fine-tuned in minutes, as he says it can, on an hour of your own audio. Only the 86M reader moves.

### The 288×

The arithmetic doesn't close at first. An hour in about 13 seconds is 3,600 ÷ 13, about 277×, not
288×. The site that hosts the app has the exact figure, "1 hour in 12.6 s", and 3,600 ÷ 12.6 is
286×, still short. The clip settles it. The test file is `speckled-band-librivox-1h.wav`, and it is
60 minutes 30 seconds long: 3,630 ÷ 12.6 = 288.1. So the 288× is honest; the "hour" is 30 seconds
longer than an hour and the 13 is rounded. The progress bar also counts 202 windows for that file,
one every 18 seconds of audio, which suggests 20-second windows with about 2 seconds of overlap.

Two things in the same material don't line up as neatly. The run in the clip isn't the 12.6-second
one; it finishes in 15.7 seconds at 231×, which is 3,630 ÷ 15.7 again. A screen recording running
at the same time would explain the gap, but that's my guess. And the speed table's comparison row,
IBM Granite Speech 5.0 TurboCTC on the same Air, reads "~150×" over "1 hour in 25.7 s", which is
141× on 3,630 seconds and 140× on 3,600. The "about twice as fast" holds either way (25.7 ÷ 12.6 is
2.04). The three Parakeet rows come from FluidAudio's published benchmarks on M4 Pro and M5 Pro
machines with different audio, and the table's own footnote calls them "a rough guide, not a
like-for-like ranking", which is right.

None of the speed figures is something I could check: I have no Mac here, and the app is a binary.
Neither is the accuracy. In replies he gave 5.18% word error rate on LibriSpeech test-clean and

won't rank them. What I'd say is that the speed comes mostly from being CTC on the Neural Engine,
with no decoder loop, about 62 ms per 20-second window if you divide 12.6 s by 202, and less from the
embedding model.

## 22 October: Google reposts the transcriber, and an open copy runs

*Added 23 October 2026.*

On 22 October Google's Gemma account [asked](https://x.com/googlegemma/status/2113294644222259486)
"Can you use EmbeddingGemma 2 for Speech-to-Text?" and answered with Shahir's app from [the section
above](#an-embedding-model-that-transcribes-and-what-it-actually-runs): the experiment "uses
EmbeddingGemma 2's unified audio-text vector space to match speech against text via vector
similarity", and transcribes "an hour of audio in about 13 seconds". The second post in the thread
links Shahir's breakdown. Nothing new is released by Google. The 26-second video is Shahir's own
promo, the same 60 min 30 s `speckled-band-librivox-1h.wav`, with "not affiliated with Google" in
its last frame; its counter's last visible reading is 13.4 s, which is 271× on 3,630 seconds
(reasoned). So the 288×, the 5% word error rate and the Mac-only binary are still his claims, and I
still can't run them.

What changed since 13 October is that someone else rebuilt it in the open.
[eg2-transcribe](https://github.com/john-rocky/eg2-transcribe) (Apache-2.0) calls itself "an
independent reproduction for phones" of Shahir's idea, with training code, an Android app on LiteRT,
an iOS and Mac app, and the weights on Hugging Face.

<RepoCard repo="john-rocky/eg2-transcribe" />

The recipe is the one I reverse-engineered from Shahir's app, with one difference in where it taps
the model. The audio tower runs frozen, and its output goes through `embed_audio` to the 512-wide
"soft tokens" that would normally be fed to the text encoder, 25 a second. A 6-layer, 19.6M-parameter
transformer (reader v2) turns each token into a 768-wide unit vector and a blank score; the logits are
a learned scale (216.35) times the cosine to each of 200,000 frozen word vectors, the LibriSpeech
lexicon embedded by EmbeddingGemma 2 itself with the query prompt. Greedy CTC, no language model. The
repo reports 4.21% WER on LibriSpeech test-clean and 10.42% on test-other, trained on the 960 hours
of LibriSpeech on Colab A100s (reported). Shahir's reader reads layer 11 instead, before `embed_audio`;
both skip the text encoder at inference.

<ModelCard repo="mlboydaisuke/EmbeddingGemma-2-Transcribe-Reader" />

This one I could run. On a shared 4-core cloud VM (Intel Xeon at 2.8 GHz, two threads, CPU only,
float32, transformers 5.19.0), with the released reader and `google/embeddinggemma-2`:

- **The repo's numbers reproduce exactly** (measured). On its 20 test-clean fixture utterances I got
  3.70% WER, 18 substitutions, 1 deletion and 1 insertion over 541 words, the same counts as its
  `RELEASE.md`, with the same errors ("titian holborn" for Holbein, "kio" for chiaroscurists, a word
  that isn't in the 200,000). On 60 test-clean utterances, 1,314 words, 4.19%, beside its 4.21% for
  the whole set.
- **Slow on a CPU** (measured). 477 seconds of audio took 364 seconds, 1.3× real time: 0.34 s per
  audio second in the encoder and 0.42 s in the reader, most of that scoring every frame against
  200,000 words. Peak memory was 3.6 GB once I dropped the unused text encoder. The repo's 548× is an
  M4 Max with the reader on the Neural Engine (reported); the speed is the accelerator's, as I
  argued above.

Then the question Google's wording raises. Does the shared space match speech to text *without*
the trained reader? Partly, and this surprised me (all measured on the 20 fixtures):

- My own text pipeline reproduces the repo's word table to a cosine of 1.0000, so the words really
  are EmbeddingGemma 2 text embeddings, the thing I couldn't confirm for Shahir's table.
- Whole-clip retrieval works: each clip's pooled audio embedding picks its own transcript out of 60,
  20 out of 20 (cosine 0.86 against 0.62 for the others).
- The pooled vector is a topic, not a transcript. Its ten nearest words average 1.35 that occur in
  the clip, and they are clusters like "stewed, stew, stews" or "uncontrollable, uncontroll'd".
- A reader with no trained weights at all, pooling 15 soft tokens (0.6 s) through the text encoder,
  hopping 8, taking the nearest word and merging repeats, gives 80.6% WER. That sounds useless until
  you read it: "he'e hoped would'st stoved dinner turn's turnip carr's carrots brews pottered
  potatoes … thick pepper peppered flowered flower fattens sauce" for "he hoped there would be stew
  for dinner turnips and carrots and bruised potatoes … thick peppered flour fattened sauce".

So Google's sentence is closer to true than I gave Shahir's headline credit for: short stretches of
speech do land near their words. What the 19.6M-parameter reader buys is timing and choice, which
40 ms frame owns which word and which of "carr's" and "carrots" was said, and it takes the error rate
from about 81% to about 4%. It also answers the reply asking whether you need the candidate text up
front: you need a vocabulary, not the transcript, and a word outside the 200,000 can never come out.

One more reply is worth a line. Mohamed Maslooh says the same recipe, with LoRA on the audio encoder
and a small CTC head, gives 14% WER on his own Egyptian Arabic at about 160× real time on an RTX
4060. He links no code or data, so it is unverified, but it is the only report so far of the idea
working outside English, and it needed the encoder unfrozen to get there.

## What I'd do with it

I'd use it for a personal or on-device index of mixed media: notes, screenshots, photos, voice
memos, a codebase. The text-first workflow is the real feature. Start with the 271M configuration
at 256 dimensions, which is small and keeps 98% to 99% of its text scores and about 97% on code, and add the vision
tower when images show up, without touching what is already indexed. If the index outgrows RAM,
I'd cut bits before cutting more dimensions; Qdrant's numbers favour 1 bit a dimension at a wider
width over a narrower vector with more bits.

I wouldn't pick it for a text-only search service where model size doesn't matter; there are
stronger text embedders at 600M. I'd test general-audio retrieval on my own data before relying
on it, since the audio tower is a speech encoder that didn't train here and MAEB is its weakest
benchmark. And I'd chunk long video and audio deliberately rather than trust the 8,192-token
window, because the default preprocessing already does a cruder version of that for you, silently.

Related reading on the site: [Gemma 4](/articles/gemma-4) for the backbone family and per-layer
embeddings, [SigLIP 2](/articles/siglip-2-coreml) for how a contrastive image-text tower is
trained and what a Core ML port of one measures, [Ovis-Embedding](/articles/ovis-embedding) for
the 3B omni embedder that reads its vector off the last token instead of averaging, and
[jev-semgrep](/articles/search-by-meaning) for the case against cosine similarity as a search
primitive at all.

## How I checked

- **Parameters.** Read the `model.safetensors` header from the Hub with two range requests (8
  bytes for the length, then the 171,296-byte JSON), summed every tensor's shape, and grouped by
  module prefix. Compared with the Hub API's `safetensors.total`.
- **Shared weights.** Fetched individual tensors' byte ranges from `google/embeddinggemma-2` and
  from `google/gemma-4-E2B`, `gemma-4-E2B-it` and `gemma-4-E4B`, and compared SHA-1 hashes: 36
  audio tensors and the audio output projection (all identical), eight vision tensors and the
  vision patch projection (all different). Compared the two `tokenizer.json` vocabularies entry by
  entry.
- **Mechanism.** Read `modeling_embedding_gemma2.py`, `processing_embedding_gemma2.py` and
  `video_processing_embedding_gemma2.py` from transformers' main branch as of 6 October 2026, plus
  Gemma 4's `feature_extraction_gemma4.py`, and the repo's `config.json`,
  `processor_config.json`, `1_Pooling/config.json` and `config_sentence_transformers.json`. Line
  numbers above refer to those files on that date. I did not run the model.
- **Charts.** Located each dot's centre in the launch blog's 1,000-pixel chart images and
  interpolated between the y-axis tick marks. Checked the axis against Qwen3-Embedding-0.6B's
  published MTEB code score. Parameter counts for jina-embeddings-v5-omni-nano and -small come from
  the Hub API's header totals.
- **GGUFs.** Parsed the header of every file in `unsloth/embeddinggemma-2-GGUF` and
  `ggml-org/embeddinggemma-2-GGUF` from range requests (the text headers are 15.8 MB, the mmproj
  ones under 60 KB), summed parameters and bytes per quantisation type, and hashed the last 4 MB of
  each file that both repos ship. Read llama.cpp's `src/models/gemma-embedding2.cpp`, the symmetric
  window mask in `llama-hparams.h` and `clip_init` in `tools/mtmd/clip.cpp` at the merge commit of
  pull request 30054. Compared `unsloth/embeddinggemma-2` with `google/embeddinggemma-2`: the
  safetensors and tokenizer have the same LFS hashes, so it is a mirror.
- **Unsloth guide and notebooks.** Read the guide's Markdown and the three EmbeddingGemma 2
  notebooks in `unslothai/notebooks`, including their saved outputs, and checked the text
  notebook's trainable-parameter count against EmbeddingGemma 1's shapes. I ran none of them.
- **Qdrant's storage numbers (added 7 October).** Read the Qdrant EmbeddingGemma 2 blog and its
  TurboQuant article, and rendered both charts in a headless browser to capture them. Derived the
  8-byte per-vector overhead from the blog's 104, 72 and 40-byte counts; the HNSW link figure is the
  layer-0 maximum for m = 16, an estimate, not a Qdrant number. Read the X thread and replies through
  the fxtwitter mirror and looked up arXiv 2608.30384's abstract page. I ran no Qdrant benchmark.
- **Typed Decision (added 12 October).** Read the model card, `config.json`,
  `modeling_gemmadecision.py` and `configuration_gemmadecision.py` from
  `mrm8488/embeddinggemma-2-ft-typed-decision`; summed the safetensors header's shapes and fetched the
  byte ranges of `temperatures`, `head.gate` and the two scale scalars. Read the X post, its replies
  through the fxtwitter mirror, discussions 2, 10 and 17 on `LocalLLaMA/typed-decisions`, the
  dataset README and the Hub leaderboard API for that benchmark. Pulled the 400 test rows from the
  datasets-server API to compute the mean gold entropy, the uniform Brier reference and the texts per
  case. I did not run the model, and the KL of about 0.12 is derived, not reported.
- **EG2 Transcribe (added 13 October).** Read the post, its thread and the replies through the
  fxtwitter mirror. Unpacked the app's DMG with 7-Zip without running it and read the NOTICE and the
  binary's strings. Fetched manifests v1 to v5 from `haku-models.tslfiles.org/eg2demo/`, and from the
  current one the Core ML `metadata.json` and `model.mil` of the encoder, reader, scorer and
  punctuation model, the reader's JSON config and the word list; sizes come from the manifest. Pulled
  eleven rows of `word_unit_f16.bin` and the scorer's projection with range requests. Compared 12
  float16 norm vectors and four palettized matrices (unpacked from their 6-bit indices and lookup
  tables) against the same tensors in `google/embeddinggemma-2`. Read the speed figures off the
  thread's table, the app's web page and frames of the clip. I ran neither the app nor any model, so
  the speed, memory and word error rates are his.
- **awesome-EmbeddingGemma (added 13 October).** Cloned the list and the six application repos it
  links (DigUp, Recall, VectorDash, SemanticSearchMCP, embeddingGemma2-jeff, embeddinggemma2-search)
  and read their READMEs, plus DigUp's `ModelFiles.swift`, `Model.swift` and `Searcher.swift`. The
  two screenshots are single frames of their README animations. I ran none of them.
- **Google's repost and eg2-transcribe (added 23 October).** Read the post, its thread and replies
  through the fxtwitter mirror and pulled frames of its video. Cloned `john-rocky/eg2-transcribe` at
  `e9c1c26` and read its README, `reader/RELEASE.md`, `reader_config.json` and the training, decoding
  and soft-token code; checked the Hugging Face reader files against their `SHA256SUMS`. Ran the
  released reader v2 with `google/embeddinggemma-2` in float32 on a shared 4-core Xeon VM (two
  threads, CPU only, torch 2.14.1, transformers 5.19.0, whose default audio attention the repo
  requires; 5.20.2 refused it) on the 20 fixture utterances and 40 more from the start of the
  LibriSpeech test-clean shard the repo names, scoring lowercased words with jiwer. For the zero-shot
  checks I ran the text encoder on hand-built `<|audio>` sequences and mean-pooled, which reproduces
  the word table to cosine 1.0. Downloads were about 2.2 GB and the scratch directory peaked at
  3.7 GB; all of it was deleted afterwards. I did not run Shahir's app, the phone apps, or anything on
  an accelerator.
- **Not checked.** Anything about the training recipe beyond what the files imply; on-device
  quality at 70 vision tokens; whether vLLM or LiteRT apply the 280-token or the 750-token audio
  cap; the 191 MB and 567 MB memory figures; the real resident memory of llama.cpp with these
  GGUFs (the 0.4 GB is a sum, not a reading); whether llama-server embeds images or audio through
  an mmproj; what the 135 MB in Unsloth Desktop refers to.
