Five things crossed my feed in the same few days of November 2026, and they turned out to be one subject seen from
five heights. A ROS 2 SLAM system that keeps a building's floors apart. A GNSS library that gets a car's antenna to
centimetres. A tool that tells you whether the transform between your LiDAR and your IMU is still right. A fifty-dollar
time-of-flight board stuck to an iPhone. A Livox Mid-360 plugged into an Android phone. All five are answers to the
question every mapping pipeline asks before it can do anything else: where was the sensor when it took this
measurement, and how sure are we?

I cloned the five repositories involved, read the parts that do the work and checked the claims against the code, the configs
and the datasheets. I did not build or run any of them; where a number comes from a project's own benchmark, I say so in
the sentence. The arithmetic in between is mine, and the two widgets below are toys I wrote to show the mechanism, not
ports of anyone's solver.

If you work on LiDAR SLAM, the first section is the one to read: StrataSLAM's answer to perceptual aliasing is a
structural one, and it is more interesting than its README makes it sound.

## StrataSLAM keeps one map per floor and refuses everything else

[StrataSLAM](https://github.com/EasyNavigation/StrataSLAM) is a multi-floor 3D SLAM system for ROS 2 Jazzy from José
Miguel Guerrero at Universidad Rey Juan Carlos, released as v0.2.0 under Apache-2.0 at the start of November (the
citation file says 2 November; the repository was created on 3 November and had 41 stars when I looked on 12 November).
It takes an RGB-D camera, a 3D LiDAR, both, or a 2D laser scanner, with an IMU, a barometer and wheel odometry as
optional extras, and it writes one point cloud per floor plus a global one. The README's one-line pitch is "one point
cloud per floor and a global one, without the floors collapsing into each other". I read it at commit `ec705b1`
(10 November).

### Why identical floors collapse

Loop closure is the step that makes SLAM more than odometry. The front end proposes "I have been here before" by
comparing a descriptor of the current scan or image against a database of past keyframes: a Scan Context ring
descriptor for LiDAR, a bag of visual words for a camera. A verification step registers the two scans, and if the fit is
good, the back end adds a relative-pose factor between the two keyframes and re-solves the graph. That factor is
precise. Two scans of the same corridor registered with GICP agree to a few centimetres, which is exactly why one good
loop closure can pull a whole drifted trajectory back into shape (the [GTSAM article](/articles/gtsam-4-3) has a loop you
can close by hand).

Now put the robot in an office block where floor 2 has the same corridor, the same doors and the same lift lobby as
floor 1. The place recognizer is doing its job when it says floor 2's lobby looks like floor 1's lobby: the descriptors
are the same because the geometry is the same. Verification passes too, because the scans really do register. The
back end receives a precise, internally consistent, completely wrong constraint saying two keyframes three metres apart
vertically are the same place. Repeat that at every lobby and corridor, and the optimizer does the only thing least
squares can do: it moves floor 2 down onto floor 1, because dozens of centimetre-precise loop factors outvote one sloppy
stair measurement. That is perceptual aliasing, and it is a failure of the problem formulation, not of any component.

You can watch it happen in a stripped-down version. Floor 1's height $h$ is the only unknown. The stairs say
$h = 3.0$ m with $\sigma = 0.15$ m, which is the z standard deviation StrataSLAM assigns to a stair or ramp transition
(its ADR-0015). Every accepted cross-floor loop says $h = 0$ with the registration's $\sigma_\ell$. The least-squares
estimate is a weighted mean:

$$
\hat h \;=\; \frac{h_{\text{stairs}}/\sigma_t^2 \;+\; \sum_{k=1}^{n} 0/\sigma_\ell^2}{1/\sigma_t^2 + n/\sigma_\ell^2},
\qquad
\sigma_{\hat h} = \Big(\tfrac{1}{\sigma_t^2} + \tfrac{n}{\sigma_\ell^2}\Big)^{-1/2}.
$$

With $\sigma_\ell = 0.05$ m one false loop has weight 400 against the stairs' 44.4, so it already outvotes the stairs
nine to one and puts floor 1 at 0.30 m. A barometer at $\sigma = 0.05$ m slows the slide, since it adds a second honest
vote, but it is one vote, and the loops keep coming.

The widget's default is the number StrataSLAM's own negative control produces. Scenario S04 is S03 (two identical
floors joined by stairs, camera only, no IMU) run with the floor gate switched off by a debug flag. Its report in
`docs/scenarios.md` lists 41 accepted loops, 35 cross-floor candidates, one floor instead of two, an ATE of 1.46 m and a
collapse rate of 0.853659. The collapse rate in `metrics.cpp` is the share of accepted loops whose two ends are on
different floors, and 35 / 41 = 0.853659, so every one of the 35 cross-floor candidates was accepted, alongside six loops on the right floor. With the gate on
(S03, same building, same seed) the run has 8 loops, all on-floor, two floors, an ATE of 0.022 m and zero cross-floor
candidates. Zero candidates, not zero accepted: with the gate on, the recognizer never even proposes one, for a reason
that comes up below. The test harness fails the run if the negative control does *not* collapse, which is the right
way round: a test that cannot see the bug cannot prove the fix.

### Noticing that you changed floor

Refusing cross-floor loops only works if every keyframe carries the right floor label, so most of StrataSLAM's
complexity is in `floor_manager.cpp` (843 lines), a state machine that decides when the robot has left a floor and
where it arrived. Any of three signals can open a transition:

- **Odometry height.** The tracked altitude moves more than `depart_dz` = 0.30 m from the floor's reference height.
- **Barometer.** The filtered pressure height moves more than 0.50 m.
- **IMU.** The trimmed one-second mean of gravity-free vertical acceleration exceeds 0.25 m/s² for half a second while
  the odometry sees no vertical motion. The trimmed mean drops the top and bottom 20 % of samples, because a wheel
  hitting an edge reads tens of m/s² for one sample, and walking bobs average out.

The IMU path is for elevators, where the camera or LiDAR sees a cabin that moves with it and reports no climb at all.
The transition closes after two seconds of calm, and is then classified:

- **Elevator** if the barometer moved at least 1.0 m while the odometry moved less than half of that; or, without a
  barometer, if the IMU's double-integrated height is at least 1.0 m with the odometry static. The double integration
  uses a zero-velocity update at the end (the cabin is at rest again), spreading the residual velocity linearly over the
  ride, which removes most of an accelerometer bias.
- **Aerial** if the platform is a drone, or the horizontal run was under half the climb.
- **Ramp or stairs** otherwise, split on the median slope over 1 m windows of the climb: under 15° is a ramp.

The height change is then snapped to a level. With the default `floor_height_prior` of 3.0 m, a change counts as a
floor only when it is within 0.4 of a whole number of storeys, which is how the half-way landing of a U-shaped stair is
kept from becoming a floor. Matching an existing level uses a tolerance of 0.4 times the prior (1.2 m), widened by the
level's own uncertainty; an elevator ride measured by the IMU is trusted to 5 % of its height. Those numbers came from
real data: the comments cite sequences from the Elevator-VIGS dataset by name, such as an "Office4" run that rode
14.75 m up and walked 15.43 m down the stairs, ended 0.68 m off, and opened a phantom third level before the 5 % term was
added. That run had no height prior, so the tolerance was 0.4 times the 1.5 m minimum change, 0.6 m, not 1.2 m. A plateau rule (20 s calm while travelling 10 m) also accepts a mezzanine that is no multiple of 3 m.

### Three layers of refusal

The floor gate is three independent checks, and the code names them:

1. **Per-floor place recognition.** Each floor has its own Scan Context database and its own bag-of-words index
   (`scan_context.hpp`: "the caller owns one instance per floor (gating layer 1)"). A query only searches the current
   floor, so a keyframe on floor 2 is never compared with floor 1. This is why S03 reports zero cross-floor
   candidates.
2. **Label check.** `Backend::verify_loop_allowed` rejects a loop whose two keyframes carry different floor labels, or
   any keyframe without a label.
3. **Graph membership.** There is a separate factor graph per floor, and `FloorGraph::add_loop` refuses a factor to a
   node it does not hold. ADR-0040 records that the original layer 3 just re-compared the labels, so a mutation test
   could delete it unnoticed; it was rewritten to check what the factor actually joins.

The consequence is stronger than "we filter false loops". No online code path can write a cross-floor factor, so the
failure in the widget cannot happen however similar two floors look. What can still go wrong is the floor label itself:
a missed transition maps two floors into one graph, and a phantom transition splits one floor in two. Every hard case in
the floor manager's comments is one of those.

### Putting the floors back together, offline

Each floor is then a separate map with its own frame, and the building has to be assembled. `strataslam fuse` does it
offline in a gravity-aligned four-degree-of-freedom group, $\mathrm{SE}(2)\times\mathbb{R}$: yaw, $x$, $y$ and $z$, with
roll and pitch fixed by gravity. The documentation in `docs/math/pose4.md` follows Solà, Deray and Atchuthan's
[micro Lie theory](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.01537) notation. A pose $X = (\theta, \mathbf t)$ composes as

$$
X_1 X_2 = \big(\theta_1+\theta_2,\; R_z(\theta_1)\,\mathbf t_2 + \mathbf t_1\big),
\qquad
\exp(\varphi, \boldsymbol\rho) = \big(\varphi,\; (V(\varphi)\,\boldsymbol\rho_{xy},\, \rho_z)\big),
$$

which is SE(2) with a free height bolted on. The constraints between floors are the transition records (the relative
pose measured across the stairs or the ride, with a covariance that grows 3 % per metre the odometry travelled), the
barometer, user-placed anchors, a weak floor-height prior with $\sigma$ = 0.10 m, and a structural registration that
correlates rasterised wall slices of two floors.

That last one is where aliasing comes back, and the project handles it honestly. Registering two floors by their walls
cannot tell one level seen twice from two identical levels stacked. ADR-0027 reports that on a Gazebo garage with
near-identical floors, every pair was declared ambiguous (best score 0.996 against a runner-up of 0.97) and contributed
nothing, which is the correct outcome. ADR-0031 adds "same-level candidates": pairs of floors whose structure matches and
whose heights differ by under 2 m are listed as warnings and never applied. On one real bag it listed three candidates,
one of them right. The user confirms which with `--same-level`. A candidate list is advice, not a classification, in the
ADR's own words.

### What the evidence covers

The fifteen synthetic scenarios run on 20 seeds each, and `docs/results.md` reports every failure with its seed: S07
(an elevator with no IMU or barometer, fused with anchors) passes 13 of 20 on a 0.10 m translation bound; S13 (a 2.9° ramp)
fails once at 0.2512 m against 0.25 m. S15, four identical floors up by elevator and back down by the stairs, passes but
with a mean floor-height error of 0.4507 m. Read the simulator results with ADR-0017 in mind: the camera is simulated at
feature level, as keypoints with descriptors and depth, not rendered images, so the visual front end's real failure modes
are not in them.

The real-data evidence is thinner and is said to be. On TUM RGB-D fr1/xyz and fr2/xyz the visual odometry gets 0.0143 m
and 0.0152 m ATE. On KITTI drive 0027 (692 m) the full LiDAR pipeline gets 0.40 m ATE with one loop closure at 52 ms a
scan on one thread. The multi-floor claims rest on Elevator-VIGS: ADR-0059 reports that on its 16 real sequences one-way
rises land within 0.06 to 0.9 m of the truth and round trips close within 0.07 to 2.6 m. The structural floor registration
has, per ADR-0027, "not yet contributed a constraint" on real data. None of that undercuts the gate: refusing cross-floor
loops does not need a benchmark to be correct. It does mean the hard part, labelling floors correctly on a real robot in
a real stairwell, has been tested on far fewer buildings than the scenario table suggests. A multi-floor equivalent of
the [Hilti SLAM datasets](/articles/hilti-slam-dataset), graded against surveyed points, would settle it.

Forty-one stars and a week old, with 69 ADRs (each deviation from its own plan written down with a reason), a negative
control in CI and mutation testing on the gate. I would try it on a multi-storey capture before I would trust a
single-graph SLAM system to keep two identical car-park levels apart.

## libgnss++: from a thirty-metre guess to a centimetre fix

[libgnss++](https://github.com/rsasaki0109/gnssplusplus-library) is Ryohei Sasaki's C++20 GNSS toolkit (MIT, 260 stars
on 12 November, read at `1f7bd1e`, version 0.6.0). It implements single-point positioning (SPP), real-time kinematic
(RTK), precise point positioning (PPP), Japan's CLAS and MADOCA correction services, RTCM, UBX and QZSS L6 decoding,
a GTSAM-backed GNSS/IMU factor graph, Python bindings, a ROS 2 player and a WebAssembly build that runs `gnss solve` in a
browser tab. Its `src/` directory alone is about 104,000 lines. It is a lot of library, and the core of it is two pieces of
estimation worth understanding.

<RepoCard repo="rsasaki0109/gnssplusplus-library" />

### Pseudoranges and four unknowns

A receiver measures, for each satellite $s$, a pseudorange: the signal's travel time times the speed of light, biased by
the receiver's clock.

$$
P^s = \lVert \mathbf p^s - \mathbf x \rVert + c\,\delta t_r - c\,\delta t^s + I^s + T^s + \varepsilon^s .
$$

The satellite position $\mathbf p^s$ and clock $\delta t^s$ come from the broadcast ephemeris, the ionospheric delay
$I^s$ and tropospheric delay $T^s$ from models. That leaves four unknowns: the position $\mathbf x$ and the receiver
clock bias $c\,\delta t_r$. Linearise around a guess $\mathbf x_0$, with $\mathbf e^s$ the unit vector from receiver to
satellite, and each satellite gives one row of a linear system:

$$
\underbrace{P^s - \hat P^s(\mathbf x_0)}_{r^s} = \begin{bmatrix} -\mathbf e^{s\top} & 1 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}\Delta\mathbf x \\ c\,\delta t_r\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\Delta = (H^\top W H)^{-1} H^\top W\,\mathbf r .
$$

Iterate until $\Delta$ stops moving. Four satellites determine it, more over-determine it, and $W$ down-weights low
satellites whose signals cross more atmosphere. `spp.cpp` is this loop (3,007 lines once you add multi-constellation
clock offsets, robust re-weighting and satellite exclusion modelled on MRTKLIB's `estpos`), starting cold from a seed
about 24,000 km out. The answer is good to a few metres, limited by the code measurement's noise and the atmosphere
models.

### Carrier phase and the integer problem

Centimetres need the carrier phase. The receiver tracks the phase of the 1575.42 MHz L1 carrier, whose wavelength is
$\lambda = c/f$ = 0.1903 m, to a small fraction of a cycle. But it only knows the fractional part when it locks on: the whole number of cycles between satellite and antenna, $N^s$, is unknown.

$$
\lambda\,\Phi^s = \lVert \mathbf p^s - \mathbf x \rVert + c\,\delta t_r - c\,\delta t^s - I^s + T^s + \lambda N^s + \epsilon^s .
$$

RTK removes almost everything else by differencing. Subtract the base station's measurement from the rover's (the
satellite clock cancels), then subtract a reference satellite's single difference from each other satellite's (the
receiver clocks cancel). Over a short baseline the atmosphere is nearly the same at both receivers and cancels too:

$$
\lambda\,\nabla\Delta\Phi^{s} = \nabla\Delta\rho^{s}(\mathbf b) + \lambda\,\nabla\Delta N^{s} + \epsilon .
$$

What is left is the baseline $\mathbf b$ and one integer per satellite pair. In libgnss++, as in RTKLIB, an extended
[Kalman filter](/articles/kalman-filter) carries the baseline, velocity, optional ionosphere terms and one single-difference ambiguity per satellite
and frequency; `rtk_filter.cpp` forms each double-difference phase row from two of those states
($\lambda_\text{ref} N_\text{ref} - \lambda_s N_s$). The filter's ambiguities are real numbers, the **float** solution,
with a covariance $Q_{\hat a}$. Snapping them to integers is integer least squares:

$$
\check{\mathbf a} = \arg\min_{\mathbf a \in \mathbb Z^n}\; (\hat{\mathbf a} - \mathbf a)^\top Q_{\hat a}^{-1}\,(\hat{\mathbf a} - \mathbf a).
$$

Rounding each $\hat a_i$ on its own is wrong whenever the ambiguities are correlated, and double differences always are:
they share the reference satellite, and over a few seconds the satellites barely move, so $Q_{\hat a}$ is a long, thin,
tilted ellipse. Teunissen's [LAMBDA method](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00863419) decorrelates first, with an integer,
volume-preserving transform $Z$ (built from integer Gauss transforms and permutations of an $LDL^\top$ factorisation),
so the search in the transformed space is small, then maps the answer back with $Z^{-\top}$. libgnss++'s `lambda.cpp`
says at its head that it is "Ported line-by-line from RTKLIB's lambda.c" (BSD-2-Clause, T. Takasu), with Eigen in place
of C arrays. That is a good choice: it is the most exercised LAMBDA in open source, and the README's "without an external
RTKLIB runtime" is true while the heritage is plain.

The search returns the best and second-best integer vectors with costs $q_1 \le q_2$. The ratio test accepts the best
only if

$$
\frac{q_2}{q_1} \;\ge\; \kappa ,
$$

with $\kappa$ = `ambiguity_ratio_threshold` = 3.0 by default (`rtk.hpp`). Optional modes make it 1.5 with 20 or more
satellites, 2.0 with 15 or more, 2.5 with 10 or more, and 2.0 while an ambiguity is being held after a run of fixes. There
is also a fixed-failure-rate ratio table from Hou, Verhagen and Wu ([Sensors 2016](https://doi.org/10.3390/s16070945)) and
partial fixing of a subset. Once the integers are accepted, the baseline is conditioned on them,
$\check{\mathbf b} = \hat{\mathbf b} - Q_{\hat b \hat a} Q_{\hat a}^{-1}(\hat{\mathbf a} - \check{\mathbf a})$, and the same
formula appears as `xa = head_state - sQab * slv.solve(sf - sx)` in `rtk_ar_resolve.cpp`.

<AmbiguityFix />

The toy shows the three regimes. With few epochs (noise run 3, four epochs), rounding picks (1, 1) while integer least
squares already finds (0, 0), because it measures distance along the ellipse. Run 2 at one epoch is the dangerous one:
the best integer is (−3, −2), wrong, and its ratio is 3.1, so a threshold of 3.0 accepts it. A wrong fix is worse than
staying float, because the solution then reports centimetre confidence about a position that can be off by a
wavelength per satellite; that is why the PPC tables below count wrong fixes separately.

### What the benchmark tables say, and what they do not

The README leads with that Odaiba animation: median horizontal error 0.38 m against RTKLIB demo5's 7.41 m over 172

## Calibrex checks a calibration instead of trusting it

[Calibrex](https://github.com/rsasaki0109/Calibrex), from the same author (Apache-2.0, 62 stars, read at `2f9d8d0` on
12 November, labelled alpha), starts from a question most calibration tools skip. You calibrated the rig once. Is the
transform in `/tf_static` still right after someone remounted the IMU? It has three commands: `calibrex estimate` reads a
bag and estimates each sensor pair's transform from the motion alone, with no target; `calibrex check` compares a
deployed calibration against that estimate on another recording; `calibrex drift` asks whether it changed across bags.
It reads rosbag2, MCAP and ROS 1 bags without a ROS install, and covers IMU-LiDAR, camera-IMU, camera-LiDAR (rotation only),
LiDAR-LiDAR, GNSS lever arms and vehicle frames.

<RepoCard repo="rsasaki0109/Calibrex" />

The IMU-LiDAR rotation solver shows the method in one equation. Between consecutive LiDAR odometry poses $R_i$ and
$R_{i+1}$, the mean angular rate in the LiDAR frame is $\boldsymbol\omega_L = \mathrm{Log}(R_i^\top R_{i+1})/\Delta t$. The
gyro measured the same rotation in its own frame, shifted by a clock offset $\delta$ and biased by $\mathbf b$:

$$
\boldsymbol\omega_L \;=\; R_{LI}\,\big(\bar{\boldsymbol\omega}_\text{gyro}[t_i+\delta,\; t_{i+1}+\delta] - \mathbf b\big).
$$

Calibrex initialises $R_{LI}$ with a weighted Procrustes alignment of the two sets of rates (so any mounting works), then
solves for rotation, offset and bias with a nonlinear least squares in which the interval means of the gyro come from a
cumulative trapezoid integral. Rotation about an axis is only observable if the recording rotates about the other axes:
a car that only yaws says nothing about the yaw of its IMU mount.

The lever arm (translation) is harder, and the docs explain why. It enters only through rotation:
$(R_j - R_i)\,\mathbf l = R_i(\mathrm{Exp}(\boldsymbol\theta) - I)\,\mathbf l$, so the information on axis $a$ scales with the
rotation perpendicular to $a$. A planar vehicle can see the horizontal components of a GNSS antenna's lever arm from its
turns, and never the vertical one. When an axis is not observed, Calibrex writes it as `NOT MEASURED` instead of
inventing a number.

The verdict is a rule you can audit in `check/verdict.py`. For each judged axis with error $\delta$ between the deployed
transform and the estimate, the tolerance is $\max(3\sigma, \text{floor})$, with a floor of 0.5° for rotation and 0.02 m
for translation. The axis passes if $|\delta|$ is within the tolerance, fails beyond twice it, and warns in between. An
axis the data did not observe is listed as unchecked, and a pair with no judged axis at all is "inconclusive".

That frame checks against the rule: 3.31 / 0.50 = 6.6, beyond the factor of 2, so `fail`; the pitch error of 0.52° is
between 0.50° and 1.0°, so `warn`, which is what the panel prints. The README's other demos are all on public data with
their own numbers: a second Ouster on NTU VIRAL snapping from a 19.1 cm to a 4.4 cm median point-to-plane residual
(the design value gives 4.6 cm), and camera-IMU on Hilti 2022 landing 0.45° and −0.21 ms from Kalibr's target-based
result. On camera-LiDAR, the README says the check flagged 23 of 27 perturbed candidates and never flagged the
unmodified reference, and calls that development evidence, not a claim.

  caption="One 0.1 s Mid-360 sweep at 180°/s, raw and deskewed with Calibrex's own IMU-LiDAR estimate: surface thickness 3.42 cm raw, 2.19 cm deskewed (Calibrex README animation, RTK-SLAM data, CC BY 4.0)."

The deskew clip is the clearest argument for caring. A Mid-360 sweep takes 100 ms; at 180°/s the head turns 18° during
it, and every point has to be moved to one instant using the IMU, which is only as good as the IMU-LiDAR rotation and
time offset. The same deskew happens at the start of [FAST-LIO2](/articles/fast-lio2-lidar-inertial-odometry); a wrong
extrinsic there smears every scan before the filter ever sees it. A tool that tells you, per axis, that your extrinsic has
moved by more than the data's own noise, and also tells you when your recording could not have detected it, is the kind
of thing I would put in a fleet's nightly checks.

## Fifty dollars of time-of-flight on an iPhone

On 10 November [Laan Labs posted](https://x.com/laanlabs/status/2120207457402396739) a short video: "Testing 3d scanning
with this ~\$50 lidar sensor (ST VL53L9CX) attached to an iPhone. Works pretty well for quick indoor scans using just ARKit
poses." It had about 67,000 views two days later, and @taziku_co, the CEO of the Japanese studio taziku,
[reposted the same video](https://x.com/taziku_co/status/2120367058491949234) the next day under a line that
translates as "how far can 3D scanning go with a roughly \$50 small LiDAR and a phone?".

<Video
  src="/articles/open-robotics-mapping-roundup/clip3"
  poster="/articles/open-robotics-mapping-roundup/clip3-poster.jpg"
  alt="An iPhone app view of a living room with a purple and cyan mesh being built over the sofa, floor and walls as the phone moves, followed by an orbit of a whole apartment scanned into a coloured mesh. A status line reads VL53 to mesh, 54x42, 15 fps."
  caption="The VL53L9CX board on an iPhone, meshing a living room from ARKit poses and then a whole apartment; the app's status line reads 54x42 at 15 fps (video from @laanlabs's post on X)."
/>

The sensor is ST's [VL53L9CX](https://www.st.com/resource/en/datasheet/vl53l9cx.pdf), and its datasheet (DS14879 Rev 7,
July 2026, included in the board's repository) is specific. It is a direct time-of-flight module: two 940 nm VCSELs
flood the scene, and a SPAD array (single-photon avalanche diodes) times the returning photons. Distance is
$d = c\,t/2$, so one nanosecond of round trip is 15 cm, and the module gets its accuracy by building a histogram of photon
arrival times over many pulses per zone and finding the peak on chip. The specs:

- up to 54 × 42 zones (2,268 depth values a frame), with binned modes down to 4 × 4;
- 55° × 42° field of view, 71° diagonal;
- ranging from under 5 cm to 8.8 m in its precision mode;
- up to 100 Hz in some profiles; the datasheet's example profiles run full resolution at 100 fps for a short 5 m range,
  30 fps for 8 m;
- accuracy in precision mode at full resolution, indoors, under 8 mm below 1 m and under 0.5 % beyond, on a 17 %
  grey target;
- a 12.8 × 6.1 × 4.6 mm module.

The resolution is the number to keep in mind. 55° across 54 zones is about 1° per zone, so at 3 m one depth value covers
about a 5 cm square of wall. That is a coarse depth camera, not a scanning LiDAR, and it explains how the scans look: good
room shape, soft edges on everything small.

The board is Remi De Coster's [USB-LiDAR-VL53L9](https://github.com/RemiFM/USB-LiDAR-VL53L9) (MIT for his hardware and
code, 136 stars, read at `39a2dd4`). It is a 34 × 30 mm KiCad 10 PCB with the VL53L9CX, an ST LSM6DSV16BX 6-axis IMU and
an STM32C552 Cortex-M33 that enumerates as a USB CDC serial device.

<Figure
  src="/articles/open-robotics-mapping-roundup/fig2.png"
  alt="Block diagram: the VL53L9 dToF 3D LiDAR connects over I3C, and the LSM6DSV 6-axis IMU over SPI, to an STM32C55xx Arm Cortex M33 microcontroller, which connects over USB to a USB-C Full Speed USB port."
  caption="The board's architecture: sensor on I3C, IMU on SPI, one Cortex-M33, USB Full-Speed out (USB-LiDAR-VL53L9 README, block diagram)."
/>

The firmware does very little on purpose. It triggers a frame, polls the ready flag every 500 µs, reads the raw
14,842-byte frame (a `_Static_assert` pins the size for binning 2) into one of two ping-pong buffers and ships it in a
framed packet with a sequence number, a microsecond timestamp and a CRC-32. The README measures about 35 frames a second
and 520 kB/s; 14,842 bytes times 35 is 519,470 bytes a second, so the two agree, and that is about a third of USB
Full-Speed's 12 Mbit/s. IMU samples go out at 120 Hz in the same stream. Depth is not computed on the board: the host turns
raw frames into depth with ST's processing library and the sensor's factory calibration, which the bundled web app
compiles to WebAssembly. That library is under ST's SLA0111 licence, for use with ST sensors only, so the pipeline is
open hardware with a closed-source step in the middle. That is normal for ST's ToF parts and worth knowing before you
plan to port it.

<Figure
  src="/articles/open-robotics-mapping-roundup/fig3.jpg"
  alt="Photograph of the assembled board: a small green PCB with notched corners for mounting screws, the black VL53L9CX module with its two windows on the left, a microcontroller with a red LED lit beside it, and a USB-C cable plugged into the right edge."
  caption="The assembled board, 34 × 30 mm, with M3 holes on a 20 mm pitch for drone stacks (USB-LiDAR-VL53L9 README, assembly photo)."
/>

Laan Labs' iPhone app is not in that repository and has not been published, so what follows is only what the video and
their replies show. The app's status line reads "VL53 → mesh · edges off · 54x42 · 15 fps", so it ran full resolution at
15 frames a second, 34,020 depth values a second. The pose comes from ARKit, Apple's visual-inertial odometry, not from
the ToF sensor; the sensor contributes depth only. That is the easy half of mapping: with poses given, fusing depth frames
into a volume or mesh is a solved problem, and the scan's quality is bounded by ARKit's drift and by how well the depth
frames are registered to the phone's camera. In the replies, Laan Labs said indoor results look similar to the iPhone's
own LiDAR, the iPhone's works much better outdoors, and the VL53L9CX "might have a bit more range indoors", adding that
the iPhone pipeline also has ML depth upsampling this demo does not. Two people asked the questions I would ask: how the
sensor's timestamps are synced to ARKit, and how the extrinsic to the camera was calibrated. Neither had an answer among
the replies I could fetch through a mirror on 12 November. Both are exactly the IMU-camera problem Calibrex measures, at the scale of a phone case.

The "~\$50" is Laan's figure. The repository's bill of materials lists parts and Digi-Key numbers but no prices, so I
cannot check it; the author says he is only exploring selling assembled units.

The 11.6-second clip is a phone on a table, with an Android navigation bar, so not an iPhone. Its status chips read
"Ethernet", "Mid-360 145k点/s" and "IMU 200Hz", and a counter shows 37,844 points four seconds into a map. The Mid-360's

The post names no SLAM algorithm and links no code. A LiDAR with a built-in IMU and a scan pattern that never repeats is
the textbook input for a tightly coupled LiDAR-inertial odometry like FAST-LIO2, and running one on a phone-class ARM SoC
at a Mid-360's rate is plausible, but that is my guess, not something the post says.

One reply under it linked [Foadsf/parallax](https://github.com/Foadsf/parallax), and it reads as if it might be related. It is not. The same account posted the same link under Laan Labs' video ("What if you wouldn't need
extra hardware?"). Parallax (GPL-3.0, created 2 November, read at `a1bc267`) is an Android app that does photogrammetry on
the phone: RootSIFT features, five-point RANSAC, incremental structure from motion with a Schur-complement bundle
adjustment that self-calibrates each lens and models rolling shutter, then semi-global matching for dense stereo, with
metric scale from two rear cameras' factory baseline or a printed ArUco marker. A case-insensitive search of the
repository for "lidar", "livox" and "mid360" finds nothing. It is a reasonable piece of work about a different problem,
camera-only object scanning, and it has nothing to do with the Mid-360 post.

## Where this leaves a LiDAR person

The two phone demos are the fun ones and the least informative. Both delegate the hard part: Laan Labs to ARKit, schan
to an unnamed odometry on hardware that costs more than the phone. Both are still the right demos to build. A 54 × 42
zone sensor at fifty dollars that ranges 8.8 m indoors is a real new option for close-range fill on a capture rig, next to the survey-grade
heads in [Hesai's Kosmo](/articles/hesai-kosmo) or the 360-camera rigs in
[Gaussian-splat capture in practice](/articles/3dgs-capture-pipelines), and the
VL53L9CX board is the cleanest way I have seen to get raw frames off one, licence caveat and all.

libgnss++ is a serious library with honest documentation and benchmark claims you should treat as the author's own
tuning, which the author says. Its RTK core is RTKLIB's mathematics in modern C++, and the widget above is how that core
decides when to believe itself.

Calibrex and StrataSLAM are the two I would actually spend time on. They share a habit: each refuses to output something
it cannot support. Calibrex will not write a lever-arm axis it did not observe. StrataSLAM will not write a factor
between two floors, and lists "these two floors might be the same level" as a warning for a human rather than applying it.
Aliasing on repeated floors and drifted extrinsics share a nasty property: the map comes back wrong while every
residual looks fine. Both projects attack them by making the wrong answer impossible to write down, rather than by
making the estimator cleverer. That is the right instinct. StrataSLAM still has to prove
its floor labelling on more real buildings than Elevator-VIGS and a few Summit bags, and I would want to see that before
putting it in front of a customer. The gate itself I would take today.
